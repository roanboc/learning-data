## The promise

Every line passes two tests: someone who has only heard of AI agents can follow it, and an enterprise architect, a data governance lead and a platform engineer each find nothing wrong in it. The series so far has given an agent's freedom its stages (*Five stages, not a switch*) and shown what a helper borrows on a person's sign-in (*Borrowed rights*). This film is the step from a helper to a colleague: before an agent works on its own, it's designed into the university's enterprise architecture, the way a new colleague is placed in the organisation. Its place on the map starts from the work, not from the agent: the capability the work serves, the service and its steps, and a role assigned only to the steps the agent will do, so the people's steps (review, approve, release) stay with people and separation of duties is on the map. Then the agent is assigned to that role, with an owner who answers for it, and its stage for each kind of task is written on that assignment. A gate comes before it works alone, and who owns, sets up and may stop an agent is written down. Where each right comes from, and how the tools hold it to the map, are the next films' (*Rights follow the role*, *The map, enforced*). **Before an agent works alone, it's architected like a colleague.**

## The story in one paragraph

Southern California, the autumn of 1970: for thirteen days, wildfires burned across the hills; sixteen people died, and about seven hundred homes and other buildings were lost. Some twenty thousand firefighters came to help, from about five hundred agencies, but each agency organised its crews its own way, with its own words, on its own radio channels: near the fires, crews struggled to reach each other, nobody had one up-to-date picture of all the fires, and scarce engines and crews were hard, sometimes impossible, to send where they were needed most. Every agency cooperated as well as it could; what was missing was one way of working they all shared. In 1972, seven of the agencies began to build it, FIRESCOPE, whose Incident Command System starts where help arrives: everyone checks in, each crew is written on a card that takes its place on one board, in one structure, each place has a role and one person it answers to, nobody leads more than about seven, and everyone uses the same plain words. By 1981 it was used widely in Southern California, and in 2004 it became part of the national standard in the United States: crews from anywhere could arrive, check in, and work as one team. At the university, on Monday 2 November 2026, a new kind of colleague joins Planning, the planning agent, which will work on its own; if it simply started, nobody could say what it may do, who answers for it, or who can stop it, so, like a crew at a fire, it checks in first and takes its place on the map. That Monday, Planning has two newcomers, Lucía Ortega and the planning agent, which takes over Planning's tickets from Jun's helper under a name of its own, and both are welcomed the same way, line by line: a badge, and an identity of its own set up by Sam, never a person's; a role; a manager she answers to, and an owner who answers for it, Clara Jensen; rights from a group, never its own and never an admin's; and the confidentiality agreement, which Clara signs on its behalf. Lucía's helper works on her sign-in, while she watches; the planning agent works alone, so it needs a place of its own. A week before, Noor, the data architect, gave it one on the university's map, starting from the work: the capability Planning's tickets serve, *provide data products*; the process every ticket goes through, *change a data product*, seven steps from a ticket to production, each assigned to a role, whoever fills it; a new role, *planning contributor*, on the three drafting steps, while people keep the rest (Jun reviews the code, Clara approves the change, Noor releases it), so whoever drafts a change never approves or releases it. She assigned the planning agent to that role, with Clara as its owner: like a crew's card on the board, it had its place. Its role says what it may do, what it may not, and whom it asks, and when it isn't sure, a person decides. The same day she placed Anil Kurian's workload agent, its stage for each kind of task on its assignment, beside Yusuf's governance review agent, which reviews pull requests and never approves them. Before it worked alone, the planning agent passed its gate: in a sandbox it redid Planning's last forty tickets against a bar Clara and Jun set first; thirty-seven matched, on three it asked, and none was confidently wrong; so Clara agreed stage four for Planning's tickets, Noor wrote it on its assignment with stage one for what a word means, and from then on a check fails any agent on the map without an owner, a role, or a stage for each kind of task. Who may do what with an agent is written down too: only a person owns one; only Sam's platform team sets one up, once it has a place on the map; Clara or Sam may stop it, Sam at any hour; and anyone who sees it go wrong may ask them to. In 1970, help couldn't become one team until there was one structure to join; the planning agent has done the same, so everyone knows what it may do, who answers for it, and who can stop it. Next: rights that come from the work, and not from a person's.

## The reference, and practice

The film's reference is the map's own language, ArchiMate 4, as *The map before the data* teaches it, because the example project's map is written in it. It names no framework on screen, and teaches what they share in plain words.

| His practice | Agents as contributors with a role, their own identity and a person who owns them; roles specific down to what an agent can and can't do; every change to production approved by a second person; agents never approve; an agent in the wrong hands is more dangerous | The welcome, the role's rights, the people's steps, the gate's bar |

**Where the film adopts, extends or differs.** It adopts ArchiMate's elements and relationships as the map writes them. ArchiMate has no element for how far an agent may act alone, so the map extends it: the stage is written on the agent's assignment to its role, one line for each kind of task, and `agent: 'yes'` marks an application component as an agent, both checked by the project's map check (the series' decision of 5 November 2026). It differs from role engineering only in what it leaves for later: which rights each step needs is *Rights follow the role*'s.

**What practitioners would recognise.** An identity for software with a named person who answers for it, never a team or a shared mailbox; a new joiner's checklist that a service account goes through like a person's; access granted to a role's group, never to the account itself; a pilot in a sandbox, judged against a bar agreed before it runs; and a change process where whoever writes a change can't approve or release it, enforced by who holds which step, not by trust.

## The look

*Marble and oak*, the module's look (chosen 3 November 2026): a pale grey marble wall with soft diagonal veins, a slim white skirting, a pale oak floor, daylight from a window on the right, matte off-white cards with soft shadows. No film grain.

- **The opening,** in the module's matte style (no outlines, muted colours, daylight from the right, soft contact shadows), painted once as places and dressed for 1970: the hills, the dusty lot at their foot where help arrives, and a paper map on a table. The fires keep their own deep red-orange, far off on the ridges; nobody is shown hurt. The engines wear their agencies' own colours (red, maroon, cream, white and charcoal), none of them the module's meaning colours, each with a plain mark of its own, a shape with no letters.

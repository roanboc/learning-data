## The promise

Every line passes two tests: someone new to data work can follow it, and a data manager, a data architect or an analytics engineer finds nothing wrong in it. As the module's opening film, it says the module's promise once: data work now asks for new skills, and leading the people who do it is a skill too. Its own idea is the generalist: depth in one thing, and enough of the business, the systems and the data to join the parts into one picture of a decision. The specialists stay essential; someone has to see across them. The work crosses the whole route of one question: the decision, the question behind it, the data that answers it, where that data is born, who keeps it, and the rule that applies.

**Takeaway:** "Go deep in one thing; learn enough of the others to see the whole."

## The story in one paragraph

Kaifeng, the Song dynasty's capital, in 1015: one night a fire breaks out in the palace and spreads, and by noon the next day it has burned two gates, a great hall, a treasury and the imperial library. Ding Wei, a senior minister who had run the state's finances and the building of a palace, is put in charge of the repairs. Rebuilding means three jobs, each slow and costly: earth dug far away and carried in, timber arriving by water hauled through the streets, and the burned rubble carted away, carts crossing a crowded city load after load. His plan makes the three one: the main streets are dug up for earth and become trenches, the bank of the Bian canal is broken and its water let in, rafts and boats float the materials to the palace gate, and when the work is done the rubble fills the trenches and the streets are streets again. "One move, and three jobs done," wrote Shen Kuo some seventy years later; the saving, in his words, ran to hundreds of millions. At the university, a year from now, Ivy Reyes joins the data team on Monday 1 March 2027, with a degree in statistics. Helena Novak, the data manager, who wears two hats (product owner of the team's services, thinking like an architect, and people leader: two sides of one role, not rivals), gives her a month to learn the team's work and one real question to follow through all of it: Graham Ellis's, on whether to ask the University Council for a new teaching building, agreed with him in writing as "do we have too few rooms, or too few at some hours?". She spends a few days with each part (Mei's timetable, Sam's platform and the rooms' sensors, Yusuf's rule, Noor's map, Jun's models), an hour a day beside Jun, and asks each the same question: who uses this, and for which decision? In her fourth week she draws the whole route on one page, from Graham's decision back to the sensors, and at the join sees what no one part could: 14 of the 186 rooms were renumbered in the timetable after their sensors went in, so a join by name would drop them without a word. Nobody was wrong. Mei and Sam match the 14 rooms in a day, and when the team takes up Graham's question the next week, it starts from her page, with all 186 rooms.

## The reference, and practice

The module's reference for the skills films is SFIA, with T-shaped skills and DAMA-DMBOK's roles (the module's plan, 5 November 2026). This film names each once, with David Epstein's *Range*, and teaches what they share: depth in one area, enough of the others to work across them, and someone who sees across the specialists.

| Body of knowledge | What it adds | Where the film uses it |
|---|---|---|
| T-shaped skills, the shape named by David Guest in "The hunt is on for the Renaissance Man of computing", *The Independent*, 17 September 1991 | One deep skill, the stroke of the T, and a bar of working knowledge across the others, so a specialist can work with people in other fields | *Deep in one, enough of the rest*: "That shape has a name: T-shaped. One deep stroke, and a wide bar across the top." |
| SFIA, the Skills Framework for the Information Age, version 9 (the SFIA Foundation) | A shared language for the skills of digital and data work: 147 skills, each described at levels of responsibility | "Frameworks map the work: SFIA lists well over a hundred skills, and DAMA's body of knowledge names the roles of data work." |
| DAMA-DMBOK, the Data Management Body of Knowledge, 2nd edition (DAMA International, 2017) | The parts of data management, and the functions and roles that do the work (stewards, architects, modellers, analysts) | The same line; a card, "The roles of data work · Stewards, architects, modellers, analysts, and more" |
| David Epstein, *Range: Why Generalists Triumph in a Specialized World* (2019), after Robin Hogarth's "kind" and "wicked" learning environments | Specialists win in "kind" settings, where the rules stay fixed and feedback is quick and exact, as in chess; where problems are messy and change, breadth does better | "In his book Range, David Epstein argues that specialists win where the rules stay fixed, as in chess." "Where problems are messy, people with range do better." |
| Practice (the module's plan and its briefs) | Keep depth in one area; learn the words of the others; spend time with each specialist; draw the whole route of one real question on one page; ask "who uses this, for which decision?" | *A few days with each*, *The whole route, on one page*, and *On Monday*, as four steps |

**Where the film adopts, extends or differs.** It adopts the T as the shape of a person's skills, and SFIA's and DAMA's view that data work has many parts, each with its own skills and roles; neither is taught as a list, and SFIA's levels aren't shown. It extends the T from practice: the bar isn't a list of skills held a little, but enough of each part to follow it (its words, its tools, what worries the people who keep it) and to join the parts on one decision, which is why Ivy draws one question's whole route. It differs from the common reading of *Range*, that generalists beat specialists: the film says the specialists stay essential (Mei and Sam match the 14 rooms in a day, which Ivy couldn't), never breadth instead of depth (Ivy keeps her statistics), and that someone has to see across them.

**Practice, from the module's plan.** The material the author shared shaped the emphasis (the module's `proposal.md`): the generalist who joins business, solutions and data. A practitioner will recognise the newcomer who finds the mismatch because nobody else ever had both lists open; the join that drops rows without a word; rooms, assets or codes renamed in one system after another system started using the old names; and a team where every part is right and the whole answer is still wrong. The advice is the briefs': keep depth in one area, learn the words of the others, draw the whole route of one real question on one page, spend time with each specialist, and ask who uses each part, for which decision.

### 4 · The whole route, on one page · 4:55–6:14

**Narration.** In her fourth week, she draws the whole route of Graham's question on one page, starting from his decision. At the top, the decision: whether to ask for the building, which the University Council weighs in June. Under it, the question behind it: too few rooms, or too few at some hours? Then the data that answers it: each room's bookings, beside the people counted in it. Where that data is born: the timetable, and the sensors. Who keeps it: Mei, Sam, Jun and Noor. And the rule: counts, never people. Where the bookings meet the counts, she sees what no one part could. The two must name each room the same way, and for 172 rooms, they do. For 14, they don't: those rooms were renumbered in the timetable after their sensors went in.

| 10 November 2026 | *Less, on purpose* opens in Britain, in the four beats, from Martha Lane Fox's letter of 14 October 2010 and the Government Digital Service's own posts: in 2010 the government publishes millions of pages across hundreds of websites, most run by one department alone; to renew a passport or pay a tax, people must first work out which part of government does it and which site to trust, the same advice sits on several sites in different words, people lose time and can't be sure which page is right, and taxpayers pay around 560 million pounds a year; her advice is one website for the whole of government, one team that answers for what people find there, and no move of the old pages (write only what people need, and archive the rest); the new team reads every page of Directgov and later of Business Link, and what people searched for, keeps only what meets a real need and writes it again, and in 2012 writes down its principles (start with what people need; do less); in October 2012 GOV.UK replaces both sites with about 3,800 pieces of content, people finish 61 tasks in 100 against 46 and a minute faster, it's expected to cost up to 70 million pounds a year less, and the next spring the Design Museum names it Design of the Year. No count of the old sites' pages is given, as none was found in an official source. It comes back in *Stopped, openly* (GOV.UK archived its old pages, where they could still be found) and at the close (it found what people needed, did less, and did it well; Helena's team did the same). |
| 10 November 2026 | Its data story, as the module's `numbers.md` has it, with this film's own dates and details as assumptions: on Tuesday 4 May 2027 Helena Novak's team of eight puts everything it runs and attends on one wall: 38 old reports, 17 not opened in a year; about eight hours of standing meetings a week each; about 60 alerts a week, most needing nothing. It asks who uses each thing, and for what (21 reports read by about 260 people, 17 by nobody), agrees what a meeting, an alert and a report are for, and designs its week from Monday 10 May: a 15-minute stand-up each morning, planning on Monday, a review with the people who asked every second Thursday and a half-hour one-to-one, about three hours a week each. It writes to each of the 17's last readers and whoever asked for it (one needs last year's figures, and gets a copy); the data council approves the plan on Tuesday 25 May (the data management module's case); on Monday 31 May the 17 are switched off, not deleted, any of them back on within a day until the old warehouse closes in December. Sam goes through the alerts with Noor and Jun, one kind at a time ("when this fires, what do we do?"): those that need nothing become a line in a Monday summary, and by 7 June about 12 a week reach him, each needing someone to act, with a note that says what to do. In June each person has about five hours a week back (across eight people, about one person's whole week), the time goes to rebuilding the 21 reports on the platform, each with an owner, and one rule keeps it: anything new needs a reader, a reason and an owner, with the wall again at each quarterly review. The team chooses every cut; nobody's work is called waste. |

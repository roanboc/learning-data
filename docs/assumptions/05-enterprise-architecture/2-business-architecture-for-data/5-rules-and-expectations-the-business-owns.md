# Rules and expectations the business owns: questions and assumptions

*Enterprise architecture* · *Business architecture for data*, film 5 of 5 · PR #PR · [pull request](https://github.com/roanboc/learning-data/pull/PR)

[Watch it on the site](https://data.learn-all.org/business-architecture-for-data/rules-and-expectations-the-business-owns/)

> Written by the film's thread on 12 November 2026, from the series' proposal the author confirmed and merged on 11 November 2026 (PR #318). Every question below would have gone to the author, and was answered instead with the best-reasoned option, for him to review when he watches the film.

| # | Question | Assumption taken | Where it shows |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Which part of the qanat story does this film tell? | The rules a qanat's people kept, as the proposal's row has it ("who repairs the channel, what happens in a dry year; a qanat nobody maintains silts up and dies, and many have"): Sehdeh's three hamlets, Dastgerd, Parvar and Karton, their shares of eight, eight and nine set in the 1600s under Shah Abbas the Great and built into the outlets of their basin, eight, eight and nine spans wide; the diggers clearing the silt each year; the mirab paid with a free share; and the shares still kept in the 1960s, from Hans E. Wulff's "The Qanats of Iran" (*Scientific American*, April 1968) | Chapter 1; `script.md`, *Opening story* and *Rigour* |
| 2 | Where is Sehdeh? | Wulff says "the region of Sehdeh in western Iran". The film says "in Iran" and shows no map, so it makes no claim the author would need to check against a gazetteer | Chapter 1 |
| 3 | Do outlets of eight, eight and nine spans share any flow eight, eight and nine? | The film says "whatever came into the basin, in a wet year or a dry one, left it in the same shares". That is its reading of shares built into the outlets: outlets side by side at one level, with the water falling freely over them, share the flow by their widths, whatever it is. Wulff says only that the shares are built into the outlets' widths | Chapter 1, the bars "8 : 8 : 9, whatever comes in" |
| 4 | Drought "about one year in four", and the rest of the problem: for Sehdeh? | Paul Ward English (1998) gives drought "on average once every four years" for the plateau's qanat villages, and the flow falling with the water table; Wulff gives the silt and the diggers kept busy clearing it. The film applies both to Sehdeh. That a hamlet taking more than its share would leave the others' fields dry is the film's plain reading of shares in a dry year | Chapter 1 |
| 5 | Who set the shares, and who kept them? | Wulff: "the civil engineer in the reign of Shah Abbas the Great", unnamed, as in the film; and a water bailiff, elected by the owners, "who sees that each farmer receives his just share of the water at the proper time—and who receives a free share himself for this service". The film calls him the mirab, the water master, as the series does | Chapter 1 |
| 6 | "More than three hundred years on"? | Shah Abbas the Great reigned from 1588 to 1629, so the shares were set more than three hundred years before Wulff wrote in 1968, when the hamlets "still receive" them | Chapters 1 and 9 |
| 7 | What didn't the story achieve? | As the series' last film must say (the author, 11 November 2026): after the Second World War came deep wells with motor pumps, drawing water faster than rain put it back, and in the last fifty years about half of Iran's qanats have run dry, from poor upkeep or as wells lowered the water underground. The estimate is Iranian hydrologists', reported by Fred Pearce in *Yale Environment 360* (18 December 2025); "after the Second World War" stands for the early 1950s, when, by Roohollah Noori's study cited there, groundwater depletion began with the move from qanats to deep wells. Wulff already saw farmers drilling wells with diesel pumps in 1968. The film doesn't say whether Sehdeh's qanat is among those run dry | Chapter 9 |
| 8 | Is "policy, process, procedure, record" the chain ISO 9000 draws? | It's the author's own material, as the proposal records it: "A policy is carried out by processes, processes by procedures, and procedures make and keep the artifacts, as ISO 9000 does". ISO 9000:2015 defines the four words (a procedure, a "specified way to carry out an activity or a process"; a record, a "document stating results achieved or providing evidence of activities performed"); their order as one chain is the usual shape of a quality management system's documents. The film says "the chain the ISO 9000 standards draw", and names no clause | Chapter 3; `script.md`, *Rigour* |
| 9 | Whose policy, and which rules make it checkable? | The head of retail's, "bills use actual readings wherever they can", invented, and *Rules with a home*'s data rule (RUL-020, every reading marked actual or estimated) and business rule (RUL-021, an estimate replaced when the actual reading arrives), named and not retold. The third, RUL-022, shows on its card in chapter 2 and isn't said | Chapters 2 and 3 |
| 10 | The decision table: its hit policy, rows and owner? | DMN's form, with the hit policy F (the first row that matches), so a new row placed second, for a new meter whose first reading is lower than the last, comes before the row that would estimate it. The rows are the utility's own, simplified. Grace's team owns the table: Grace owns readings (*Who creates it, who owns it*), and the metering lead, who owns check readings as a process (*Processes, as deep as data needs*), is in her team. The film says "Grace's team" and doesn't name the metering lead there | Chapter 4 |
| 11 | *Late, for whom?*: is the case right? | Invented, as the series' world is: a January fault holding back the night's readings from 2,600 meters until nine, billing at six, the data team's check of a day, network planning reading a week later. The data team guessed a day because billing's service level had never been written down; the film doesn't say the data team was wrong to guess, only that the guess wasn't the business's | Chapter 5 |
| 12 | Who writes the expectation, and does moving billing to ten fit? | The head of retail, who owns billing, the step that uses the readings (the series' world: retail owns bills). Moving billing from six to ten is the head of retail's business choice, giving the meter system time to report what's missing; the expectation's three lines (by ten on billing day; a reading or an estimate for every active meter; no more than one in a hundred estimated) are invented. Grace, who owns readings, agrees to meet it; she doesn't set it | Chapter 6 |
| 13 | How is the expectation drawn on the map? | As a requirement, in the motivation layer's purple with its glyph, realised by check readings, with ArchiMate's realisation line, its owner and its reason (billing's service level), as *Rules with a home* drew rules with their reasons | Chapter 6 |
| 14 | Where do checks run, and who fixes what? | From the author's past material (the proposal: "A source system is accountable for its own data quality; the platform reads sources and never writes to them, and builds a quality rule only when the source can't"): the meter system checks each reading as it comes, makes the estimates and reports what's missing by ten, run by the metering team; the data platform only reads it and never writes back; the one check that needs billing's accounts too runs on the platform, and its result goes to the metering lead each billing morning (two active accounts with no reading, invented). Problems are fixed at the source, by its own team | Chapter 7 |
| 15 | Is *Meter readings* a canonical table, with an enterprise contract? | The film doesn't say. It shows the data product's contract holding what each of its readers expects, set by that reader's owner: a contract the data team agrees team to team with the teams that read its data, which needs no agreement from data governance (AGENTS.md, the author, 12 November 2026). If it were canonical, its enterprise contract would be written first by data governance's information architect | Chapter 8 |
| 16 | "When prices change by the hour, billing will need a reading for every hour"? | Invented, and the utility's plain reading: prices that change by the hour need a reading for each hour, so the expectation's second line changes, and its check is rebuilt from what's written. A first draft said "every half hour", which hourly prices don't need | Chapter 9 |
| 17 | The last line: "What the business owns about its data is drawn"? | Yes, instead of the first draft's "The business layer is drawn": the series' part of Tomás's map, bracketed as *What the business owns* draws it, spans the strategy, business and information layers, so the line names what the series drew, not one layer. "Data architecture, the next series, starts from it", with an arrow to the information layer, where that series works | Chapter 9 |
| 18 | Which sound? | Nothing new fitted, and the glass harmonica first planned was taken by *Every change, checked*, which reserved it first in the topic's `palettes.md`. So, as the series' three films before it do, this film pairs two instruments the site has but no film pairs, and no film of this topic plays: a flugelhorn (*Change without surprises*) and a tenor saxophone (*A capability, not a project*'s saxophone quartet), never together. Kept apart from *Who creates it, who owns it*'s clarinet, also a single reed. Whether the four architecture series may do this when nothing new fits is already a question on the author's checklist | The soundtrack; `script.md`, *Sound* |
| 19 | Pause and think: which chapters? | Three, as in the series' other films: after *A decision, in a table*, *Written down by its owners* and *Fixed at the source*. *Late, for whom?* gets no question of its own: its idea, that a reading is late only for the step that needs it, is the reason the second question gives. No labs yet: the series' lab comes with the platform series' last film, as the proposal plans | `site/assets/rules-and-expectations-the-business-owns/think.en.js` |

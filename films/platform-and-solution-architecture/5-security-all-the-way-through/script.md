## The promise

Every line passes two tests: someone new to data work can follow it, and an enterprise architect, a security architect or a data engineer finds nothing wrong in it. Its idea: security runs through every layer of a data platform, and it is the platform's job. Every request says who's asking: people sign in as themselves, through the organisation's single sign-on with a second check, and every job has an identity of its own, given a pass that lasts an hour for each run, so there's no key to keep or to copy. Each identity gets only the rights it needs, and people get theirs through groups, by their role. Every request is checked where the data is, by the catalogue, every time: being on the organisation's network proves nothing, and there's no side road to the files under the tables. Data is encrypted where it's kept and as it moves, set once by the platform. The few keys that can't go away live in the platform's secret store, never in the code, and every pull request is checked for keys. Every line of the audit log names who asked. And because, left to each domain, the quick way wins, the platform makes the safe way the easy way: a template that gives every new job all of it from its first day.

**Takeaway:** "Every request says who's asking, and is checked where the data is."

## The story in one paragraph

Japan, the early 1600s: a century of war has just ended, but the lords who fought it still rule their own domains. To keep them from rising again, the shogunate keeps their wives and children in Edo, as hostages, so on the roads it fears two things: a lord's family slipping out of Edo, and guns going in, to arm a revolt. It needs to see who leaves Edo, and to stop anyone who has no right to. Around 1619 it sets a checkpoint on the Tōkaidō, in the Hakone mountains, by Lake Ashi, and every traveller passes through its gate, between dawn and dusk. A woman leaving Edo shows a pass, written by officials in Edo Castle: it says who she is, where she's going and why, and describes her hair and any marks on her face, hands and feet. A woman examiner checks her against it, and even unties her hair; if she doesn't match her pass, she goes no further. Nobody may go round: taking a side road past a checkpoint is a crime, punished severely. Most men need no pass at all, since the checkpoint looks hardest at what the shogunate fears, so whoever leaves Edo by this road is seen and checked, and the road stays open to everyone else, for about 250 years, until 1869. At the utility, in March, the regulator asks who read customers' bank details in February, and Ama Owusu, the regulatory lead, asks the data team in a ticket. The platform's log has a line for every read, but every line names the same account: data_loader. Made in the platform's first month, it now runs 47 jobs, four people sign in as it from their laptops, its key sits in a script in the repository with no end date, and it can read and change every table in every domain; so nobody can tell the regulator who read the bank details, and anyone who copied the key could still read them today. Nobody meant harm: a new account took a ticket and two weeks, and data_loader worked at once. Ravi Menon starts with identity: people sign in as themselves through single sign-on with a second check, and each of the 47 jobs gets an identity of its own, each run given a pass that lasts an hour. Each identity gets only the rights it needs, people through groups by their role, and customers' bank details can be read by two jobs and the billing team, nobody else. Every request is checked where the data is, by the catalogue; the office network proves nothing, there's no side road to the files under the tables, and data is encrypted where it's kept and as it moves, set once by the platform. The keys that can't go away go in the platform's secret store, every pull request is checked for keys, and six weeks after Ama's ticket data_loader is switched off. In June the regulator asks again, about May, and the answer takes ten minutes: two billing jobs, and one billing analyst working on a ticket; Ama checks it and closes her ticket. A new job now starts from the platform's template, with all of it from the first day, in minutes: since June, twelve new jobs, and not one borrowed account.

## The reference, and practice

The series follows TOGAF 10 and ArchiMate 4, as *The map before the data* does (`../proposal.md`, *Reference and alternatives*), and the plan names NIST SP 800-207 as this film's reference. This film names, once each:

| J. Saltzer and M. Schroeder, *The Protection of Information in Computer Systems* (1975): least privilege; complete mediation, every access to every object checked; fail-safe defaults; psychological acceptability, protection easy enough that people apply it routinely and correctly | Only the rights each identity needs; every request checked, every time; the safe way made the easy way, from the platform's template, by default | *Only what it needs*, *Checked where the data is*, *The safe way, the easy way* |
| TOGAF 10: architecture principles, each with a statement, its rationale and its implications | The rule as a principle: every request says who's asking, and is checked where the data is | *The data practice on the map* |
| ArchiMate 4: technology services offered by a node and used by application components; the motivation layer's principle | The platform's security as services of its own, sign-in through the identity provider, the access check by the catalogue, the secret store and the audit log, used by every data product and every job that builds one | *The data practice on the map* |
| DAMA-DMBOK 2, *Data security*: identification, authentication, authorisation, access and audit; least privilege; roles and groups; encryption at rest and in transit | Who's asking, what they may do, rights through groups by role, encryption set once, and a name on every line of the audit log | *Who is asking* to *A name on every line* |
| MITRE CWE-798, *Use of hard-coded credentials* | A key in a script, which works for whoever holds it; the keys that can't go away kept in a secret store and asked for by name; every pull request checked for keys | *One account, every door*, *No key in the code* |

Where the film differs: the frameworks say what good security looks like, not whose job it is; the plan's position, from the author's brief, is that it is the platform's, because left to each domain the quick way wins. The film never says "zero trust", a phrase that has come to mean a product; it says what the idea asks, in plain words. It doesn't teach how each lock is built, one by one: that is *Locks before the data*, which it names.

Practice, from the author's brief and the series' plan: security and integration are the components that run through every application ("being relevant the integration and security components as they traverse all applications"); the film's data story is the plan's, a service account with rights to everything found in a script, and the platform making the safe way the default. The regulator's question reaches the data team as a ticket from Ama, and she checks the answer before she closes it (AGENTS.md, *Business users ask in tickets*). Keys are found by a check on every pull request, which the data team reviews; nobody in the business reviews code. The film names no product: the identity provider, the catalogue's access check, the secret store and the audit log are what most platforms have, and *Rigour* says what each is in Databricks.

## Rigour to check before recording

Checked on 12 November 2026:

- The checkpoint at Hakone was set up at its present site, by Lake Ashi on the Tōkaidō, around 1619 (Genna 5), "it is said"; Japanese Wikipedia gives about 1618. The film says "Around 1619". It was abolished in 1869, when the new government abolished every checkpoint: about 250 years. Travellers slept at Odawara or Mishima and crossed the mountain the next day. Sources: the Hakone Checkpoint's own pages, [data_01_02_01](https://www.hakonesekisyo.jp/db/hs_disp_fr.html?hdid=data_01_02_01) and [data_03_01_01](https://www.hakonesekisyo.jp/db/hs_disp_fr.html?hdid=data_03_01_01); Japanese Wikipedia, [箱根関所](https://ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/%E7%AE%B1%E6%A0%B9%E9%96%A2%E6%89%80).
- *Iri-deppō ni de-onna*, "guns going in and women going out": the lords' wives and children were kept in Edo as hostages, and the shogunate had its checkpoints watch for guns going into Edo and those women leaving it, to prevent a revolt and keep order (Yamakawa's *Nihonshi shōjiten* and *Nihon daihyakka zensho*, on Kotobank, [入鉄砲に出女](https://kotobank.jp/word/%E5%85%A5%E9%89%84%E7%A0%B2%E3%81%AB%E5%87%BA%E5%A5%B3)).

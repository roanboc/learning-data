## The brief

The module, in the author's words of 3 November 2026: the basic ideas first, then the journey from people using agents on their behalf to autonomous contributors, with their own identity, permissions and human owner, architected and managed as enterprise architecture, governance and security describe. Four series: *What an agent is*, *Agents at work on data*, *From helper to colleague* (this one) and *A colleague that isn't a person*.

*Agents at work on data* put the agent to work and showed it on Databricks, governed, and ended on a promise: "from an agent that helps one person, to one that works beside the team". On 5 November 2026 the author said where that happens: "This is where we should design in our enterprise architecture, business elements, roles, functions, etc; as that's how we can define which tools and which functions they are allowed to work on." So this series designs an agent into the map before it gets any rights: the work it serves, the role it fills, an owner who answers for it, and its stage for each kind of task. Its rights come from the steps of its role, and the tools are set from the map, one way, so they can't drift from it.

The five stages stay as the road an agent travels as a team comes to trust it, each with a gate: it suggests; it acts for a person, step by step; it acts for them, within limits; it becomes a contributor that proposes, with a name of its own; and, for some kinds of task, a contributor with a written mandate. Each stage changes whose identity the agent uses, how much it may do and what the person does. An agent moves up a stage only when it passes the gate, and back down after a serious incident, and its stage is set for each kind of task and each environment, written on the map beside each.

On memory, the same day: "I think that's fine if the tool manages its own memory, but knowing it is just part of the context with a second priority after all the other elements are properly governed. That's why it's still important to govern all these other elements, because we cannot rely only on memory that could be just wrong."

Alternatives worth knowing, named once:

- **TOGAF** gives the order (business first, then data, applications and technology), not its phases.
- **BIZBOK** maps capabilities to the organisation, as *The map before the data* already does.
- **Domain-driven design**: an agent works inside one domain, where words keep one meaning.
- **Levels of driving automation** (SAE J3016, levels 0 to 5) define autonomy by what the machine does. The series defines it by what the person does, as Feng, McDonald and Zhang do, because in data work the person's part is what keeps the accountability.
- **The NIST AI Risk Management Framework** (govern, map, measure, manage): named once, as a way to organise the risks this series designs for.

Where the series extends or differs from them:

- ArchiMate has no element for an AI agent's autonomy or its mandate. The map writes the stage on the agent's assignment for each kind of task, and the mandate as a requirement, as *Rules with a home* writes a rule.
- ArchiMate says where something sits, not how it's enforced. The tools' settings are written one way from the map, as *A map people and agents can read* makes its views, and a check fails when they drift.
- Mining roles from existing permissions fails for an agent: it has no history, and its person's rights are too wide. Its role is designed top-down, from the work.
- An agent is a contributor with a role, never an approver, and however far it goes, a person releases every change to production.

## Practical advice the films carry

- Set an agent's stage for each kind of task and each environment, write it on the map, and write down the gate to the next.
- Start a helper on a person's sign-in only to learn: a development environment, read-only tools, drafts in a sandbox, no sensitive data, and the person approving each action.
- Before an agent works alone, give it a place on the map: the work it serves, the steps its role does, an owner who answers for it, what it may decide, what it only drafts, and whom it asks.
- Derive its rights from its role's steps, each right with the step that needs it. Write down what's left out, and never copy a person's rights.
- Let it read governed context first (its place on the map, the glossary, the business and data rules, the data's own descriptions), and its memory second.
- Write the tools' settings from the map, one way, with a check that fails when a tool holds a right the map doesn't. Give the agent its own identity in every tool, with no secret it can see or leak, so its work is labelled and it can be stopped without stopping anyone else.
- Let it propose, never release: checks on what changed, a diff of the data, reviews from the roles on the map, and a second person's approval for production.
- Write its autonomy down as a narrow mandate on the map, signed by its owner and approved by a second person, enforced where the work happens, with a switch that stops it and an undo for every change.
- After a serious incident, move the task down a stage until the gate is passed again.

Four films open with a story from somewhere else, at another time; the three in the tools open today, at the university. Numbers are invented and believable, and every story is blameless.

The example dbt project, `films/analytics-engineering/project/`, is the baseline, as the author asked. The agents work together in it: Jun's and Lucía's helpers, the planning agent, the agent with a history, and the governance review agent already on the project's map. They hand work on only as people do, through a ticket, a pull request, a review for reference, a report and the team's memory, and a person decides at every hand-off. What a film needs that the project lacks is added to it in place: the work's steps, the roles and the agents on the map, the rights made from them, and the files that carry them into each tool. How the project is built is told in *In the weeds of data crafting* (`films/analytics-engineering/`): the films name its films by title and don't teach them again, and every film page links it under *See it in the project*.

## The look

The module's look, from `films/what-an-agent-is/shared/`: the white lab made Nordic, a pale grey marble wall over a pale oak floor, daylight from the right, matte off-white cards; the agent a ceramic sphere with a band of violet light, the loop an oak ring, the hands pale robot arms with oak joints, a sandbox for drafts and a pane of glass in front of production. Colours mean the same in every film: blue tools and structure, violet the model thinking, orange data coming back, green a check that passes, amber a check that fails or a loop. Several agents appear, and they look alike: each has its name and its badge, never a colour of its own. People have an adult's proportions and one three-quarter head, with their arms down and a hand only on the thing a story is about. Openings from other places and times are drawn in the same matte style, dressed for their time. The tools' screens are drawn as cards in the same look.

## The sound

Each film has a palette of its own, made in its `tools/score.py`, with a lead instrument the site doesn't have, one for each film in order: rototoms, a kit, a Novachord, brake drums, almglocken, tuned flowerpots and drawn sound. Music plays under the opening story, then only at a chapter's turn, under a moment on screen or in a pause without words, at most about once every 10 seconds, and no figure comes back again and again. Effects are soft, low, one at a time and tied to something on screen; held notes sit at even levels. Music and effects sit no louder than the governance films'. An urgent pulse plays only where a story turns to an error or a rush. The module's `quiet.py` checks it all, and the mix is at -16 LUFS.

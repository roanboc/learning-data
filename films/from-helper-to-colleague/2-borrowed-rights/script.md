## The promise

Every line passes two tests: someone who has only heard of AI agents can follow it, and a platform lead, a data engineer or a security specialist finds nothing wrong in it. An AI helper in an editor or a chat starts on the sign-in of the person using it: nothing to set up, and it can do whatever that person can. The first three of the five stages work that way, and it's how most people meet a helper. But borrowed rights are a person's rights: every step the helper takes reads as that person's, no tool can narrow or stop it without its person, and on a busy day a step its person would never take alone can pass as theirs. The film shows the near miss that made Jun and Sam narrow his helper; what they narrowed it to (a development workspace, tools that only read, a sandbox of its own, small samples with names masked, and what only Jun should do kept his); why the helper's own setting to ask first isn't the boundary, and why the boundary is in its tools. Narrowed, borrowed rights are good for learning: Lucía's helper starts that way on her first day. A helper that works for the team needs what the penny post gave every letter, a way to say who really sent it: a name of its own, and a place on the university's map.

**Takeaway:** "Borrowed rights are for learning, in a sandbox."

## The story in one paragraph

Britain, in the 1700s: a letter is a folded sheet, sealed with wax, and the person who receives it pays, more the further it travels and the more sheets it holds. But a member of parliament may send his letters free: he writes his name on the cover, a frank, and the post carries it for nothing, so whoever holds a cover he has signed can use his privilege. Members sign covers for family and friends; signed blank covers are given away, even sold, and signatures are copied; the post carries free letters no member ever sent, while everyone else pays dearly for theirs, and nobody can tell who really sent a letter with a member's name on it. Rules try to narrow it: from 1764, the whole address in the member's own hand; from 1784, the post town and the date as well, the day in words, and the letter posted that same day. Still, in the 1830s, about seven million letters a year travel free. A schoolmaster, Rowland Hill, argues for a cheap post that the sender pays; Parliament agrees, and on 10 January 1840 the penny post begins and franking ends. In a year, letters more than double, from about 76 million to nearly 170 million, and every letter pays its own way. At the university, Jun Park's AI helper drafted Planning's tickets through 2026 on his sign-in, at stages 1 to 3, with everything Jun may do: he builds the project, and he reviews and approves its code. On Friday 16 October, drafting a new mart for Monday's board, its next step was "Approve, as Jun Park", on a fix of Anil Kurian's workload agent that was in review for Jun, its checks passed: the thirty-eighth step Jun was asked to allow that day. He said no, reviewed the fix himself on Monday, and Noor approved it for production; a yes would have had an agent's change approved by an agent, with a person's name on it. On a person's sign-in every step reads as that person's, like a franked cover that can't say who really sent it, and the helper can't be narrowed or stopped on its own: a confused deputy, and, as security experts say, excessive agency. From Monday 19 October, Jun and Sam narrowed what it borrows, as Saltzer and Schroeder's fewest rights ask: a development workspace, tools that only read, drafts only in a sandbox of its own, samples of at most twenty rows with names masked, and approving, merging, production, approved tests and rights kept Jun's; the project's page for agents now says so. A helper's own setting to ask first isn't the boundary: one maker calls it a productivity feature, not a security boundary, so the boundary is in its tools. Lucía Ortega's helper is narrowed the same way from her first day, Monday 2 November, and helps her learn the project in its sandbox. The rules of 1764 and 1784 narrowed the frank but couldn't say who sent a letter; every letter paying its own way did. A helper that works for the team needs the same, a name of its own: that same Monday, Planning's tickets passed to the planning agent, owned by Clara Jensen, which was given a place on the university's map before it took a single ticket.

## The reference, and practice

The module's references for this film are the two oldest ideas in computer security that fit a helper on a person's sign-in, and the name security experts now give its risk; the film names each once, in plain words, and teaches what they share.

**Where the film adopts, extends or differs.** It adopts least privilege as Saltzer and Schroeder wrote it, for programs and people alike, and OWASP's three ways an agent gets more agency than its job needs (more tools, more rights, more freedom). It takes Hardy's name the way security writers now use it for agents: his deputy, a compiler, held rights of its own and was steered to use them for a caller's purpose; Jun's helper holds only Jun's, and the shape of the risk is the same, a person's rights used for a purpose that isn't theirs, by a ticket's hidden words, a stolen sign-in, or a step allowed unread. It differs from OWASP in one place, and says why: OWASP advises running an agent's tools in the user's own context, with that user's rights, rather than under one broad account; the film agrees for a helper beside one person, as long as it's narrowed, and adds from practice that a helper that works for the team needs a name of its own, so its steps can be told from a person's in the log, and it can be narrowed or stopped on its own. The maker's sentence is quoted, not argued: a setting its person can change with a click is a convenience, and the boundary is in the tools. The film names no product, and says no release status.

**Practice, from the author (the plan's insights):** start a helper as a junior assistant on its person's sign-in, in a development space with samples and no sensitive data; tools read only by default, and writes only as drafts in sandboxes; agents never approve, and nothing reaches production without a person and a second person's approval; an agent in the wrong hands is more dangerous than a person, because it does anything its rights allow, faster; and once an agent works for the team, it gets its own identity, an owner and a role. A practitioner will recognise the afternoon of allowing steps without reading them, a log that says only their own name, a helper that could approve because they could, and the relief of a sandbox where nothing it drafts can reach production.

The kit is the dancing master's pocket fiddle of the 1600s and 1700s: a small, thin bowed string, played softly with no vibrato, in a small room, as the master played it while a pupil learned the steps, never at the ball. It's the instrument of the lesson, and of whoever borrows a person's rights: whoever holds a member's signed cover, and Jun's helper. Its body is tiny, so its lowest notes are thin and its tone sits in the middle, nasal and plain; it's kept low in its range and soft, with no strings ringing in sympathy, so it never reads as a violin, nor as the viola d'amore of *Memory: from one chat to the team*, nor as the muted quartet of *Governed in dbt*: measured the same way, the kit's tone centres near 750 Hz, and theirs between 330 and 570 Hz. ## Rigour to check before recording

Checked on 5 November 2026; the sources are listed below.

- **A letter before 1840.** The person who received a letter paid for it, and the cost grew with the distance and the sheets: "How much you'd pay depended on two factors: the distance your post travelled, and the number of sheets of paper within it" (Hampshire Archives Trust, *Cheating the Post Office*), "But usually the person getting the post paid for it" (London Museum). A letter of the time was a sheet folded and sealed with wax, its address written on the outside, which the film calls its cover. "Britain, in the 1700s" is when the rules the film tells were made; the privilege itself is older.
- **The frank, and how it was abused.** A member of parliament could send letters free by writing his name on the cover: "peers and MPs made their signed 'covers' available to enclose correspondence conducted by other parties. By 1754 the amount of franked material, representing lost revenue, amounted to £23,600" (History of Parliament). The commonest abuses were "to sign the front of letters written by friends and relatives", "to sign blank sheets of writing paper and hand them out as gifts, or even to sell signed sheets to the general public" (Hampshire Archives Trust); signatures were counterfeited, and an Act set "a penalty of transportation for seven years for anyone convicted of counterfeiting hand writing in order to send mail free of postage". The film says "given away, even sold, and signatures are copied", and leaves out the penalty and the lost revenue: the story needs the name that can't say who sent a letter.
- **1764.** Raguin, *British Post Office Notices*, volume 1: the notice of 20 April 1764, in force from 1 May 1764 (p. 128), asks for franked letters "whereof the whole Superscription shall be of his own Hand-writing" (p. 129): the superscription is the address on the cover. The film: "From 1764, the member must write the whole address in his own hand."
- **1784.** The same volume, pp. 164 to 166: from 20 August 1784, members were to write "the Name of the Post Town, and the Date, in Words at Length" at the top of the superscription, and a letter dated otherwise than the day it was put in the post was charged. The film: "From 1784, the town and the date as well, the day in words, and the letter posted that same day."
- **About seven million a year.** Rowland Hill's estimate for 1837, as the *Encyclopædia Britannica* (1911) gives it: "the number of chargeable letters at about 88,600,000, that of franked letters at 7,400,000"; its table for 1839 has about 6,500,000 franks. The film's "in the 1830s, about seven million letters a year travel free" holds for both.
- **Hill, and Parliament.** "A teacher called Rowland Hill had ideas for reforming Britain's broken postal service" (London Museum); the film says schoolmaster, the word of his time for a teacher in a school. His pamphlet of 1837, *Post Office Reform: its Importance and Practicability*, is drawn with its own title. "Later in 1837 the Select Committee of Postage was set up and by one vote only, they recommended that Parliament adopt Hill's scheme" (Victorian Web), and Parliament did: a fourpenny rate from 5 December 1839 to 9 January 1840, a step the film leaves out, then the penny.
- **The penny post.** "On the 10th of January 1840 the uniform penny rate came into operation throughout the United Kingdom" (*Britannica*, 1911), "one single postal rate: one penny, no matter the distance", and "With the Penny Post, you had to pay for postage before sending the letter" (London Museum). The penny paid for a letter of up to half an ounce, and "The postage was to be prepaid, and if not to be charged at double rates" (*Britannica*, 1911); the film's "one penny a letter, anywhere in Britain, paid by its sender" says it as most letters met it. The notice on the wall is the film's own, in its own words.
- **Franking ends.** "Free Franking of any kind was abolished in 1840, with the introduction of the penny post" (Hampshire Archives Trust); "franking was also abolished, so MPs, peers and even the recently-crowned Queen Victoria could no longer send letters for free" (London Museum).
- **More than double.** The *Britannica* (1911) table: about 76,000,000 letters in 1839 and about 169,000,000 in 1840; the film says "from about 76 million to nearly 170 million", and its piles, "1839 · about 76 million" and "1840 · nearly 170 million". "Every letter pays its own way" is the prepaid penny; the red PAID on the film's cover is its own drawing.

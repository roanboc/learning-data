## The promise

Every line passes two tests: anyone who has changed something other people depend on can follow it, and an experienced data governance lead finds nothing wrong in it. Sweden switched sides of the road in one morning. Driving on the left, unlike its neighbours, cost lives, and many feared the switch would cost more. It surprised no one, because everyone knew what would change, when, and what to do, and in the next two months half as many people died on the roads as a year before. A change to data that people depend on needs the same three things. The symptom is common: a change board that meets once a month, with a form answered from memory, because nobody keeps a record of who uses what. What's worth keeping is small. A record of who uses what, with an owner on every line. An impact note on every change request: what it reaches, whether it breaks, and who must be told, filled in with the owners. A notice in each team's own words, with a date and help. And, for a change that can't be added, the old edition kept beside the new one until a date everyone knows. The data's owner decides whether a change of meaning breaks; each user's owner decides when they're ready; the data team makes the change and writes the note; governance makes the note a step of every change. What it gives is what Sweden got: a change made on time that surprises no one; here it also breaks nothing, and the two reports agree. **Change control is a step in the change, not a meeting.**

## The story in one paragraph

At ten to five on the morning of 3 September 1967, every car still on Sweden's roads stopped, crossed from the left side to the right, and at five drove on. Sweden had driven on the left, unlike its neighbours, in cars mostly with the wheel on the left, so drivers couldn't see past the car in front to overtake, and many crashed head on. Switching would be safer, but many feared the switch itself: one driver who didn't know could meet another head on. Everyone had to know. Parliament had decided four years before, and a commission had prepared ever since: new signs went up on the right and stayed covered until one night, a booklet reached every home, the change's sign was on milk cartons, and thousands of buses got doors on the other side. The change was huge, and it surprised no one, and in the next two months half as many people died on the roads as a year before. At the university, the word *award* has just changed: microcredentials of at least 6 credit points now count, and this morning Mei, who owns the word, signed off the new meaning on the change notice the nightly check sent on Monday night (*One word, one home*). Now Jun must change what the reports count. The binder's policy 19 says changes are assessed for impact before they're made; it runs as a change board once a month, with a form answered from memory, and the next one meets after the draft submission, so the draft would count the old way; and anyone nobody remembers isn't told. Jun needs a better way. To see what a change reaches, you need to know who uses what, and nobody keeps a record of all of it. So Jun and Yusuf start a record of who uses what, and each owner writes their own line: the two reports that count awards, the wallet app, Planning's dashboard of learners near an award and Finance's forecast. Jun's change request now carries an impact note. His first draft relabels the big microcredentials as awards, and the note lists five things it reaches: the two reports, which asked for it, and three that didn't. Planning's dashboard and Finance's forecast count the credit learners have towards an award, part of which comes from microcredentials, so their numbers would move; learners' wallets would show microcredentials as awards; and the table would look the same. Mei, who owns the meaning, decides it breaks. Jun adds instead: every microcredential stays one, and a new mark says which count as an award, yes or no. The note now lists three things, the ones that use credentials, and nothing breaks. A change that can't be added gets a new edition beside the old one, kept until a date: credentials' edition 1 retires on 31 March 2027. Jun writes to the wallet team in their words, with a date and his help. The draft submission counts with the new mark from Wednesday, and Planning's census report from its next run, so the two numbers will agree. Each owner says when they're ready, and the change goes in, before Wednesday's draft runs (*The facts the films share*). Yusuf takes policy 19 off the shelf: an impact note on every change request, a record every team adds its line to, and the board only for the rare change that needs it. The change went in the same day, not a month later; nothing broke, and the two reports will agree: like Sweden's morning, it surprised no one. One question is left for the next film: when a table that many teams use is late, who answers for it?

## Rigour to check before recording

Checked on 21 October 2026 (Dagen H; why Sweden switched, the fear and the relief again on 27 October 2026) and 27 October 2026 (the rest); the sources are listed below.

- **Dagen H, the hour.** On Sunday 3 September 1967, every vehicle on the road stopped at 04:50, changed to the right, and waited, "the ten minutes' standstill" (*de tio minuternas stillestånd*, Swedish Wikipedia, *Högertrafikomläggningen*); at 05:00 it drove on. A general traffic ban held across the whole country from 01:00 to 06:00 ("Ett generellt trafikförbud rådde i hela landet från klockan 01.00", M Sverige, 1 September 2017), with longer ones in some towns (Stockholm's from 10:00 on Saturday 2 September). So "every car still on Sweden's roads": only the few allowed were on them.
- **The decision and the commission.** The Riksdag decided on 10 May 1963, by 294 votes to 50. Statens högertrafikkommission (HTK) was set up on 28 June 1963 to plan and carry out the switch, and closed in 1967: "four years". The referendum of 16 October 1955 had gone 82.9% against, of valid votes; the film doesn't mention it (one source says "of the population", which is wrong).
- **Why Sweden switched.** All its neighbours drove on the right, Norway and Finland among them, with about 5 million vehicles crossing the borders each year, and more than 90 percent of Swedes drove cars with the wheel on the left, which led to many head-on collisions when overtaking on narrow two-lane roads (English Wikipedia, *Dagen H*). Swedish sources give traffic safety, seen internationally, as the main reason (*Ökad trafiksäkerhet i ett internationellt perspektiv*, Swedish Wikipedia; Företagshistoria), with a uniform traffic direction in Europe (Norrbottens museum). After the switch, overtaking was safer, because most cars already had the wheel on the left (*omkörningar blivit säkrare*, Swedish Wikipedia). So the narration's "most Swedish cars were built for the right, with the wheel on the left" (Swedish carmakers built cars with the wheel on the left from the start, and most of them went to countries that drove on the right, Företagshistoria), and its "When overtaking, drivers couldn't see what was coming": with the wheel on the left, on the left of the road, the driver sits at the kerb. The border post is a road into Norway, not a particular one; its sign carries an arrow, not words.
- **What many feared.** "Many doomsayers and supporters of keeping left-hand traffic had feared that the switch would mean more accidents" (*Många olyckskorpar och förespråkare för att behålla vänstertrafik hade befarat att omställningen till högertrafik skulle innebära fler olyckor*, Norrbottens museum). The narration's "one driver who didn't know could meet another head on" says what that fear meant, and the picture paints it in grey as a what-if that stops before the cars meet. The first serious accident after the switch, that Sunday afternoon in Gagnef, was a head-on crash with a driver on the wrong side, thought to be drunk (Swedish Wikipedia); the film doesn't show it.
- **Signs.** New signs for the right were put up beside the old ones and covered; in the night to 3 September the covers came off (Norrbottens museum, 1 September 2017; Smålandsposten, 31 August 2017, has 20,000 people at work on the day "to uncover new signs and cover old ones"). Counts differ: about 300,000 (Norrbottens museum), about 350,000 (a 1966 estimate, in English Wikipedia) and 360,000 (Smålandsposten), so the film gives no number. Smålandsposten's 130,000 reminder signs (one source) aren't in the film.
- **The booklet and the cartons.** A 32-page booklet, printed in 8 million copies, went to every household in Sweden the week before (Norrbottens museum). The change's sign was on milk cartons, and even on underwear (English Wikipedia; Smålandsposten's *mjölkpaket* and *H-kalsonger*). The film draws a plain letter H, not the 1967 logo.
- **Buses.** About 8,000 buses with doors on the wrong side had to be rebuilt or replaced (Smålandsposten); English Wikipedia gives over 1,000 new buses and about 8,000 rebuilt; Swedish Wikipedia about 7,500 buses in all. "Thousands" holds across them. The buses left the narration on 27 October 2026, to make room for why the change was needed; one stays in the morning traffic, and the story paragraph keeps them.
- **Afterwards.** In September and October 1967, half as many people died on Sweden's roads as in the same months of 1966: 59 against 99 by the end of September, and 70 against 159 in October, 129 against 258 in all (Norrbottens museum; "halverades", M Sverige). That's the opening's relief, "In the next two months, half as many people died on the roads as a year before", said as a fact for those two months only: accidents rose again in 1968 and 1969, with more cars on the roads (Norrbottens museum), and by 1969 they were back to the levels before the change (English Wikipedia). The day itself had 157 minor accidents, 32 with injuries (English Wikipedia), not in the film.
- **The name.** *Dagen H*, "H Day", the H for *högertrafik*, traffic on the right. Said once ("Dahgen Hoh" for the voice), never written on screen; the narration explains it as "the day of right-hand traffic" (28 October 2026).

Stockholmskällan's record of the information film of 1967–68 shows the switch at Slussen, with the police directing it by radio at five in the morning; Swedish Wikipedia has the police at Kungsgatan, Stockholm, on 3 September 1967.

## The promise

Every line passes two tests: a newcomer can follow it, and a data quality lead who has run Thomas Redman's measurement and a platform engineer who profiles tables on Databricks each find nothing wrong in it. *Good enough for what?* agreed what the graduation list is for and what each use needs. This film measures it before anyone writes a rule: first by looking, the people who know the list marking a hundred of its most recent records by hand and counting the perfect ones (79 of 100), then by profiling, the platform describing every column of the whole list and showing where to look (names cut at 30 characters, names in capitals, accents gone). Only then come the checks, starting with what the profile showed, each with an owner and a level agreed in advance. And it ends on what no check of the list can see, which is the next film's subject. **Look at a hundred records before you write a single check.**

## The story in one paragraph

Dublin, 1899: a young chemist, William Gosset, joins a large brewery that wants better barley. A trial means a few plots and a whole season, so it gives only a handful of results, and a handful can mislead: the brewery could choose the wrong barley and live with it for a year, or turn good barley away; and the mathematics of the day worked only for large numbers. Gosset works out how far a few results can be trusted, tries it by hand on 3,000 cards drawn four at a time, and in 1908 publishes it with tables, as "Student". Now a few careful trials are enough to decide, and his tables say when more are needed; his paper's eleven pairs of trials show that kiln-dried seed probably gave a little more grain, and almost certainly worse barley. At the university, the graduation list passed every check, yet 41 degree certificates were printed again in December 2025. On Friday 6 February 2026, Malia Fonoti, Mei Tanaka and Jun Park look at the hundred most recent records of December's list, mark every wrong field, and count the perfect ones: 79. Good to about eight either way, it's enough to act on, and it raises a puzzle *The hidden factory* answers: over 400 wrong records, only 41 reprints. Then Jun profiles the whole list on Databricks: the longest family name is exactly the field's 30 characters, the same names appear in capitals, and with three custom metrics he counts 41 names cut short, 312 in capitals (none in the hundred, which were all students admitted since 2020) and not one accent. Only then does he write checks, from what the profile showed: three that hold the list for a person, owned by Mei. The swapped names, awards, ceremonies and dates are allowed values, and wrong: no check of the list can see them, so the next film finds something outside it to compare with.

## The reference, and practice

The module's reference is DAMA-DMBOK's data quality chapter as the map, with Danette McGilvray's *Ten Steps* and Thomas Redman's practice as the spine, and the quality movement underneath (the module's *Decided* table records why). For this film, three bodies of knowledge, each named once and none taught:

| Body of knowledge | What it adds | Where the film uses it |
|---|---|---|
| Thomas Redman's Friday afternoon measurement (*Harvard Business Review*, 2016; with Nagle and Sammon, 2017) | The people who know the data mark every wrong field of the hundred most recent records; the share of perfect records is the measure | *A hundred, by hand*: "Thomas Redman, a pioneer of data quality, calls this the Friday afternoon measurement." |
| Student (W. S. Gosset), *The probable error of a mean* (1908), and the sampling it began | How far a few can be trusted; a margin for the measure | The opening, and *Is a hundred enough?*: "It's Gosset's question again" |
| Data profiling, as DAMA-DMBOK and McGilvray's *Ten Steps* use it, and as Databricks' data profiling does it | Describe every column of the whole table, its empty, distinct, shortest, longest and most frequent values, before writing rules | *The whole list, described* |

**Where the film adopts, extends or differs.** It adopts Redman's measurement as he gives it: the most recent hundred, the people who know the data, every wrong field marked, a record with nothing wrong counted as perfect. It narrows his ten to fifteen fields to the five the certificate and the ceremony use, because the module's first film agreed what the list is for. It extends the measurement with a margin, said as holding only if the hundred are like the rest, and calls that Gosset's question, not his answer (his formula is for an average). It pairs the measurement with profiling, as McGilvray's steps do (assess, then find causes), and says what each gives: the hundred say how good, the profile where to look. And it puts the checks after both, from what they found, where much practice writes checks first, from what is easy to check.

**Practice (the module's plan and briefs, 5 November 2026):** measure before you write rules; a hundred records and the people who know them, in an afternoon, tell a team more than a week of writing checks; and a check worth writing holds the data for a person before it stops anything. A practitioner will recognise the data story: a table that passes every check, a first honest number that's lower than anyone guessed, and errors that only a full profile of the old records shows.

**What the film leaves to others.** How a check warns or stops, and whom it tells, is *Rules that run*'s and *Too good to be true*'s; the hidden work that kept the list's errors from the printer is *The hidden factory*'s, named; accuracy against a reference outside the data is *Valid isn't true*'s, the next film; the platform's monitoring is *Quality on the platform*'s, named.

## Pause and think

## Rigour to check before recording

Each source with the day it was read: 5 November 2026, and the links on the film's page again on 10 November 2026.

- **Gosset at the brewery.** William Sealy Gosset (1876 to 1937) joined the brewery in Dublin in 1899 after a degree in chemistry at New College, Oxford (MacTutor). The brewery isn't named, as the module's brief asks. "A young chemist": he was 23. "It wants better barley": the brewery ran trials of barley varieties and of how to grow them, which is what made its experiments small (MacTutor: the t-test was invented "to handle small samples for quality control in brewing"). "Live with it for a year" is the film's plain word for a choice that waits a season to be tried again.
- **The problem, in his words.** *The probable error of a mean* (*Biometrika*, vol. 6, no. 1, March 1908, pp. 1 to 25), introduction: "it is sometimes necessary to judge of the certainty of the results from a very small sample", and such experiments, "most agricultural and large-scale experiments", have "hitherto been almost outside the range of statistical inquiry". The narration's "The mathematics of the day worked for large numbers of measurements. Nobody could say how far a few could be trusted" says that plainly.
- **By hand, on cards.** Section VI: "Before I had succeeded in solving my problem analytically, I had endeavoured to do so empirically", with measurements "written out on 3000 pieces of cardboard, which were then very thoroughly shuffled and drawn at random… each consecutive set of 4 was taken as a sample—750 in all". The measurements were of 3,000 criminals, from a published table (heights and finger lengths); the film says only "measurements", as nothing in the idea needs whose they were. The four values on his cards (65.8, 63.4, 67.1, 64.9) are drawn for the film, in the range of the paper's heights in inches.
- **"As Student".** The paper's byline is "By STUDENT". The brewery let its scientists publish on condition that they mentioned neither the brewery nor beer, and didn't sign with their own names (the published accounts, from Ziliak; Wikipedia cites them). Not used, because no source read confirms them: the barley varieties, hops, and the story of a "Pupil" pen name.
- **"Tables anyone could use".** The paper gives tables for samples of 4 to 10 (its conclusion 4: "Tables are given by which it can be judged whether a series of experiments, however short, have given a result which conforms to any required standard of accuracy or whether it is necessary to continue the investigation"), the film's "and his tables said when more were needed".
- **The kiln-dried seed.** The paper's barley illustration, from published agricultural trials: seven varieties sown both kiln-dried and not kiln-dried in 1899, and four in 1900, eleven pairs; "the odds are about 14 to 1 that kiln-dried corn gives the higher yield" (the film's "probably gave a little more grain") and "the odds are very great that kiln-dried seed gives barley of a worse quality" (its "almost certainly worse barley"). "In his paper" because his illustrations use published data, not the brewery's own. The plots' four yields on screen (2.8, 3.4, 3.1, 2.6) are drawn for the film.
- **"Still used".** Student's t, as it came to be called, is taught and used in medicine, agriculture and industry today; the film says "his way", the idea of judging how sure a few results can make you.
- **The university, the list and its people.** The module's invented ones (numbers.md): 2,031 graduates in December 2025; 41 certificates printed again; Malia Fonoti, Mei Tanaka and Jun Park. "In January, they agreed what the list is for" is *Good enough for what?*'s story, matched to that film as merged on 10 November 2026 (its certificates need "every name, award and date exactly right", zero wrong).
- **The hundred-record measure.** Redman's method, as the module's numbers give it: the people who know the data take the most recent hundred records and mark every field that's wrong; a record with nothing wrong is perfect; the share of perfect records is the measure. Redman's own words: "I call it 'the Friday Afternoon Measurement'" and "many people report that FAM works well any day of the week" (his interview with EWSolutions, updated 19 March 2026); and the 2017 summary in *Harvard Business Review* (Nagle, Redman and Sammon): "assemble 100 data records completed by your department and work through each". Five fields, not the ten to fifteen often advised, because those are the fields the certificate and the ceremony use. Two hours: the length of the meeting usually advised. "A pioneer of data quality": Redman has worked on data quality since the 1980s. The records on screen (Daniel Nunez, S-19362; Priya Desai, S-19518; Hannah Kowalski, S-19740) are drawn for the film, in the example project's style, and aren't in its seed data.
- **79, and its errors.** numbers.md: the first measure, Friday 6 February 2026, on the last hundred records added to December 2025's list, the last list built in full: 79 perfect; 21 records with at least one error, 26 errors in all: names 15 (accents lost 8, cut short 3, swapped 4), awards 6 (a double degree as one award 4, a major printed as the award 2), ceremonies 3, conferral dates 2. All hundred are of students admitted since 2020, so none is in capitals.
- **"Good to about eight either way."** For 79 of 100, the usual margin for a share at 95% is 79 ± 8.0 (71 to 87); the Wilson interval is 70.0 to 85.8 and the exact one 69.7 to 86.5, so "about eight" and "between 71 and 87" hold. This is the margin for a share, not Gosset's formula, which is for an average: the film calls it "Gosset's question", not his answer. It holds only if the hundred are like the rest, which the narration says: the most recent hundred aren't a random sample. The hundred are taken by the day each joined the list (`added_on`), then by key. Databricks' `TABLESAMPLE` would give a sample, but its docs warn that `TABLESAMPLE (num_rows ROWS)` "is not a simple random sample but instead is implemented using LIMIT"; the film uses the most recent, as Redman's method does.
- **The puzzle.** 41 of 2,031 is 2.0%; 21% of December's list (a row per certificate, so a little over 2,031 rows) is over 400. *The hidden factory* explains it: two people fixed most errors by hand before the list went, and the hidden work hid the true error rate (numbers.md). This film says only that someone had been putting most of them right by hand.
- **Databricks' data profiling.** *Data profiling* (updated 11 September 2026): "For each profile, Databricks automatically creates a dashboard"; a snapshot profile "calculates data quality metrics over all data in the table" ("The maximum table size for a snapshot profile is 4TB"). *Create a profile using the Databricks UI* (updated 11 September 2026): in Catalog Explorer, the table's **Quality** tab, **Enable** or **Configure**, then in the **Data Quality Monitoring** dialog, **Data profiling**, **Configure**, and the **Profile type**; "To configure a custom metric, click **Add custom metric**" (Name, Type: Aggregate, Derived or Drift, Input columns, Output type, Definition). *Profile metrics table* (updated 11 September 2026): for a column, `count` ("Number of non-null values"), `num_nulls`, `percent_null`, `distinct_count` (approximate: "This uses the approx_count_distinct function, so the result may not be exact"), `min_len`, `max_len`, `avg_len` ("Minimum/Maximum/Average length of string and binary values"), and `frequent_items` ("Top 100 most frequently occurring items").

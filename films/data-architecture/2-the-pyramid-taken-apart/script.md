## The promise

Every line passes two tests: anyone who has read a report and wondered where its number came from can follow it, and an experienced data or enterprise architect finds nothing wrong in it. The pyramid of data, information, knowledge and wisdom is half right. It's right that a number needs context before it means anything, and that people, not systems, decide. It's wrong in four ways: it's drawn as a one-way factory, with data going in at the bottom, when a question comes first and decides what's recorded; there's no raw data, since every record was designed by someone for some question; its levels aren't separate things, as DAMA says (data is a form of information, and information is a form of data); and its top is vague, and in practice is a person, deciding. So Inés redraws it with arrows both ways: down, a decision asks a question, the question needs a measure and the measure a definition, which decides what's recorded; up, the records answer the measure, the measure answers the question, and a person decides. On the data practice on the map, that's a business object defined once and owned, a data object that realises it, and a measure that traces to both. **Decide what to count first; then the records can answer, and a person decides.**

## The story in one paragraph

In 1972, Chile's government was linking the factories it ran to Santiago, in a project called Cybersyn, and every afternoon their numbers came in by telex. But a number alone didn't say whether anything was wrong: today's cloth at a mill was a good day or a bad one depending on the mill, and with every factory reporting every day, one in trouble would be lost among the ones that were fine. The people in charge needed to see the factories that needed them, and only those. So the project's software, Cyberstride, worked out from each factory's own history what was normal for it, compared each day's numbers with that, and passed on only what had changed. A warning was to go first to the factory's own manager, with time to put it right, and on to Santiago only if it stayed wrong. On 21 March 1972 the software printed its first results, and Stafford Beer, who designed Cybersyn, telexed that it really worked: instead of a wall of figures, the people in charge would see a few warnings, each beside its factory's normal. At the utility, Grace reads one measure every month, interruptions per customer, per year; it looks like a number the systems produce, but it exists only because someone first decided what an interruption is. There's a famous picture of how records become decisions, a pyramid: data at the bottom, then information, knowledge and wisdom. No one person invented it: a poet asked in 1934 where the knowledge lost in information had gone, writers on management drew the levels in the 1980s, and Russell Ackoff made them famous in 1989. Half of it is right: forty-one interruptions mean nothing until you know on which line, over what time and among how many customers, and the measure doesn't renew a line, Grace decides. What's wrong starts with how it's drawn, like a factory; but nothing is recorded until someone decides what to record, and before the outage system logged a single interruption, someone wrote down what one is: power lost to a connection point for more than five minutes. There's no raw data either: someone chose a meter's half hour, its kilowatt hours and its one decimal place. The levels aren't separate things: the same record of a cut is data to the outage system and information to Grace. And the top is vague; in practice, it's a person, deciding. So Inés redraws it with arrows both ways, and on the data practice on the map interruption becomes a business object owned by Grace, the outage system's record of each cut a data object that realises it, and the measure traces to both. In Chile the numbers never came raw from the factories: someone drew each one, chose what mattered and worked out its normal. Grace's measure works the same way, so it counts the same way every year, and the lines that fail most are renewed first. But each cut is recorded by more than one system: which record is the fact?

Someone who has never heard of Cybersyn can answer, from the narration alone, why it was a problem (every factory's numbers came in every afternoon, and a number alone didn't say whether anything was wrong, so a factory in trouble would be lost among the ones that were fine) and what the fix gave people (software that knew each factory's normal and passed on only what had changed, so the people in charge would see a few warnings, each beside its normal). The series follows Cybersyn in all six films (the author, 11 November 2026): this film tells its software, and says its warnings as they were designed ("was to go", "would"), since in practice the results went to CORFO without the warning step; how little of it was built, and its end, come in the last film.

## The reference, and practice

The series follows DAMA-DMBOK2's Data Architecture, and this film takes its line on the pyramid from DMBOK2's first chapter: DAMA knows the classic distinction between data and information, criticises the pyramid for treating them as separate, and uses the words interchangeably ("data is a form of information, and information is a form of data"). The pyramid itself is named once, with where it comes from (T. S. Eliot, 1934; Cleveland, 1982; Zeleny, 1987; Ackoff, 1989), and taken apart with three critiques that practitioners and researchers share: Frické (2009), that it's drawn as a one-way process when concepts and questions come first; Gitelman (2013), that raw data is an oxymoron; and Rowley (2007), who found little agreement on what wisdom is or how a system could hold it. The arrows both ways are the film's own drawing of the same chain *Now the questions appear* drew from a goal down to a measure, here carried on to the definition and the records, and back up to a person.

What practitioners would tell a newcomer, and the film shows: write the definition down before anyone builds the report, and give it an owner; record what the definition needs (here, when each cut starts and ends, at each connection point), not whatever a system happens to keep; ask who decides what with a number before asking where to get it; treat every "raw" feed as designed by someone, and find out what they chose; and draw each measure's traces, to the business concept it counts and to the records it's counted from.

## Sound

The film's palette (12 November 2026, its row in the four new architecture series' palettes register) pairs two instruments the site has, which no film pairs: a **bowed psaltery** and a **slate lithophone**. The register allows a pairing where nothing new fits, and this one is an assumption in the film's assumptions file, with the question on the author's checklist. The bowed psaltery is a triangle of steel strings on a flat box, longest at its base and shortest at its tip, each bowed on its own near its pin, either way: a note starts as the bow takes the string, holds level while the bow moves and rings on for a moment after it leaves, with no vibrato. It's the pyramid's own shape, bowed both ways, as Inés redraws the pyramid with arrows down and up. Its partner, the slate lithophone, is bars of slate struck softly with felt beaters, low (G 2 to D 4), a short, dry note: stones that look raw, though each was cut and tuned by someone for its note, as every record was designed by someone for a question, so here each bar's first overtone sits in tune, four times its note. Kept apart from the two films that have them (*It feels like understanding*'s psaltery, with a steel tongue drum, and *Ethics we can't read, accountability it can't hold*'s slate, with a melodica): the psaltery bows lower (G 3 to D 5) and plays neither film's figures, the bars are tuned where those are raw, and both play dry. Everything is made in code, in [`source/tools/score.py`](source/tools/score.py): nothing is sampled.

Keys, tuned pure from G: G major, and E minor for what goes wrong (a bad day; one factory lost among the ones that were fine; nothing recorded yet). The series' mark, at the title, in the wordless pause after Inés's redrawing and at the end card: G and its fifth, D, apart (the business and the systems), then the third between them, B, which makes the chord (the layer that joins them). The film's own figure, *both ways*: the psaltery bows a line down, D, B, A, G, one note for each arrow that asks for the next thing, and the stones take it back up, G, A, B, D, one for each record that answers; once where Inés redraws the pyramid, and once more, quicker, where Grace's measure is defined first, recorded to fit and read by someone who acts. Nothing from Chile stands for Cybersyn (no Andean flutes, no guitar, no dance rhythm), and no machine rhythm for the telex or the printout.

Music under the opening story; after it, only at a chapter's turn, under a moment on screen or in a pause without words. Every effect is tied to something on screen, one at a time, soft and below about 2.4 kHz: the telex as one soft, muffled texture, a knock as a card arrives, paper as a strip or a printout comes in, a pane of glass set down for each record and question, a pencil on paper, ticks for the pyramid's two right halves and the marked rows, a stamp for "designed, for a question" and for Grace's decision, and a breath of air as things move together. `source/tools/quiet.py` (12 November 2026): after the opening the music sounds 30% of the time and comes in 24 times, once every 11.5 s, at most 4 times in any 30 s; its loud moments sit at -30.4 dB and its loudest notes at -27.7 dB; the effects' loud moments at -45.4 dB, at most 3 beginning in one second; nothing beats, nothing pulses, and no room is laid over the notes. The mix is at -16 LUFS.

## Pause and think

The film stops at the end of three chapters, with one question each ([`site/assets/the-pyramid-taken-apart/think.en.js`](../../../site/assets/the-pyramid-taken-apart/think.en.js), Spanish in `think.es.js`):

- **After *The question comes first*:** what the definition written before the first record shows. Right: a question came before the data, and a definition decided what to record. Near miss: data still comes first, and the definition only sorts the cuts later (the system records what the definition needs: when each cut starts and ends).
- **After *No separate levels, no clear top*:** what one record of a cut, data to the outage system and information to Grace, says about the levels. Right: they aren't separate things; one record is either, depending on who uses it and why. Near miss: the record climbs a level when Grace reads it (it doesn't move; it's both at once).
- **After *Arrows both ways*:** what the arrow down adds. Right: a decision asks a question, which needs a measure and a definition, which decides what's recorded. Near miss: reports go back down so their readers can correct the records (corrections do flow back, but that isn't what the arrow down is for).

## Labs

Candidates for the series' labs and scenarios pack, to build with the series:

- **Draw it both ways:** for a measure the learner knows, write the chain down (decision, question, measure, definition, records) and back up, and find the step nobody wrote down.
- **What was chosen:** given a feed that looks raw (meter readings, a log, a sensor's file), list what someone chose for it: the interval, the unit, the precision, what's left out, and who'd decide differently.
- **Data or information:** for one record, say who uses it as data and who as information, and what changes (nothing in the record; only the question).

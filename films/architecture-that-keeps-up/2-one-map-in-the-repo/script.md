## The promise

Every line passes two tests: someone new to data work can follow it, and an enterprise architect, a data architect or a platform engineer finds nothing wrong in it. Its idea, in the author's words: "Make sure you have a proper git repo for the enterprise architecture view of your platform, so all initiatives and changes are tracked, and agents can help with ideation, information structure (even diagrams in mermaid and drawio). Every other documentation in wikis, videos and presentations comes from there. Remember, a single source of truth!" With his next point: "Architecture must be agile, every time you are updating your current and target state. Don't wait for funding or external projects to do it or you will always end up building legacy solutions. Make sure architecture is a shared capability within your team, starting with you and supported by your principal engineers."

**Takeaway:** "One map in the repository; every change updates it; everything else comes from it."

## The story in one paragraph

In the late 1990s, Ordnance Survey's detailed maps of Great Britain had been made digital as tiles, where a building was only lines: councils, water companies and the emergency services each matched their own records to those lines in their own way, so they couldn't be sure two records meant the same place, and each had to find every change on its own. In 2001 OS MasterMap made the country one seamless database, every feature with its own identifier, a version and a date, kept up to date by surveyors, with users receiving only what changed: everyone could point to the same building, and see what changed and when. In spring 2027 the university's target picture for its data platform exists three times, each changed by hand; on 25 May the data council's slide still shows the old warehouse Helena is asking it to retire. From June the map's source lives in the data team's repository, in ArchiMate's exchange format with its diagrams as text, and every other picture is made from it each night; every change is a pull request with its reason, reviewed by someone else, the platform's code and the map changed together, and a check fails if a new source has no place on the map. The team's agent drafts the change that retires the old warehouse; Noor catches one wrong line and approves the fix. By 17 December, 63 changes of the year are in the map's history, and the council reads that history at its reviews.

## The reference, and practice

The series' reference holds (`../proposal.md`): ArchiMate (The Open Group) for the map and its exchange format; TOGAF's architectures for the layers below strategy; docs as code and architecture decision records (Michael Nygard, 2011); diagrams kept as text (Mermaid, draw.io). Named once each, never taught as products.

- **What the film adopts.** ArchiMate's exchange format as the map's file in the repository: a standard first, with only what's the team's own added (each product's owner). Diagrams kept as text, so a change to a picture is a change anyone can read and review. A decision record beside a big choice: what was decided, and why.
- **Where it extends them.** The frameworks often describe architecture as a separate team's practice, saved for projects. Here it's the data manager's own habit, shared with the principal engineers, updated in the same pull request as the change that moves it, never waiting for a funded project (the author's point 4).
- **The author's own tool.** He built his own tool on ArchiMate for this (named only in the series' plan, never on screen, as the films name no product as their subject); the film says the lesson: standards first, then what's relevant to you.
- **From practice.** Every other document (the architecture tool's views, the wiki, the monthly page's picture, the project managers' page of who does what) is made from the repository each night, so it can't disagree. A check fails if a new source has no place on the map. Agents draft structure and diagrams; a person reviews every change (the agent films' rule).

### 1 · One database, every map · 0:00–2:12

**Narration.** Great Britain, in the late 1990s. Ordnance Survey, the national mapping agency, keeps the most detailed maps of the country. Its paper maps have been turned into digital ones, and cut into tiles, square by square. But on a tile, a building is only lines on a picture. It has no name, no number, nothing to point to. Councils, water companies and the emergency services each keep their own records of the same buildings and roads. Each matches its records to the lines in its own way. So one school can be one thing in a council's records, and another in a water company's. Nobody can be sure that two records mean the same place. And when a new road is built, each of them must find it, and change it, on its own. Each keeping its own copy of the same streets can't go on. In 2001, Ordnance Survey launches OS MasterMap: one seamless database of the whole country, instead of tiles. Every feature in it, about 440 million at first, gets its own identifier, a TOID, with a version and a date. Surveyors, on the ground and from the air, keep it up to date, and users receive only what has changed. Now a council and a water company can point to the same building by its identifier, with no doubt which one they mean. Each change carries its version and its date, so anyone can see what changed, and when. By 2016, it holds over 500 million features. A data team's architecture has the same problem: one picture of its platform, kept in copies that drift apart. Keep one source, date every change, and make every copy from it.

## Sound

**The palette, its own** (reserved for this film in the module's `palettes.md` on 10 November 2026, after checking every `palettes.md` and every `score.py` on main and on every branch that day): **a singing aluminium rod**, held at its middle and stroked lengthwise with rosined fingers, one pure high note, its partials the odd harmonics only, each exactly in tune; stroked in softly with a breath of rosin, held steady, ringing down once the fingers stop; no vibrato, no slide. **Its partner: a wooden ruler** twanged on a desk's edge and damped at once, one short, dry, low note and the knock of the wood, for each change merged.

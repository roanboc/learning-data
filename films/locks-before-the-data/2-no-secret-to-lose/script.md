## The promise

Every line passes two tests: anyone who has ever typed a password can follow it, and a security engineer finds nothing wrong in it. A sign-in secret is bearer paper: a password, a token or a key works for whoever holds it, and every copy works as well as the first. In 1990 the City of London stopped walking bearer paper through its streets once the Bank of England kept the record of who owned what; a platform can do the same for a job, because it knows which job it's running. So the ways a workload proves who it is are ranked by one question, who holds a secret, and for how long: from a person's token at the bottom to an identity the platform gives the job at the top. People have their own ladder, from a shared account to a passkey that never leaves the device and answers only the real site. **The best secret is the one nobody holds.**

## The story in one paragraph

London, 2 May 1990. The money markets still run on paper: Treasury bills and certificates of deposit, promises to pay a set sum on a set day, carried from bank to bank by messengers who bring back the payment. Much of that paper is made out to the bearer, so whoever holds it can cash it. That morning, in a quiet street near the Bank of England, a man with a knife takes one messenger's case: 301 bills and certificates, worth £292 million, as good as cash to whoever holds them; and every messenger in the City carries the same risk every day. The Bank of England had been building an answer since 1988, and on 1 October 1990 it opens the Central Moneymarkets Office: the paper goes into a vault at the Bank and stays there, the Bank's records say who owns each one, and a sale changes the record once both sides approve. Forty-four firms join on the first day, and paper kept at the Bank stays off the streets. A password, a token or a key is bearer paper too. At the university, the nightly job that loads the registrar's records has an identity of its own, `registrar-loader`, owned by Sam Okafor, but it still signs in with Jun Park's personal token, made on 14 March 2024 with no end date: whoever holds it is Jun to the platform, from anywhere, at any hour, and Sam finds it in three places. Sam lays out the ways a job can prove it's the job, worst to best, by who holds a secret and for how long: a person's token or a shared password; a token of the job's own; a secret in a vault, changed by a machine; a certificate, a key the job keeps and never sends; a note from where the job runs, signed for each run; and an identity the platform gives it, vouched for because the platform runs it. On the top two rungs there are still passes, but the platform makes each one for one run, nobody sees it, and it ends within the hour. Sam moves the job to the top, its code's deployments to the second rung, and ends Jun's token: the copies are worthless paper, and when Jun moves team nothing breaks. People climb a ladder of their own, from a shared account and a password alone, through codes by text and an app, to a passkey or a security key, a key that stays on the device and answers only the real site; agents climb the workload's ladder, each as itself. Like the City's paper in 1990, the job stopped carrying a secret, because the platform knows who it is.

## The reference, and practice

The module's reference holds (its proposal, *The reference*): DAMA-DMBOK's data security area for what is protected and why, and zero trust (NIST SP 800-207) for how, where every request proves who it is. For this film's one question, how a person or a workload proves it, the thread took these, named once here; the film teaches what they agree on and names none of them on screen.

| Body of knowledge | What it adds | Where the film uses it |
|---|---|---|
| NIST SP 800-63B-4, *Authentication and authenticator management* (final, July 2025) | Authenticators and their strength; phishing resistance (a key bound to the real site, never sent); passwords checked against lists of breached ones, never changed on a schedule; text codes allowed only with restrictions; syncable passkeys at the middle level, device-bound keys at the highest | *A ladder for people* |
| CISA, *Implementing phishing-resistant MFA* and *Number matching* fact sheets (31 October 2022) | Only FIDO/WebAuthn and PKI are phishing-resistant; an app's push with number matching is an interim step; text and voice codes are the weakest second factor | *A ladder for people* |
| OWASP *Non-Human Identities Top 10* (2025) | The risks of workload credentials: secret leakage, long-lived secrets (NHI7), people using a workload's identity and the other way round (NHI10), secrets not rotated | *Jun's token*, *Who holds a secret?* |
| The OAuth 2.0 bearer token (RFC 6750, 2012) and OpenID Connect | "Any party in possession of a bearer token" can use it: the standards' own word; short-lived tokens signed by an issuer the receiver trusts, which is what federation exchanges | The opening's link; *Who holds a secret?* |
| The cloud and platform vendors' guidance for workload sign-in (Microsoft Learn, Databricks and GitHub docs, read 5 November 2026) | The same order everywhere: an identity the platform gives the workload; federation for workloads that run elsewhere; certificates over secrets; a secret only in a vault, rotated, where nothing else works; personal tokens kept out of jobs | *Who holds a secret?*, *Up the ladder* |

**Where the film differs, on purpose.** The standards rank authenticators by assurance levels and the vendors by their own feature names; the film ranks both ladders by one plain question, who holds a secret and for how long, which every source's order agrees with. It doesn't teach assurance levels, OAuth flows or token formats: *Workloads without secrets* and *People and groups* show how each rung is built.

**Practice.** What practitioners recognise: a scheduled job that runs on someone's personal token because that was the quickest way to start, and that breaks the day they leave; a secret pasted into a job's settings, a notebook, a laptop and a chat, so nobody can say how many copies there are; a secret in a vault that still has to be opened with a secret, until the vault is opened by the platform's own identity; "temporary" exceptions with no owner; and, for people, codes by text message phished in real time. And what works: every new workload starts at the top of the ladder, a lower rung needs a written reason and an owner, and personal tokens never run jobs.

**The author's practice** (3 November 2026): passwordless first, "so secrets don't need to be managed by people", "similar to workspace identities in Fabric instead of using service accounts", and the methods still in use today ranked from best to worst. The film says it in its own words: "That's the goal: nobody makes, keeps, copies or changes a secret."

### 6 · A ladder for people · 6:46–8:18

**Narration.** People sign in too, and their ladder is much the same. At the bottom, a shared account: nobody can tell who did what, and nobody can leave without changing it for everyone. A password alone can be guessed, used again on other sites, or typed into a fake page. A code by text message is better, but a phone number can be moved to a thief's phone, and the code typed into a fake page too. An app on your own phone is harder to steal, but a convincing fake page can still pass on your code, or get your yes. At the top, a passkey, or a security key you plug in. It's a key that stays on your device. You unlock it with your face, your fingerprint or a PIN, and those never leave it either. It answers only the real site, so a fake page gets nothing. And the platform keeps only the matching half, which opens nothing on its own. Nothing to remember, and nothing to type. Agents climb the job's ladder too: each signs in as itself, never on a person's token.

## Rigour to check before recording

Checked on 5 November 2026; the sources are listed below.

- **The money markets of 1990 ran on paper.** "Transactions in negotiable instruments in the London money markets have until very recently had to be settled by messenger delivery of paper securities in exchange for payment, normally in the form of Town cheques" (Bank of England, *Quarterly Bulletin*, November 1990, "Central Moneymarkets Office", by R. J. Wallan, p. 514). The film's "a messenger walks it across the City, and comes back with the payment" is that; "the payment" stands for the Town cheque.
- **Promises to pay.** A Treasury bill is short-term government debt, sold at a discount and paid at its face value on a set day; a certificate of deposit is a bank's, for money deposited for a set term, at a set rate. "Promises to pay a set sum on a set day: the government's, and the banks'" says no more. The bulletin calls the market's instruments "sterling money-market bearer securities"; Treasury bills were "negotiable, bearer" instruments (UK Debt Management Office, *UK Government cash management: the new framework*, 1998).
- **The theft.** On 2 May 1990, a 58-year-old messenger of a money broker was robbed at knifepoint in a quiet street in the City, near the Bank of England, of 301 Treasury bills and certificates of deposit worth about £292 million (Guinness World Records, "Largest single street robbery"; Crime+Investigation, "The £292m heist: inside the City Bonds Robbery", which names Nicholas Lane; the Bank's bulletin, which says King William Street; both are a few minutes' walk from the Bank of England, so the film says "near the Bank of England"). "Whoever is physically holding the bearer bonds is entitled to the money" (Crime+Investigation); the papers are often called bearer bonds; the film says what the Bank says they were. All but two were recovered later, in a hunt that reached New York, Zurich and Glasgow; the film doesn't follow it, since its point is the risk the paper carried, not the chase. The messenger isn't named, and the thief isn't shown: a shadow on a wall and the case gone. "If they're cashed before word of the theft gets round, the money is gone" is what made bearer paper dangerous to its owners; the film doesn't say who would have borne the loss.
- **The answer, and its dates.** "The desirability of a book-entry transfer system for money-market securities has been apparent for some time and attempts were made by the market itself to develop a system in 1986-88 ... A decision was taken in principle in the autumn of 1988 ... the Bank put forward detailed plans for the CMO in August 1989"; "The Central Moneymarkets Office (CMO), launched on 1 October" (1990) (the bulletin, p. 514). So "has been building an answer since 1988": the theft didn't start it; the bulletin names it as the risk the office ends.
- **How it worked.** "Once lodged, they are immobilised in a safe-custody vault at the Bank of England, where, provided they are not subsequently sold to a non-participant, they can remain until maturity. Meanwhile a detailed description of each of the individual instruments and a record of ownership is maintained by the central computer system"; "instruments are not allowed to move from one member's account to that of another until both members have authorised the transfer" (p. 515). The film's "stays there" is the vault until a paper is due, as long as it's sold within the office, which the film doesn't need to say; "once both sides have approved it" is the bulletin's positive acceptance. The screen of 1990 is the film's: the office's terminals were in members' offices, over the Stock Exchange's network; members are called "Member A" and "Member B", as the bulletin's chart calls them.
- **Forty-four, and the Bank's reasons.** "By the launch, on 1 October, forty-four institutions had joined CMO as direct members; three more have joined subsequently" (p. 514); 109 more firms said they would take part through an agent, which the film doesn't count. "CMO will contribute to this aim by improving security, avoiding the risks and expense of moving paper physically around the City. These risks were well illustrated last summer when £292 million of Treasury bills and certificates of deposit were stolen from a City messenger in King William Street" (p. 514); the bulletin says "summer" for 2 May. The card quotes the bulletin exactly, from its middle: "…avoiding the risks and expense of moving paper physically around the City." The narration says it plainly: "to avoid the risk and the cost of moving paper around the City, as the theft in May had shown".
- **"Paper kept at the Bank stays off the streets."** True of paper in the office; other paper still moved by hand for some years. In 2003 the office's work moved into CREST, the system that settles UK securities as records; the film doesn't follow it.
- **"Bearer paper" for a secret.** The standards' own word: a bearer token is one that "any party in possession of" can use "in any way that any other party in possession of it can", "without demonstrating possession of a cryptographic key" (RFC 6750, 2012). A password, a personal token and a client secret all work that way; a certificate's or a passkey's private key doesn't travel, which is why they rank higher.
- **The job.** `registrar_nightly_load`, at 02:00; Jun Park's personal token, made 14 March 2024 with no end date; the job's identity `registrar-loader`, owned by Sam Okafor, its rights from the loader role (the module's `numbers.md`; *Who is it, really?*, which ends there, agreed with its thread on 5 November 2026). "From anywhere": the university's one workspace is still open to the internet until *Walls, doors and keys*. "More than two and a half years": 14 March 2024 to November 2026. The year is the module's, changed from 2025 on 5 November 2026 by *Workloads without secrets*: on the platform the module builds on, new personal tokens have had at most two years' life since 5 December 2024, so a token with no end date must be older (Azure Databricks release notes, December 2024, read 5 November 2026); the film names no product. The three copies are this film's.
- **The workload ladder, worst to best.** The order is the vendors' and the standards' (Microsoft Learn, *Managed identities for Azure resources* and *Workload identity federation*, and its guidance to prefer certificates to client secrets and avoid secrets where possible; Databricks docs, *Authorize service principal access with OAuth* and *Enable workload identity federation for GitHub Actions*, where "the Databricks SDKs and the Databricks CLI automatically fetch workload identity tokens from GitHub and exchange them for Databricks OAuth tokens", and their advice to use OAuth rather than personal tokens; GitHub docs, *About security hardening with OpenID Connect*; OWASP *NHI Top 10*, NHI7 *Long-lived secrets* and NHI10 *Human use of NHI*). The film's plain names, with the terms of art: an identity the platform gives it (a managed identity, or a role or service account attached to a cloud resource); a note from where it runs (workload identity federation, an OpenID Connect token from the platform the workload runs on, exchanged for a short-lived token by a platform that trusts that issuer and that subject); a certificate (a client certificate or a signed client assertion: the private key signs, and only the signature is sent); a secret in a vault, changed by a machine (a client secret in a key vault, rotated automatically); a token of the job's own (a service principal's personal access token); a person's token or a shared account's password.
- **"Nobody sees it, and it ends within the hour."** The platform's tokens for the top two rungs are short-lived and fetched by the workload's own process: a federated exchange gives an access token of about an hour, from an identity token of minutes; a managed identity's tokens are cached and refreshed by the platform's libraries, and fetched from an endpoint only the workload can reach. They are still bearer tokens while they live, which the film says ("there are still passes"); what changes is that nobody stores, copies or rotates them. "That's the goal: nobody makes, keeps, copies or changes a secret" is the author's "secrets don't need to be managed by people".
- **"A machine changes it every month."** Rotation periods vary; a month is the film's example. "To open the vault, the job needs a way in" is shown, not said, in *Up the ladder*: the vault is opened by the job's own platform identity, the top rung, which is how the vendors advise it.
- **The certificate.** "It proves who it is by signing, so the key itself never crosses the wire": true of a client certificate (mutual TLS) and of a signed client assertion; the certificate has an end date and is renewed, and its private key must be stored, ideally where it can't be exported. "The job · a year" on its pill is a typical certificate's life, the film's example.
- **The people's ladder, worst to best.** NIST SP 800-63B-4 (final, July 2025; superseding SP 800-63B, withdrawn 1 August 2025): phishing-resistant authenticators bind to the site's origin; syncable passkeys are allowed at the middle assurance level and device-bound keys are required at the highest; text and voice codes are "restricted" authenticators. CISA, *Implementing phishing-resistant MFA* (31 October 2022): FIDO/WebAuthn and PKI are the only phishing-resistant forms; text and voice the weakest; number matching an interim step against push fatigue. FIDO Alliance, *Passkeys*: the private key stays on the device, unlocked by the device's own biometric or PIN, which isn't sent; the service keeps only the public key. "A phone number can be moved to a thief's phone" is SIM swapping; "a convincing fake page can still pass on your code, or get your yes" is an adversary in the middle relaying a sign-in in real time. "Nothing to remember, and nothing to type" is true of a passkey's sign-in; the PIN that unlocks it never leaves the device.
- **"Everyone signs in once, with a passkey."** Single sign-on through the university's identity provider; *People and groups* builds it (Microsoft Entra ID, synced to Databricks). Admin accounts with security keys are *Power you switch on*'s.
- **Agents.** An agent is a workload: it gets an identity of its own and an owner, and never runs on a person's token (*Who is it, really?*; the module's rule that agents never get admin accounts). *AI agents in data work* shows how: *Why we govern them* ("Second, an identity: the name it works under") and *Tools are hands, and every hand is a permission* ("Its own identity, never a person's."); the film says only that agents climb the same ladder.
- **No release status** is shown or said (the author's rule of 5 November 2026). Read for the record on 5 November 2026: Databricks' OAuth token federation is labelled Public Preview on some of its docs pages; the film names no product.

## The promise

Every line passes two tests: anyone who has changed something other people depend on can follow it, and an experienced data governance lead finds nothing wrong in it. Sweden switched sides of the road in one morning, and no one was surprised, because everyone knew what would change, when, and what to do. A change to data that people depend on needs the same three things. The symptom is common: a change board that meets once a month, with a form answered from memory, because nobody keeps a record of who uses what. What's worth keeping is small. A record of who uses what, with an owner on every line. An impact note on every change request: what it reaches, whether it breaks, and who must be told, filled in with the owners. A notice in each team's own words, with a date and help. And, for a change that can't be added, the old edition kept beside the new one until a date everyone knows. The data's owner decides whether a change of meaning breaks; each user's owner decides when they're ready; the data team makes the change and writes the note; governance makes the note a step of every change. **Change control is a step in the change, not a meeting.**

## The story in one paragraph

At ten to five on the morning of 3 September 1967, every car still on Sweden's roads stopped, crossed from the left side to the right, and at five drove on. Parliament had decided four years before, and a commission had prepared ever since: new signs went up on the right and stayed covered until one night, a booklet reached every home, the change's sign was on milk cartons, and thousands of buses got doors on the other side. The change was huge, and it surprised no one. At the university, the word *award* has just changed: microcredentials of at least 6 credit points now count, and this morning Mei, who owns the word, signed off the new meaning on the change notice the nightly check sent on Monday night (*One word, one home*). Now Jun must change what the reports count. The binder's policy 19 says changes are assessed for impact before they're made; it runs as a change board once a month, with a form answered from memory, and the next one meets after the draft submission. To see what a change reaches, you need to know who uses what, and nobody keeps a record of all of it. So Jun and Yusuf start a record of who uses what, and each owner writes their own line: the two reports that count awards, the wallet app, Planning's dashboard of learners near an award and Finance's forecast. Jun's change request now carries an impact note. His first draft relabels the big microcredentials as awards, and the note lists five things it reaches: the two reports, which asked for it, and three that didn't. Planning's dashboard and Finance's forecast count the credit learners have towards an award, part of which comes from microcredentials, so their numbers would move; learners' wallets would show microcredentials as awards; and the table would look the same. Mei, who owns the meaning, decides it breaks. Jun adds instead: every microcredential stays one, and a new mark says which count as an award, yes or no. The note now lists three things, the ones that use credentials, and nothing breaks. A change that can't be added gets a new edition beside the old one, kept until a date: credentials' edition 1 retires on 31 March 2027. Jun writes to the wallet team in their words, with a date and his help. The draft submission counts with the new mark from Wednesday, and Planning's census report from its next run, so the two numbers will agree. Each owner says when they're ready, and the change goes in, before Wednesday's draft runs (*The facts the films share*). Yusuf takes policy 19 off the shelf: an impact note on every change request, a record every team adds its line to, and the board only for the rare change that needs it. One question is left for the next film: when a table that many teams use is late, who answers for it?

## Labs (candidates)

1. **Breaking or not?** Eight changes to a table other teams use, each with its impact note: sort them into "adds" (nothing changes for anyone), "breaks" (a new edition beside the old one, kept until a date), or "changes a meaning" (breaks, though the table looks the same). Two are the film's. One is a fix to a wrong number, which needs no new edition but does need telling.
2. **Who must be told?** From a page of *Who uses what*, fill in the impact note for a change: what it reaches, whether it breaks, and who to ask. Then say who decides whether it breaks, and who says when they're ready.
3. **In their words.** Rewrite three notes from the data team's words into the words of the people who read them: a planner, a finance analyst and an app team.

## Rigour to check before recording

Checked on 21 October 2026 (Dagen H) and 27 October 2026 (the rest); the sources are listed below.

- **Dagen H, the hour.** On Sunday 3 September 1967, every vehicle on the road stopped at 04:50, changed to the right, and waited, "the ten minutes' standstill" (*de tio minuternas stillestånd*, Swedish Wikipedia, *Högertrafikomläggningen*); at 05:00 it drove on. A general traffic ban held across the whole country from 01:00 to 06:00 ("Ett generellt trafikförbud rådde i hela landet från klockan 01.00", M Sverige, 1 September 2017), with longer ones in some towns (Stockholm's from 10:00 on Saturday 2 September). So "every car still on Sweden's roads": only the few allowed were on them.
- **The decision and the commission.** The Riksdag decided on 10 May 1963, by 294 votes to 50. Statens högertrafikkommission (HTK) was set up on 28 June 1963 to plan and carry out the switch, and closed in 1967: "four years". The referendum of 16 October 1955 had gone 82.9% against, of valid votes; the film doesn't mention it (one source says "of the population", which is wrong).
- **Signs.** New signs for the right were put up beside the old ones and covered; in the night to 3 September the covers came off (Norrbottens museum, 1 September 2017; Smålandsposten, 31 August 2017, has 20,000 people at work on the day "to uncover new signs and cover old ones"). Counts differ: about 300,000 (Norrbottens museum), about 350,000 (a 1966 estimate, in English Wikipedia) and 360,000 (Smålandsposten), so the film gives no number. Smålandsposten's 130,000 reminder signs (one source) aren't in the film.
- **The booklet and the cartons.** A 32-page booklet, printed in 8 million copies, went to every household in Sweden the week before (Norrbottens museum). The change's sign was on milk cartons, and even on underwear (English Wikipedia; Smålandsposten's *mjölkpaket* and *H-kalsonger*). The film draws a plain letter H, not the 1967 logo.
- **Buses.** About 8,000 buses with doors on the wrong side had to be rebuilt or replaced (Smålandsposten); English Wikipedia gives over 1,000 new buses and about 8,000 rebuilt; Swedish Wikipedia about 7,500 buses in all. "Thousands" holds across them.
- **Afterwards.** In September and October 1967, half as many people died on Sweden's roads as in the same months of 1966 (M Sverige; Norrbottens museum's monthly figures), and accidents rose again in 1968 and 1969. The film claims only that the change surprised no one, not that it made the roads safer.
- **The name.** *Dagen H*, "H Day", the H for *högertrafik*, traffic on the right. Said once ("Dahgen Hoh" for the voice), never written on screen.
- **1967's streets and clothes.** Police directing traffic in dark uniforms, peaked caps and white gloves. Stockholmskällan's record of the information film of 1967–68 shows the switch at Slussen, with the police directing it by radio at five in the morning; Swedish Wikipedia has the police at Kungsgatan, Stockholm, on 3 September 1967. Check the drawings against period photographs before painting. The crossroads is a town's, not a particular one.
- **The university's story** follows *The facts the films share*. The glossary's *award* changed on Monday of Yusuf's first week; that night the nightly check sent a change notice listing what the new words touch and the owner of each part, and Mei signed it on Tuesday morning, the narration's "this morning" (*One word, one home*). The 6 credit points are one business rule, owned by Mei, and the new mark follows it. The notice lists where the word is used (three things, from the copies of its words); the record lists every report and app that uses the table of credentials, each written in by its owner, so it also has the draft submission and Finance's forecast, and chapter 3 says nobody keeps a record of all of them, not that nobody wrote any down. Jun's change, on Tuesday, makes the counts follow the new meaning: the new mark goes in before Wednesday's draft submission runs, which is how the draft counts 4,867. Planning's census report ran on the Friday before (4,812), and counts by the new mark from its next run, so on submission day, Tuesday 24 November 2026, weeks after the draft, both say 4,867 (*Teachers of data*). The film's own scenes say only "Today", the note's "Wednesday" and "Thursday", and the last shot's "Thursday". The draft submission is the registrar's office's and Planning's census report Clara's, as *Off the shelf* shows them.
- **Plain words for the first pack.** A change request, not a pull request; a check, not a test; a table or a report, not a model; a mark, not a column; an edition, not a version; the record of who uses what, not exposures and lineage; the impact note, not the check's report. The terms come back with the tools in films 14 and 18.

- **The sample's numbers don't move.** The example project's microcredentials all carry 5 credit points, so neither draft moves a number in it. The story's 55 microcredentials of 6 or more (4,867 less 4,812) are invented (*The facts the films share*); the table on screen shows a 6-point microcredential as the story's, beside the sample's real rows.
- **The old edition.** Credentials' edition 2 replaced a yes-or-no for a withdrawn credential with a status (DEC-STU-07); edition 1 has `deprecation_date: 2027-03-31` (`models/core/student/_core_student__models.yml`, line 221 on main). The brief made this change a new version; the film follows the author's rule of 20 October 2026: prefer adding, and a new edition only as a last resort, when a change can't be added. The change is on credentials, not on `core_award`, which the brief named: the number reported counts awards conferred, which are credentials, and `core_award` is the list of awards the university offers.
- **For films 14 and 18, not this film.** The check that writes the impact into the pull request (`scripts/check/impact.py`, on pull request #53), contracts and how they tell adding from breaking, versions and deprecation dates, exposures and their owners, and the reviewers file (`CODEOWNERS`). Also what the checks don't show: edition 2 is built a little each night (DEC-STU-08), so a new mark is empty on existing rows until the table is rebuilt in full, while every check still passes (a scratch run on 20 October 2026); Jun rebuilds it in full on Tuesday night.
- **The change board.** A change advisory board is ITIL's. ITIL 4 renamed the practice *change enablement*, gives each kind of change a change authority suited to its risk, and keeps a board for the changes that need one (the practice guide is PeopleCert's; the summaries below were read). DORA's research found that approval by an external body, such as a change advisory board, didn't lower the change failure rate and slowed delivery; it recommends review in the work. So the film keeps the board for the rare change, and names neither.
- **Add, don't break** is parallel change, or expand and contract (Danilo Sato, 2014, on Martin Fowler's site). Data contracts agree: adding is safe; an intended breaking change comes as a new version beside the old one, with a date for the old one to go.
- **Not named in the film:** ITIL, DORA, DAMA-DMBOK (only its cover, on Yusuf's desk, as decoration), dbt, Databricks, or any product or service.
- **Marta Nowak** is invented, as are all the university's people.

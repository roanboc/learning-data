## The promise

Every line passes two tests: someone who has only heard of AI agents can follow it, and an engineering lead who runs GitHub, dbt and Databricks for a data team finds nothing wrong in it. The series takes Planning's agent from a helper on Jun's sign-in to a colleague with a place on the university's map; this film is stage 4, *A contributor that proposes*, at its fullest hand-off. It follows the planning agent's first pull request from its evidence to production: the checks on what changed and what depends on it, a diff of the data, the governance review agent's review for reference, the approvals of the people the map names for each step, and a release that a second person approves. The author's practice is its spine: every change to production is approved by a second person, for agents as for people; agents are never approvers, and an agent's review is only for reference. The film shows the rules that make that hold and where each one is set: on `main` in GitHub, in the code owners and the teams written from the map's roles, at the environment the release waits at, and in the bundle the release deploys to Databricks as its own service principal. How the project's checks, owners and contracts were built is *In the weeds of data crafting*'s; this film names what each is for, in a line, and builds nothing again.

**Takeaway:** "An agent proposes; a person releases."

## The story in one paragraph

Today, at the university: on Tuesday 3 November 2026, the planning agent, Planning's contributor since Monday, opened its first pull request. It changes Planning's class plan for next semester, 2027 Semester 1: how many classes each of 1,236 units runs, which the faculties hire their tutors from, in November, for February. Until now the plan put every unit's students in classes of 25, but a laboratory holds 16, and every faculty plans with that number. A change that dropped a unit, or doubled its classes, would leave students without a class, or pay tutors for classes that never run; and an agent that could approve its own change could put one there before anyone had looked. That had to change before the agent started. So every change, an agent's or a person's, goes through review, with the rules set in the tools: the agent hands in its evidence; checks build what changed and what depends on it; a diff shows the data before and after; the governance review agent reviews it, for reference; people approve it, each in their role on the map; and a second person releases it to production. Jun, Clara, Mei and Noor saw exactly what would change, more classes in 161 units and nothing else, and the faculties can hire tutors for 263 more classes in time, with a place for every student. The film then follows the pull request: Clara's ticket on Monday, the glossary's *class size* read first, the agent's own branch and schema, its stage on the map, its two tests and its evidence (*What the agent hands in*); dbt's build of what changed, the impact report and the diff (*What changed, and what it touches*); the governance review agent's comment, its finding of 31 units with no class size, Clara's yes and the agent's second push (*An agent's review, for reference*); the map's steps and roles, GitHub's code owners and teams written from them, and the rules on `main` (*Who approves, from the map*); Jun's merge, the night in test, Noor's approval on Wednesday morning and the release as its own service principal (*A person releases*); and since Wednesday, the faculties' plan, with the lesson (*An agent proposes, a person releases*).

## The reference, and practice

The module's reference for changes to production is the practice of review and release that software teams share, with segregation of duties at its heart, shown in GitHub, dbt and Databricks, the tools the series' last three films use. The film names no framework in its narration: it teaches what they share, in plain words, on the university's own pull request.

| Body of knowledge | What it adds | Where the film uses it |
|---|---|---|
| DORA, *Streamlining change approval* (dora.dev, last updated 30 October 2025, read 5 November 2026) | Segregation of duties as a common requirement, "which states that changes must be approved by someone other than the author, thus ensuring that no individual has end-to-end control over a process"; change approvals "best implemented through peer review during the development process, supplemented by automation to detect, prevent, and correct bad changes early"; "Use peer review to meet the goal of segregation of duties, with reviews, comments, and approvals captured in the team's development platform as part of the development process"; and "no evidence was found to support the hypothesis that a more formal, external review process was associated with lower change fail rates" | The path: evidence, checks and a diff (the automation), then people's approvals in the pull request, captured in GitHub: "Now every change, an agent's or a person's, goes through review, and the rules are set in the tools." |
| ISO/IEC 27001:2022, Annex A, control 5.3, *Segregation of duties*, and control 8.32, *Change management* | Conflicting duties kept apart, and changes to systems made through a change management procedure: the controls an auditor asks a data team to show | "Nobody can approve their own pull request, and the latest push must be approved by someone who didn't push it." "Whoever starts a release can't approve it, even an approver." |
| GitHub's rules, as GitHub documents them (rulesets, code owners, deployment environments, checked 5 November 2026) | Rules on a branch that hold for everyone; reviewers named for each file; approvals from designated teams for files that match a pattern; an environment that waits for a reviewer, who can't be whoever started the run | *Who approves, from the map* and *A person releases* |
| His practice (the module's plan, 3 November 2026) | Every change to production approved by a second person, for agents as for people; agents are never approvers, and an agent's review is only for reference; an agent opens a pull request from its own space, and its owner answers for it | The lesson, and every chapter |

**Where the film adopts, extends or differs.** It adopts DORA's way: peer review inside the team's own platform, with the automation doing the heavy checking (dbt's build of what changed, the impact report, the data's diff), and every approval recorded in the pull request. It extends it to agents, from practice: an agent may open a pull request, but its review is only for reference and its approval never counts, because the rules ask for people or teams; and who reviews and approves each step comes from the roles on the university's map, written into GitHub, so a new person in a role takes over its reviews. It differs in one place, on purpose: it keeps a second person's release before production, as the author's practice and an auditor's segregation of duties ask, where DORA warns against heavyweight approval outside the team. The film keeps that step light: one person in the team's own platform, a release approver named on the map, who says yes at a gated environment once the night's test has passed; no board, no form. The tools show the ideas and don't lead them: the rules come first, and each is shown where it's set.

**Practice, from the author (the plan's insights):** every change to production approved by a second person, for agents as for people; agents never approvers, their review only for reference; an agent at stage 4 opens a pull request from its own space, under its own identity, and its owner reviews every change; automate deterministically first, so the checks and the diff run on every pull request and no agent decides what passes. A practitioner will recognise the first pull request an agent opened under its own name, the comment from a bot that nobody needed to answer, the approval that went stale with one more push, and the release that waited overnight for the one person allowed to say yes.

## Rigour to check before recording

As the author set on 5 November 2026 (10:39), the film never shows or says a feature's release status (preview, beta, generally available): not in the narration, on screen, in the captions, on its page or in Pause and think. The status read on the day is kept only in *Sources*, with its date.

- **The rules on `main` (GitHub Docs, checked 5 November 2026).** "Pull request authors cannot approve their own pull requests." Code owners must have write access, and are users or teams: "The people you choose as code owners must have write permissions for the repository. When the code owner is a team, that team must be visible and it must have write permissions." The pull request rule's options: required approvals, "require reviews from code owners", "require an approval from someone other than the last person to push to a branch" (the most recent reviewable push, `require_last_push_approval`), stale approvals dismissed on a new push, conversations resolved. The required reviewer rule: approvals from designated teams for files that match a pattern; "For an approval from a team member to count, the team must have write permissions (or higher) for the repository"; for an organization's repositories (the project's own repository is in its organization, `uni-example`). A ruleset's bypass list empty: nobody bypasses it, an administrator included; changing the ruleset itself is a settings change that the audit log records, which the film doesn't call skipping.
- **An agent's approval.** A GitHub App with pull request rights can submit a review, and GitHub would let it approve. So the film says the governance review agent *never* approves and *never* blocks (its rules, in its role on the map: "Never approves"), not that it can't; and it says that its approval never counts because every rule that asks for an approval names people: code owners and the required reviewer rule's teams are users or teams, an App can't be a team member, and every file in the project has a code owner (`*`, the analytics engineer). The planning agent can't approve its own pull request at all (its author).
- **Merging.** GitHub documents no rule that takes merging alone away from an App that can push to its own branches, so the film says the agents *don't* merge (their rules) and that merging isn't releasing; it never says they *can't* (the series' `numbers.md`, *What GitHub's rules can and can't say about merging*).
- **The review agent's comment.** GitHub's three ways to submit a review: "Select **Comment** to leave general feedback without explicitly approving the changes or requesting additional changes." Its App can't push (Contents: read), so it changes no file. Its stage for this task is 1 (`numbers.md`, *The three agents on the map*): it flags and suggests, and each finding names who decides.

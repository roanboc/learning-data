# Governance you can use · Governed in Unity Catalog: script

*The script of *Governed in Unity Catalog*, film 2 of 6 of *Governance you can use*, the third series of data governance, in nine chapters, in English, painted in watercolour. Drafted on 28 October 2026 and made the same day, when the author asked for the films of the three series to be made and merged one after another. It follows the plan the author approved on 23 October 2026 (film 15 of that plan: the tools films open on a scene of the university's own, on a line from the film they expand), and the rule of 28 October 2026 for every opening (scenario, problem, solution and benefits, each said outright; *Opening story* quotes them). Every file on screen is the example project's own, with what this film adds to it (*What the example project has*); a table or a column made up for the film is drawn in pencil and says so ("a sketch", "built tomorrow"). The platform's pages are drawn from Databricks' documentation as it stood on 28 October 2026, and each feature's status is said where it isn't generally available. As the author asked for these films, it isn't rendered here: the release workflow renders it.*

## The promise

Every line passes two tests: someone who has never opened a data platform can follow it, and a platform engineer or a privacy officer finds nothing wrong in it. *Who may see it* drew the line between columns: each column other teams read has a class, one rule shows stars in place of direct personal data, and access is for a purpose, until a date. This film shows the platform's side, in Databricks' Unity Catalog: the build writes each column's class as a governed tag; one attribute-based policy masks every column tagged direct, in every table, including tables built later; the learner code, a scrambled student number, stays readable for counting, but it's still personal; access goes to groups, never to people, with end dates kept where the groups are; lineage shows where a column goes; the audit log says who read what; and the access review reads the grants themselves. The project says what each column is, and the platform holds the line. **The platform enforces what the metadata says.**

## The story in one paragraph

Today, in the platform team's room, Yusuf asks Sam Okafor who can read the learner table, which holds every learner's name, email and student number. When Yusuf first asked what slowed him down, Sam said: tell me which column, I'll protect it in minutes. Nobody could tell him, so access was all or nothing, given by hand: a form, an emailed approval, and three weeks later the whole table, to one person, with no end date. Sam finds forty-one people who can read it; nine have moved to other jobs and still see every learner's name and email. Now each build tells the platform which columns identify a person, from the classes in the project; one policy shows stars in those columns to everyone outside the registrar's office, on every table, even tables not built yet; and access goes to groups, until a date. The film then opens the platform: the catalog, a schema for each layer, and the Discover page, which lays the tables out by area, from a tag each build sets from its model's domain; the governed tag `personal_data`, written by every build; one policy by tag, and Clara reading the learner table with stars where the names are; the learner code, a scrambled student number that anyone with the registrar's list could match, so still personal; a Planning group that Clara joins until 15 December, in the university's directory, which the platform reads; lineage down to the column; the audit log, in preview, that says who read the table and when; and December's access review, read from the grants, where forty-one people give way to two groups. It ends with Sam protecting a column in minutes, because the project tells him which.

### 3 · A tag from the class · 2:09–2:50

Platform and solution architecture

The fourth series on enterprise architecture. Ravi Menon, who leads the utility's data platform, shows why it's a platform and not an application: what's inside it, why one is simpler than many, where it meets the applications, how security runs through it, and how a solution is designed on it.

One thread: the road every domain shares

Billing, outages and metering each have their applications; the data platform is the road they share. Film by film, Ravi Menon draws its parts, keeps it to one, joins it to the integration platform, secures it end to end, designs one solution on it, makes it the road teams choose, and makes sure it can change.

One story, film by film: Japan's Gokaidō

FilmOpens withStill true today
An application or a platform?Ieyasu's post stations, Japan, 1601A road every domain shares carries their business; what it carries stays the sender's.
Inside the platformHakone's station, Japan, 1618The road's hardest stage needed a station of its own, kept by the road's owner for every traveller.
One platform, or manyJapan's toll barriers, 1462 to 1635Every owner of a stretch of river or road could take a toll; one set of rules for the roads let travellers pay only for what they used.
Where it meets the applicationsThe farmers' horses of Shinano, Japan, 1764Farmers' horses carried loads straight through the mountains, beside the stations' relay; a ruling set which goods went which way.
Security all the way throughThe Hakone checkpoint, Japan, about 1619A woman leaving Edo showed a pass that described her, checked against her at the gate; nobody could go round, and the road stayed open to everyone else.
One solution, end to endAlternate attendance, Japan, from 1635Each lord's journey to Edo was planned before it set out, with a notice sent ahead to every station, so the stations' porters and horses served everyone, and each procession found what it needed.
A road people chooseThe pilgrimage to Ise, Japan, the 1700sVillages paid in together and sent two or three by lot, priests from Ise lodged and guided them, and guidebooks listed every station, so ordinary people chose to go: in 1771, more than two million crossed the ferry into Ise.
Built to changeThe Tōkaidō's last years and the railway, Japan, the 1860s to 1889The relay was kept for over 250 years by adding horses and calling on villages, until its cost broke the stations and the villages; in 1872 it ended, and in 1889 the railway along the Tōkaidō's route took Tokyo to Kobe in about twenty hours.

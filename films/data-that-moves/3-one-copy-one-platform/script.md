# Data that moves · One copy, one platform: script

*One copy, one platform* is film 3 of 3 of *Data that moves*, the first series of *Modern data basics*. It runs 5:43. In Amsterdam in 1609, merchants stop weighing bags of clipped coin and pay each other in one ledger; at the university, the casual claims app writes each claim once, on Lakebase, and everyone who needs a claim reads that one row, through Unity Catalog. Transactional and analytical work on one copy of the data, governed once: one copy, one platform, not one engine. Light on purpose: it names the idea, shows it once in Databricks, gives the architectural advice, and hands over to the official docs, linked on its page.

## The promise

For someone new to data: why keeping one copy of the data, which the app writes and everyone else reads where it's written, removes the pipeline, the second platform and the copy that's always a little behind; and that one thing may still move, on purpose, under a contract. For someone who builds platforms: the architectural choice behind it, two purpose-built engines over one logical copy under one governance, a single writer for each table, and what still needs a contract (a system you buy), with Databricks' own docs for the setup. Both leave with one sentence: keep one copy, governed once.

## The story in one paragraph

Around 1600, Amsterdam's merchants pay each other in coin, of 800 to 1,000 kinds, much of it clipped or worn: every payment means weighing, nobody knows what a bag is worth, and a merchant owed a thousand guilders may get coin worth about nine hundred. In 1609 the city opens the Wisselbank: merchants bring their coin once, the bank writes its value on their own page, and from then on a payment is a line moved from page to page, with no coin carried, while the ledger shows what each merchant holds. Data has its bags of coin too. At the university, tutors' claims went round by email, and payroll and Finance each kept a copy that never quite agreed. When the university built an app for claims, the usual design was ready: the app on its own database, a pipeline copying each change to an analytics platform, reports on the copy. Noor, the data architect, chose one copy on one platform instead: the app is built on Lakebase, the Postgres database in Databricks, where Linh writes each claim once, as a row; Postgres serves the app, and the lakehouse reads the same rows in place for payroll's list, the job that builds the hours to pay and Finance's casual teaching cost, all through Unity Catalog. The pipeline, the second database and the work of keeping two copies in step are gone; the integration services come together on one platform; and one thing still moves, on purpose: the hours to pay, to the payroll system the university buys, by noon each Tuesday, under a contract. On pay day, 856 casual staff are paid from rows like Linh's, and not one claim was copied to get there.

## The reference, and practice

The module's reference is DAMA-DMBOK as the map (here its *data integration and interoperability* and *data storage and operations*) and the data engineering lifecycle of *Fundamentals of Data Engineering* (Reis and Housley, 2022) as the spine, where serving data for analysis is a stage of its own. For this film, the tool's own description is the reference for what one copy on one platform means today: Databricks' *LTAP architecture* (read 5 November 2026), with Lakebase's docs. HTAP, one database that both writes and reads, is *Both at once*'s (*From words to data*), named in a line and not taught again. Where the film differs: it says "one copy, one platform", never "one engine", because the point is separate engines over one copy, under one governance.

| Body of knowledge | What it adds | Where the film uses it |
|---|---|---|
| DAMA-DMBOK, data integration and interoperability | Integration is work in itself: moving, transforming and reconciling copies between systems, each copy a cost to keep in step | *The usual design*: the pipeline, two platforms, two sets of permissions, a copy always behind |
| *Fundamentals of Data Engineering*, serving | Data is served for analysis as a stage of the lifecycle; where it's served from is an architectural choice | *One copy*: the readers read where the data is written |
| Databricks, *LTAP architecture* (read 5 November 2026) | One logical copy in open storage, one governance model, purpose-built engines (Postgres for transactions, the lakehouse for analysis), no CDC or replication pipelines, a single writer for each table | *One copy*, *Fewer platforms* |
| Databricks, *Register a Lakebase database in Unity Catalog* and *Serve lakehouse data with synced tables* (read 5 November 2026) | How the lakehouse reads Lakebase's rows under Unity Catalog, and how the lakehouse serves a table it writes back to an app | *One copy* (the readers; the contracts served to the app) |
| *Both at once* (From words to data) | One database that writes and reads removes the copy, not the model | *What to choose*, in a line |
| *Moving is easy, agreeing is the work* (this series) | The contract at each interface, which still needs people | *Fewer platforms* and *What to choose*: the hours to pay, under a contract |

**From practice.** The author's point, which the film carries: "That means you can consolidate your data integration services in a single unified platform instead of having multiple isolated platforms." What practitioners would tell a newcomer, as the brief and the module's setup gather it: start by asking, for each table, which system writes it, because one writer is what lets everyone else read without a copy; don't copy data only so reports don't slow an app, when the platform can read it in place with its own engine; and expect one thing to still move, to a system you buy, and agree its contract. What goes wrong: a copy made "just for reporting" becomes a second source of truth, with its own permissions, its own lag and its own arguments about which number is right.

## Pause and think

Three questions, at the end of *The usual design*, *One copy* and *Fewer platforms* (`site/assets/one-copy-one-platform/think.en.js`, with `think.es.js` in the same places). Each has one right answer, one near miss that is wrong for a reason the film gives, and options of like length; the right answers sit in three different places.

1. **What does the usual design's copy cost?** Right: two platforms, a pipeline to run, two sets of permissions, and a copy always a little behind. Near miss: two platforms but one set of permissions (each platform grants its own access).
2. **Why isn't the app slowed when everyone reads its rows?** Right: Postgres serves the app, and the lakehouse reads the same rows in place, on its own engine. Near miss: the lakehouse reads a copy made each night (there is no copy).
3. **Why do the hours to pay still leave the platform?** Right: payroll pays from a payroll system the university buys, which isn't on the platform. Near miss: payroll's team needs its own copy of every claim (one thing moves, not a copy of everything).

## Rigour to check before recording

Checked on 5 November 2026. Product names and what they do are as Databricks' docs give them that day; their release status is written here only, never in the film or on its page.

- **One copy, under one governance, with purpose-built engines.** Databricks, *LTAP architecture* (https://docs.databricks.com/aws/en/oltp/projects/ltap-overview, last updated 30 October 2026): LTAP "serves both transactional (OLTP) and analytical (OLAP) workloads from a unified data storage layer in the lake, under one governance model, so you don't have to keep separate transactional and analytical systems in sync. It removes the change data capture (CDC), replication, and transformation pipelines that teams traditionally maintain to copy operational data into a separate analytical system"; "Purpose-built engines. Postgres serves transactions and the Lakehouse serves analytics, and neither compromises the other"; "Each table has a single writer, either Lakebase or the lakehouse." The film's "Postgres serves the app, and the lakehouse reads the same rows for analysis, in place, without slowing it" and "Each table has one writer" follow it. The film says "one copy, one platform", never "one engine".
- **Release status (sources only, 5 November 2026).** The LTAP page's table: *Register Lakebase in Unity Catalog*, GA; *Serve data with synced tables*, GA (LTAP Direct Writes: Beta); *Lakehouse//RT querying Lakebase*, Private Preview; *Lakebase Change Data Feed*, Public Preview; availability varies by cloud. The film names none of these four, and says no status anywhere.
- **The lakehouse reads Lakebase's rows under Unity Catalog.** Databricks, *Register a Lakebase database in Unity Catalog* (https://docs.databricks.com/aws/en/oltp/projects/register-uc, last updated 7 October 2026): registering "creates a read-only catalog that represents your Postgres database"; the LTAP page: "After you register a Lakebase database, Unity Catalog applies permissions, lineage, and audit to the external compute that reads it." Nuance kept here, not in the film: Unity Catalog governs the lakehouse's reads; the app's own access to its Postgres tables is granted in Postgres (GRANT and REVOKE). The film's "One catalog governs who may read the claims for analysis" says only the first.
- **The lakehouse serves the contracts to the app.** Databricks, *Serve lakehouse data with synced tables* (https://docs.databricks.com/aws/en/oltp/projects/sync-tables, last updated 4 November 2026): Unity Catalog table data served in Lakebase for low-latency reads; the LTAP page: "The lakehouse owns the write ... Use synced tables to serve that data into Lakebase so your application can read it with low latency." The film says "serves them to the app" and doesn't name synced tables.
- **Lakebase.** Databricks, *Lakebase Postgres* (https://docs.databricks.com/aws/en/oltp/projects/, last updated 30 October 2026): "a fully managed Postgres database integrated into the Databricks platform"; *Lakebase architecture* (last updated 27 July 2026): storage separated from compute. The film's "Lakebase, the Postgres database in Databricks".
- **Announcement.** Databricks, *Databricks launches LTAP* (16 June 2026) and the engineering blog *From monolith to Lakebase to LTAP* (30 June 2026), read for background; nothing in the film depends on them.
- **800 to 1,000 kinds of coin.** Stephen Quinn and William Roberds, "The Bank of Amsterdam and the Leap to Central Bank Money", *American Economic Review* 97(2), May 2007, 262–265: an "open economy with 800 to 1,000 different circulating coins". (Wikipedia's article says 500; the film follows the peer-reviewed paper.) "Clipped, shaved at the edge for their silver, or worn thin": Adam Smith's "clipt and worn foreign coin".
- **About 9% less; a thousand owed, about nine hundred paid.** Adam Smith, *The Wealth of Nations* (1776), book IV, chapter III: "Before 1609, the great quantity of clipt and worn foreign coin which the extensive trade of Amsterdam brought from all parts of Europe, reduced the value of its currency about nine per cent. below that of good money fresh from the mint." The thousand guilders is the film's own example of that average.
- **31 January 1609, in the town hall; the merchant's own page; no coin carried.** Wikipedia, *Bank of Amsterdam* (read 5 November 2026): "Established on 31 January 1609"; the bank in the old town hall on Dam Square (Saenredam's painting; the 1652 fire in the town hall). Smith: the bank "gave a credit in its books" for coin deposited, and bank money "can be paid away by a simple transfer, without the trouble of counting, or the risk of transporting it from one place to another". The film's "brings their coin once" is the deposit; merchants could also withdraw and deposit again, which the film leaves out.
- **About 5% more for a guilder in the ledger.** Smith: the agio of the bank "at Amsterdam is generally about five per cent."
- **For over a century, bills from all over Europe are paid there.** Smith: "all bills drawn upon or negotiated at Amsterdam, of the value of 600 guilders and upwards, should be paid in bank money"; Wikipedia: "an effective, efficient and trusted system for national and international payments", the guilder a de facto reserve currency in the 17th and 18th centuries, the agio gone only in 1790.

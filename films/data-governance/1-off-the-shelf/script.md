## The promise

Every line passes two tests: anyone who has seen a policy library gather dust can follow it, and an experienced data governance lead finds nothing wrong in it. The film opens the series and says what it's about. A policy that only lives in a binder is a promise, like Magna Carta's one measure for the whole kingdom, which took six centuries to keep. What made measures the same in the end was all of it together: one definition, a copy where trade happens, and someone who checks, with authority. Governance works the same way. Its job is to decide who decides, and to make sure each decision reaches the work; management does the work. The bodies of knowledge are named in the series' prologue; here the newest DMBOK only stands on Yusuf's desk, and the series takes what they agree on. Governance is people's work: it starts by listening, and tools carry out what people agree. Every line of the binder faces one question, *where does it run?*, and behind every line that stays there's something worth keeping, and someone to keep it: owners decide, stewards keep it up to date, the teams who do the work use it, and governance checks it's kept. **A policy that doesn't run anywhere is a wish.**

## The story in one paragraph

In 1215, Magna Carta promised one measure of wine, ale and corn for the whole kingdom. For six hundred years the promise was repeated; kings sent standards to the towns, and they sat in cupboards. In 1871, a member of Parliament said that in his county alone a bushel meant thirty-two different measures. What worked, by the end of the century, was one definition in law, a copy in every county, and an inspector who checked against it, again and again. Today, Yusuf Demir starts as the university's data governance lead. He inherits a shelf of 34 policies, a maturity score of 2.1 out of 5, a chart of who's responsible for what with half the names "to be confirmed", and a council that meets once a month; his brief is to fix governance before the next submission to the government. The same week, Planning's census report and the draft submission disagree: 4,812 awards, or 4,867. Both are right. On Monday the word *award* changed, so microcredentials of at least 6 credit points now count, and one report ran before the change. The government counts awards its own way, and nobody has decided how the university's meaning maps to it: Mei owns the meaning, Jun builds it, Planning reports it, and the chart says "to be confirmed". Policy 12, "business terms are defined consistently", doesn't say who decides, or how a decision reaches the work. That's what governance is for. Yusuf doesn't write a 35th policy: he listens first, then asks of every line where it runs. Behind each line that stays is something worth keeping (rules with owners, one glossary, classes of personal data, a list of who owns what) and someone to keep it. Over nine films, he follows one symptom at a time to what's worth keeping, and to who decides and acts; three more draw it all as one model on the university's architecture map, and six build it with the tools.

## Rigour to check before recording

Checked on 16 October 2026; the sources are listed below.

- **Magna Carta, clause 35.** The quote is the Magna Carta Project's translation (University of East Anglia), cut before "namely the quarter of London": "There is to be one measure of wine throughout our kingdom, and one measure of ale, and one measure of corn". The British Library's translation reads "There shall be standard measures of wine, ale, and corn (the London quarter), throughout the kingdom". The clause *promised* one measure; it didn't achieve it. It was kept in every reissue (as chapter 25 of the 1225 and 1297 texts) and repealed in 1948. Kings had tried before: Edgar's code, and Richard I's assize of measures of 1196.
- **"Repeated for six hundred years."** The years in the corner: the reissues of 1225 and 1297, the Clerk of the Market Act 1640 ("one Weight one Measure and one Yard... throughout all the Realme") and the Weights and Measures Act 1824. Measures still differed: the Magna Carta Project says local variations persisted long after the thirteenth century.
- **The king's standards.** An Act of 1495 had bronze ("brass") standard measures made and sent to the towns; they were found defective, and remade under an Act of 1496, and sent out about 1497. Elizabeth I's standards of 1588 stayed the primary standards until 1824. Sources disagree on how many towns received them (37, 43, 57 or 60), so the film gives no number. The Henry VII bushel, gallon and quart survive at Winchester; a 1497 bushel at Salisbury. Clerks of the market carried the standards and sealed the measures they checked from the thirteenth century, so "officers checked them now and then" is fair; the film draws a cupboard, not a particular town's.
- **1871.** Sir Charles Adderley, MP for North Staffordshire, in the Commons on 26 July 1871 (Hansard, Weights and Measures (Metric System) Bill): "In his own county, a bushel meant 32 different measures." The same debate cites the 1862 Select Committee: 61 different measures for the sale of wheat. By then the base units were uniform in law; local trade units built on them were not.
- **What worked.** The Weights and Measures Act 1824 set up the imperial standards (in force 1 January 1826). The Act of 1834 had county magistrates buy copies of the standards, keep them at central places, and appoint inspectors, who compared and stamped every weight and measure used in trade and "shall keep a Book" of their comparisons; contracts made with unstamped measures were void. The 1835 Act abolished local and customary measures. The 1878 Act consolidated the law and gave the Board of Trade oversight of standards and inspectors. The film's inspector is in the 1880s, at a grocer's scale, because shop weights and measures were uniform by the late 1800s, while corn kept customary units until the Corn Sales Act 1921: so the film doesn't show the bushels becoming one.

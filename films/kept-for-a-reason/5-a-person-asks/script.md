## The promise

Every line passes two tests: anyone who has ever asked an organisation what it holds about them can follow it, and a privacy officer or a data engineer finds nothing wrong in it. A person's request is answered the way the writ of habeas corpus is: on time, with what's held and the true reasons, and with proof. In 1679, England's Habeas Corpus Act gave a keeper three days to bring a prisoner before a judge and certify the true causes of holding him, a copy of the warrant within six hours, and a fine paid to the prisoner if he stalled. At the university, Linh's request gets a number and a date it's due; the data team's ticket names no one; her numbers sit in one restricted table; a job with its own identity finds her through dbt's lineage, the platform's lineage and the classes on each column, by her numbers and by the codes made from them, without her numbers ever typed into a query; each owner checks their part of her copy, which says why each part is kept and until when; her no goes on the person, and her row leaves the mailing list and every old version of its table; and every step leaves proof in the ticket, the table's history and the audit log. **A request answered on time, with proof.**

## The story in one paragraph

London, June 1676. Francis Jenkes, a linen draper on the City's council, stands up at the Guildhall and asks for a new Parliament, to mend the City's trade. Four days later the King's Council sends him to the Gatehouse prison at Westminster, to be kept until the law sets him free, with no day named. The keeper promises him a copy of the warrant and stalls; his friends' bail is refused; a judge refuses habeas corpus, the order to bring a prisoner before a judge and say why he's held, for one reason only: the courts are on their summer holiday. In August he is still inside, writing of his great loss and the harm to his health. In 1679 Parliament passes the Habeas Corpus Act: three days to bring the prisoner and certify the true causes (ten beyond twenty miles, twenty beyond a hundred), any judge in the holiday, a copy of the warrant within six hours, and a keeper who stalls pays the prisoner £100, then £200 and his post. A prisoner learns why he's held within hours and sees a judge within days, and a century later the United States writes the writ into its Constitution. On 24 November 2026, Linh Nguyen, whose tutoring contract ended four days before, signs in to the privacy office's form and asks what the university holds about her, and to be taken off the tutors' mailing list. The office logs request 2026-117, due on 24 December; Ingrid Holm takes HR's part; the data team's ticket names no one, and her numbers go into one table only the office and its jobs may read. From her master record, P-10482, come her six numbers; dbt lists the models built from her five systems, more than thirty, the platform's lineage adds what was built outside dbt, and the tags on each column point to where a person is named or coded. The job, `privacy-finder`, owned by Jun Park, looks for her numbers and for the codes made from them: in the marts built for the wallet, planning and finance she's only a code. No query's text holds her numbers; the owners of payroll, the claims app, the mailing tool and her signed contracts search their own. Her copy says, for each part, what it is, why it's held, where it came from, who else gets it and until when: her pay records by law until 20 November 2033, her degrees and results for good; other people's data is left out, and on 3 December each owner checks their part. In the night of 25 November the deletion job took her row out of the list's table and its send log, and the mailing tool dropped her; that morning her no went on the person, in place of her 2023 yes, so the list's next rebuild leaves her out; the table's old versions were cleaned up on 2 December. Each step is ticked in the ticket with its proof: the job's run, the table's history, the audit log of who read the keys. The office's table of requests shows each one's due date, a late one as work to do, not as wrong data. On 7 December, seventeen days early, Linh opens her answer by signing in; the ticket closes, and her numbers stay in the table of keys until the last locked copy is gone, on 24 December. Like the keeper's answer to the writ: what's held, the true reasons, on time, and with proof.

**Practice the film carries** (common practice, taken as the author's on assumption, as the module's overnight rule asks):

- One front door for requests, each logged with a number and the day it's due.
- The sign-in the person already has is identity enough; ask for nothing more.
- The ticket names no one. The person's numbers live in one restricted table, never in a ticket, a chat or a query's text.
- Search by every number and every code made from it, through lineage and the classes on each column, and ask each owner outside the platform to search their own.
- The copy is written for the person: what each part is, why it's held, where it came from, who else gets it, until when. Other people's data is left out, and each owner checks their part.
- A no to a mailing list is honoured at once and kept on the person, so no rebuild can undo it.
- Each step is ticked with its proof. Due dates are tracked like work, in a table the office works through, never as a failing test.
- When the request closes, the person's numbers stay in the search table only until the last locked copy that can hold their rows is gone, so a copy restored before then has the request applied again; then they leave.

### 2 · Three days · 1:22–2:41

**Narration.** In 1679, Parliament passes the Habeas Corpus Act. Once the order is served, the keeper has three days to bring the prisoner before a judge, and certify the true causes of holding him. Ten days if the prison is more than twenty miles away, twenty if it's more than a hundred. The courts' holiday is no excuse: any judge must grant the order. A copy of the warrant, within six hours of asking. And a keeper who stalls pays the prisoner a hundred pounds. The second time, two hundred, and he loses his post. So a prisoner learns why he's held within hours, and stands before a judge within days, not a summer. A century later, the United States writes the writ into its Constitution. Today, a person can ask a university too: what do you hold about me, and why? Left in an inbox, a request waits, and nobody can show what was searched. Given a clock, the true reasons and proof, it's answered on time, and the answer can be trusted.

- **Jenkes and his speech.** *The Case of Mr. Francis Jenkes* (London, 1677), the account published by his friends: "Francis Jenkes of the said City, Linen-draper"; at "a Common Hall then assembled at the Guild-Hall" on 24 June 1676 he moved that the Lord Mayor call a Common Council "that might make an address to his Majesty, in the Name of the City, to call A New Parliament"; his speech complained of "the general decay of Trade" and the City's other grievances. He had "the honour to serve this City in Common Council". The film's "asks for a new Parliament, to mend the City's trade" is that, short. The account is his side's; the warrant gives the Council's ("in a very seditious and mutinous manner"), and the film takes no side on the speech: its point is what happened next.
- **The warrant.** Dated "at the Council Chamber in White-Hall this 28th. Day of June, 1676", to "the Keeper of the Gatehouse Westminster": "take into your custody, the Body of the said Francis Jenkes ... and him to Keep safely, until he shall be delivered by due course of Law". Hence "four days later", "until the law sets him free", "It names no day", and the warrant's card, "By the King's Council · Keep him until delivered · by due course of law · 28 June 1676".
- **The copy of the warrant.** "Soon after his commitment, he demanded of the Keeper a Copy of the Warrant ... The Keeper gave him a promise of it, but delaying the performance for two days ... could not yet obtain it"; later the keeper sent one, saying that "before he had positive order to deny him one". The film shows the promise and the stalling, and not the copy that came later, since the Act's six hours answer exactly that.
- **Bail, and the writ refused.** On 29 June his friends "desired to become Bail for him; but Mr. Secretary refused to take any Bail". They moved the Lord Chief Justice, Raynsford, for habeas corpus, "but his Lordship, denied to grant it. Alleading no other reason, but that it was Vacation". The film says "a judge" and doesn't name him; his door carries his office's name. Vacation is the courts' time between their terms, the long one in summer; "the courts are on their summer holiday" says it in plain words. Habeas corpus (*ad subjiciendum*) orders the keeper to produce the prisoner's body before the court with the cause of his detention; "have the body" is the Latin's own words.
- **Still inside in August.** His letter to the Lord Privy Seal, read at the Council on 11 August 1676: "Have been imprisoned since the 28th of June, to my ... great loss charge and prejudice of my health. I have hitherto been denied Bail, Habeas Corpus and the Writ of Mainprize". The film quotes "my great loss, charge and prejudice of my health". He was later bailed, once the King ordered "that the Laws should have their due Course"; the film doesn't follow it.
- **"Without that order, a person could be held for months, with no judge to hear why."** The Act's own preamble: "great Delayes have beene used by Sheriffes Goalers and other Officers ... in makeing Returnes of Writts of Habeas Corpus ... whereby many of the Kings Subjects have beene and hereafter may be long detained in Prison in such Cases where by Law they are baylable to their great charge and vexation." Jenkes's case is widely named as one that moved Parliament towards the Act; the film doesn't claim it alone caused it, and says only what happened, then what the Act did.
- **The Act.** Habeas Corpus Act 1679 (31 Cha. 2 c. 2), "An Act for the better secureing the Liberty of the Subject and for Prevention of Imprisonments beyond the Seas" (legislation.gov.uk, revised text, read 5 November 2026); the roll's title is its first words, in today's spelling. Section I: within three days after the writ's service, bring the body "and shall likewise then certifie the true causes of his Detainer or Imprisonment", unless the prison is more than twenty miles away: "if beyond the distance of Twenty miles and not above One hundred miles then within the space of Ten dayes and if beyond the distance of One hundred miles then within the space of Twenty dayes". Section III: "in the Vacation time and out of Terme", a prisoner may complain to any judge, who is "authorized and required" to grant the writ. Section IV: a keeper who neglects to make the return, or who "within the space of Six houres after demand shall not deliver ... a true Copy of the Warrant", shall "for the first Offence forfeite to the Prisoner or Partie grieved the summe of One hundred pounds and for the second Offence the summe of Two hundred pounds and ... is hereby made incapeable to hold or execute his said Office". Treason and felony plainly expressed in the warrant were exceptions with their own rules; the film's Jenkes was held for a speech, so it doesn't need them.

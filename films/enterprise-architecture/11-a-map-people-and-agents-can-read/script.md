## The promise

Every line passes two tests: anyone who has kept a map, a model or a glossary that went stale can follow it, and an experienced enterprise or data architect finds nothing wrong in it. A map lives while people use it, keep it true and can pass it on. The more it's used, the more it's at risk: kept in slides and in a tool only one person edits, its copies drift, and a stale map is worse than none, because people trust it. So it's kept as text: each element written once, with a stable ID that everything else points to, a name, a definition, an owner, its relationships named as ArchiMate names them, its evidence with a date, and whether its owner has confirmed it; decisions are files too, each saying why. Every change is proposed line by line, reviewed by the owner of that part and versioned, and checks run on the map itself. Every view is generated from it, one way, down to dbt's YAML and Databricks' catalogue, so nobody edits a view. Then an agent can read it: given a request, a change or a column, it finds the decision, the owners or the rule, and cites IDs and evidence so a person can check. The agent is on the map too, with its rights written down: it reads and drafts, and never confirms; people confirm. The opening story carries the idea, and says so: Mau Piailug's star compass lived in his memory, few still held what he knew, and he chose to teach it beyond his own island; fifty years on, Hōkūleʻa is still sailing, navigated by people he taught and people they taught. The film names its test after him, "the navigator's test": could someone else sail by it? **A map stays alive while people use it, keep it true, and can pass it on.**

## The story in one paragraph

In May 1976, the double-hulled canoe Hōkūleʻa leaves Maui for Tahiti, more than four thousand kilometres south, with no compass and no charts. Its navigator, Mau Piailug, from Satawal in Micronesia, finds his way by the stars, the swells and the birds; his star compass is in his memory, the points on the horizon where the stars rise and set. After thirty-one days the crew sights Mataiva, and three days later, in Papeʻete, more than seventeen thousand people are waiting. Few still held that knowledge, and it passed only from navigator to apprentice; Mau chose to teach it beyond his own island, and a young Hawaiian, Nainoa Thompson, learned it from him and taught others. A map lives while people use it, keep it true and teach it to the next navigator: could someone else sail by it? Today, Tomás's last note asks how to keep his map alive. It's used every week now, for the skipper's test, to give rules a home and to answer the minister, and that's when a map goes stale: it lives in slides and in a modelling tool only Tomás edits, its copies drift in other people's decks, and the glossary and the catalogue keep their own words. A stale map is worse than none, because people trust it; if Tomás left tomorrow, nobody could sail by it. So he moves it from slides to text: each element written once, as a short file, with a stable ID, a name, a definition, an owner, its relationships, and his two rules from day one, its evidence with a date and whether its owner has confirmed it; decisions are files too. The files live in one repository, every version kept; a change is proposed line by line, as a pull request, and the owner of each part it touches reviews it, while checks run on the map itself and find, every time, that generating power still has no owner. Everything people read is generated from the files, one way: the ArchiMate views, the glossary, the conceptual model, the ontology, the checks and the heat map, and, where data people work, each dbt model's owner, domain and rule IDs, and each Databricks table's owner, tags and description; a fix goes to the map, and every view follows. Then an agent can read it. Given a request, it applies the skipper's test; given a change, it walks the layers and lists the owners who must agree; given a column in the catalogue, it finds the rule behind it, with its owner and its reason; and every answer cites IDs and evidence, so a person can check it. The agent is on the map itself, an actor with its rights written down: it may read everything and draft changes, it may not confirm one, and where two sources disagree it asks the owner; its owner is Tomás. When the regulator changes the retail code, the agent finds the one rule whose reason is that clause, retail's half of day one's rule, and drafts the change with every check and view it touches; the head of retail confirms it, and a new version goes out. Next spring, Zofia starts as a data engineer: a badge, a page of acronyms and a list of systems, as Tomás had, and this time a map. She asks the agent who owns the readings data and which rules it keeps, gets Grace and two rules, each with its reason and a link, and by the afternoon has drafted her first change, for Grace to confirm. In 2007, on Satawal, Mau made five Hawaiians master navigators, Nainoa among them; he died in 2010, and what he taught lives on: fifty years after that first voyage, Hōkūleʻa is still sailing. Grace confirms Zofia's change, the map passes the navigator's test, and Tomás's last note has its answer; the series' eleven films fill the map, layer by layer, and its meaning goes on to *From words to data*.

## Rigour to check before recording

- **Hōkūleʻa, 1976.** The canoe left Honolua Bay, on Maui, on 1 May 1976, made landfall at Mataiva, in the Tuamotus, on 1 June, after thirty-one days at sea, and reached Papeʻete Harbor on 4 June, where more than 17,000 people gathered to welcome it (the Polynesian Voyaging Society's announcement of the voyage's fiftieth anniversary, on hokulea.com; the Hawaii Tribune-Herald, 4 May 2026). Accounts that give thirty-three or thirty-four days count to Papeʻete. Mau Piailug navigated with no instruments; Kawika Kapahulehua was the captain, and fifteen sailed from Hawaiʻi, among them Ben Finney and David Lewis, so the picture's few silhouettes are a few of them. The voyage tested whether Polynesians could have settled the Pacific on purpose, by navigation, rather than by drifting. Honolua Bay to Papeʻete is about 4,350 km on a great circle, so the voice says "more than four thousand kilometres"; the canoe sailed further. Mau went home after Tahiti, and the film doesn't show the voyage back. Checked on 17 and 18 October 2026.
- **The canoe and the sea.** Hōkūleʻa, launched on 8 March 1975, is a double-hulled voyaging canoe about 19 metres long, with two masts and crab-claw sails. It's drawn from that description and the Society's photographs, as a silhouette at night or at dawn, so no colours are claimed, and nothing is drawn from Pacific art. Wayfinders read birds that fish at sea by day and fly home to land at dusk: on the thirtieth day, the crew saw white fairy terns, the trade-wind swell faltered, and they made landfall on Mataiva the next day (Christina Thompson's *Sea People: The Puzzle of Polynesia*, excerpted in Popular Science, 13 March 2019). The swells, the stars and the birds on screen are drawn, not a record of the voyage's nights.
- **The star compass.** Carolinian navigators hold a star compass in memory: points on the horizon named for where particular stars rise and set, with the pole star in the north and the Southern Cross upright in the south, thirty-two points named by fifteen stars, and unevenly spaced, because the stars are (Ward Goodenough, *Native Astronomy in the Central Carolines*, 1953; Thomas Gladwin, *East Is a Big Bird*, 1970). Mau learned it from his grandfather. The ring in chapter 1 puts its points where fifteen bright stars rise and set at Satawal's latitude, about 7° north, computed from their declinations (`D11_DEC` in `src/plan.js`); their names aren't shown, and accounts differ on which stars the compass uses. The ring in chapter 10 is the Hawaiian star compass, which Nainoa Thompson developed with Mau's help: thirty-two equal houses of 11.25°.
- **Knowledge passed on.** Navigators taught within families and schools of navigation, and were reluctant to share what they knew with outsiders. Mau was chosen as an apprentice by his grandfather when he was about five, and made a master navigator at a pwo ceremony, between about 1947 and 1952 by different accounts. He feared that navigation would die in his own culture, as it had in Hawaiʻi, and from April 1975 he worked with the Polynesian Voyaging Society. Nainoa Thompson learned from him, and from Will Kyselka at the Bishop Museum planetarium, and navigated Hōkūleʻa to Tahiti and back in 1980, with Mau aboard as an observer on the way out (the Wikipedia articles on Mau Piailug and Hōkūleʻa, which cite the Society's records). The six islands lit in the central Carolines (Satawal, Puluwat, Pulap, Pulusuk, Lamotrek and Woleai), their lights and the four passing to an apprentice are illustrative, not a count; the chart is sketched from longitude and latitude, with no coastlines.
- **2007 and 2010.** On Satawal, on 17 March 2007 (18 March in some accounts), Mau initiated sixteen of his students as pwo, master navigators, the first pwo for navigators there in 56 years: five Hawaiians (Nainoa Thompson, Shorty Bertelmann, Chad Paishon, Chad Baybayan and Bruce Blankenfeld) and eleven men of Satawal, among them his son, Sesario Sewralur. It was the first time the title was given to anyone from outside Micronesia. Mau died on Satawal on 12 July 2010, aged 78 (the Society's "Hōkūleʻa Moʻolelo: The Pwo Ceremony", 17 March 2017; the Honolulu Star-Bulletin, 17 and 18 March 2007).
- **Fifty years on.** Hōkūleʻa is on the Moananuiākea Voyage around the Pacific, launched in June 2023. In June 2025, Nainoa Thompson captained it from Hilo to Tahiti, while its sister canoe, Hikianalia, carried navigation training led by Bruce Blankenfeld (Spectrum News Hawaiʻi, 4 June 2025); the canoes reached Aotearoa in November 2025, and the plan of July 2026 sails them to Fiji and back between September and November 2026 (Kauai Now, 30 July 2026). "Navigated by people Mau taught, and by people they taught" sums up how the Society trains its navigators; the film names none of them, and its dawn shot is drawn, not a particular leg. Checked on 18 October 2026.
- **The navigator's test** is the film's own name for its test, as the skipper's test is *Now the questions appear*'s. "A map stays alive while people use it, keep it true, and can pass it on" is the film's own summary, built on the series' insights 9 (every claim traces to evidence, and every decision records why) and 12 (a model is true when the people who own it confirm it).
- **An architecture description.** ISO/IEC/IEEE 42010:2022 describes one as stakeholders and their concerns, viewpoints and the views that follow them, model kinds, and the correspondences between them. "Each one answers the questions of the people who read it" is a view addressing its readers' concerns; the film doesn't name the standard. TOGAF's architecture repository is the idea behind keeping it all in one place.
- **Kept as text.** Keeping a model as text in a repository, changed through pull requests that owners review, with checks on every change, is what architecture-as-code and docs-as-code teams do; Structurizr does the same for the C4 model, generating its diagrams from a text model. The files are drawn as YAML, but the film chooses no tool or format. Decision records follow Michael Nygard's architecture decision records (2011), as in *Today, and where it's going*; DEC-007 is that film's record 7, shortened to fit a small file. The IDs are the film's own scheme, a prefix for each kind and a number, never reused: CAP capability, STP step, DOM domain, OBJ business object, RUL rule, MSR measure, OBL obligation, DEC decision, ACT actor and TEC technology. The counts in each folder, and the twenty-four elements dealt in chapter 3, are illustrative.
- **Named as ArchiMate names them.** The films use ArchiMate 4 (April 2026) and don't say what changed. A rule is a requirement; the step that carries it out realizes it, and it's associated with the business object it governs, so its file lists "realization" and "association", ArchiMate's own names. In version 4 a step is a process in the common domain, whoever does it. The relationships carry no multiplicity: cardinality stays in the logical model (decided on 16 and 17 October 2026).
- **Tomás's two rules** are *Day one*'s, word for word: every note names its source, and a note is a draft until its owner confirms it. Here they become two lines in every file, evidence (a document or a person, with a date) and status.
- **The checks on the map** are the film's own three; any team keeping a model as text can write them as tests that fail a pull request. - **One map, many views.** The views follow the decision of 16 October 2026, from *Meaning machines can read*: the glossary, the conceptual model, an ontology in OWL for reasoning, and the checks on the data are generated from the map, one way, so they agree. Nobody edits a generated file; in practice it says so in a header, and a check fails if it differs from what the map would generate, as this site's own `check_site.py` does for its pages.
- **dbt.** From dbt 1.10, custom properties such as an owner, a domain and rule IDs belong under `meta`, inside `config` (dbt's docs, "Upgrading to v1.10", and its deprecations page); tests are listed under `data_tests` (since 1.8). The test names follow *Rules with a home*: `rul_022_corrected_on_next_bill` becomes `rul_022_corrected_within_ten_working_days` when the rule changes. dbt's own groups also carry an owner; the film keeps the owner the map gives, in `meta`.
- **Databricks.** In Unity Catalog, a table's full name has three parts (catalog, schema, table), and Catalog Explorer shows its owner, its tags, its description (its comment) and its lineage, which Unity Catalog captures down to the column (Databricks' docs, "What is Catalog Explorer?" and its page on data lineage). The page on screen is drawn, not a screenshot. Checked on 18 October 2026.
- **How an agent reads it** isn't shown. Today, agents commonly reach tools and data through the Model Context Protocol (MCP), an open standard that Anthropic gave to the Agentic AI Foundation, under the Linux Foundation, in December 2025. dbt Labs publishes an MCP server that lets an agent read a dbt project's models, lineage and tests, and Databricks' managed MCP servers, for Unity Catalog functions, Vector Search and Genie spaces, are in preview (Public Preview or Beta, depending on the page). An agent that reads a map kept in a repository needs only read access to it and to the catalogue, and its drafts go through the same pull requests as anyone's. Checked on 17 October 2026.
- **The agent on the map.** ArchiMate has no element for an AI agent. *Who does it, and where meaning changes* drew the outage agent as an actor, with its rights written down (the series' insight 11), and said later films would come back to it: this one keeps it that way, so the agent's rights sit on the map beside everyone else's. ArchiMate 4 makes role and collaboration common to every domain, so a modeller could also draw the agent as an application component filling a role. Its rights are invented, and Tomás, who answers for it, is its owner. *Keeping it true*'s rule is "The agent recommends. People approve. Everything is versioned."; this series has said "confirm" since *Day one*, and here the agent drafts.
- **The retail code, the metering rules and their clauses** are fictional, like the regulator. The rule pair is *Rules with a home*'s: RUL-021, the network's (Grace's), and RUL-022, retail's (the head of retail's), split at the wall from *Day one*'s rule; RUL-020 marks every reading actual or estimated. Their definitions and owners are that film's, and so are the IDs; the short names are this film's. The clause numbers (4.2, 7.2 and 7.4), the amendment of January 2028 and its ten working days are this film's own, and plausible: in many markets, a retailer must correct a bill once an actual reading replaces an estimate.
- **Day one, again.** Zofia's acronyms are real ones from the industry, as on Tomás's first day, with MDM (meter data management), for a data engineer, in place of EAM.

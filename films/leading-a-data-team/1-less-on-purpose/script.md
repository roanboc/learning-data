## The promise

Every line passes two tests: someone new to leading a team can follow it, and a data manager, a team lead or an agile coach finds nothing wrong in it. As the series' opening film, it says once that the series follows four ideas for leading a team: minimalism, kaizen, Ubuntu, and decentralisation and emergence. Its own idea is minimalism for a team: simpler is better, and it doesn't just happen. Understand what matters, design for it, remove the noise (reports nobody opens, meetings that decide nothing, alerts nobody acts on), and give the time back to what people use. Cut with the team, openly and so a cut can be undone. Nobody's work is called waste.

**Takeaway:** "Keep what people use; stop the rest, together."

## The story in one paragraph

Britain, 2010: the government publishes millions of pages on the web, across hundreds of websites, most run by one department alone. To renew a passport or pay a tax, people must first work out which part of government does it, and which site to trust; the same advice sits on several sites in different words, so people lose time and can't be sure which page is right; and taxpayers pay around 560 million pounds a year for it all. In October 2010 Martha Lane Fox, the government's digital champion, reports on Directgov, its main public website: one website for the whole of government, one team that answers for what people find there, and no move of the old pages: write only what people need, and archive the rest. The Government Digital Service builds it. It reads every page of Directgov and Business Link, and what people searched for, keeps only what meets a real need, and writes that again; in 2012 its design principles say, first, start with what people need, and second, do less. In October 2012 GOV.UK replaces both sites with far fewer pages, about 3,800 pieces of content; in tests people finish 61 tasks in 100, against 46 on Business Link, and a minute faster; it's expected to cost up to 70 million pounds a year less; and the next spring the Design Museum names it Design of the Year. At the university a year from now, Helena Novak's data team of eight puts everything it runs and attends on one wall on Tuesday 4 May 2027: 38 old reports, 17 not opened in a year; about eight hours of standing meetings a week each; about 60 alerts a week, most needing nothing. It asks who uses each thing, and for what, agrees what a meeting, an alert and a report are for, and designs its week, about three hours of meetings each, from Monday 10 May. It writes to each old report's last readers (one needs last year's figures and gets a copy), and once the data council approves the plan on 25 May, it switches the 17 off on 31 May, not deleted, any of them back on within a day until the old warehouse closes in December. Sam goes through the alerts with Noor and Jun, one kind at a time: by 7 June about 12 a week reach him, each needing someone to act, with a note that says what to do. In June each person has about five hours a week back, about one person's whole week across the team, and one rule keeps it: anything new needs a reader, a reason and an owner.

**Where the film adopts, extends or differs.** It adopts GOV.UK's "do less" and Rams's "less, but better" as the aim: less isn't the point, better is. It extends minimalism from design to a team's own week: the reports it keeps, the meetings it attends and the alerts it answers, each asked the same question, who uses it, and for what. It differs from lean's word in one thing: lean names work that adds no value *muda*, waste, and the film names it once, then says the team never calls anyone's work waste, because every report on the wall once answered someone's real question. It differs from a clear-out in another: nothing is thrown away on the day. The team chooses every cut, tells the people it touches first, switches things off before it deletes them, and keeps a way back.

**Practice, from the module's plan.** A practitioner will recognise the reports nobody has opened in a year that still run every night; the weekly round of updates that reads out what the team's board already shows; the alert that fires every night and needs nothing, until the one that matters is missed; and the fear that stopping anything will upset someone. The advice is the briefs': list what you run and attend; ask who uses each and for what; stop things openly and reversibly (switch off, wait, then delete); measure the time you get back; protect it.

- **Government on the web in 2010.** Martha Lane Fox's letter to Francis Maude, Minister for the Cabinet Office, *Directgov 2010 and beyond: revolution not evolution*, 14 October 2010: "Government publishes millions of pages on the Web, via hundreds of different websites. Most of these sites are still run as silos within departments. - **The cost.** The same letter: the government's rationalisation "should reduce overall Government web expenditure from around £560 million to about £200 million a year". The film's "around 560 million pounds a year for all those websites" is the spend before it.
- **Her advice.** The letter's recommendations: "Make Directgov the government front end for all departments' transactional online services", and "a new central team in Cabinet Office in absolute control of the overall user experience across all digital channels" (the film's "one website for the whole of government, and one team that answers for what people find there"); and "There is no need for a major migration of content from existing departmental websites, they should simply be archived or mothballed when essential content has been commissioned and included in the new site" (the film's "Don't move the old pages across.

# Playbook: making learning films and material

What made the Learning Data films work, starting with *The Inner Life of Data*, written so the next film, lab or course can start from it. [The making-of story](site/journey/index.md) tells how the films came to be; this page keeps what to reuse. Each section ends with the question to ask of your own work.

## 0. Foundation first

Before story or pictures, the concept is settled. [`AGENTS.md`](AGENTS.md) gives the order to work in, for any agent.

- **Ask for the reference, and suggest others.** Name the framework the films treat as the reference, list the others worth knowing, and say where the films differ.
- **Check it against practice.** Concepts and processes come from what practitioners really do; the author's own experience goes into the brief.
- **Show the usual move before the exception.** Practice has an order, and a film follows it. The first cut of *When strategy moves* changed what a column of a data product meant, so its contract got a second version. The author's practice is to add first: a new column breaks nothing that reads the data, and a new version is the last resort, for a change that isn't additive. The film now adds the discount as a new column, keeps the contract at version 1, and names a second version as the exception.
- **Show the practice at the scale of the story.** The first cut of *A map people and agents can read* kept a utility's map only as text files in a repository. Its author pointed out that at that scale the map lives in an enterprise architecture tool, fed by the systems that already keep its records (documents, the project management tool, the IT service management tool), so keeping it true is a data integration job too. Show the way that fits the organisation in the story, show the simplest way as the small case, and say which fits which.
- **Keep the essential.** Name the bodies of knowledge once, then teach the insights they share, as *From words to data* did with modelling techniques and *The map before the data* with EA frameworks.
- **Tools show, they don't lead.** Databricks and dbt demonstrate the ideas; a topic never exists because a product feature does.
- **Show where the practice lives.** Not every idea lives where the demo tools would put it. The first cut of *Which value wins* said dbt turns the master data rules into SQL, but such rules are a specification, usually set on a master data tool's screens by people who don't write code. Say what the thing is (rules written down in one place, where the steps that build the record read them), show the screen people use without naming a product, and show a file and its SQL as the simplest form.

*Ask: would an expert in each framework agree, and would a practitioner recognise it?*

## 1. Promise and audience

- **Promise two audiences at once.** "A teenager understands it, and a data engineer agrees with it." Write the promise down and test every scene against both halves. The first keeps it clear; the second keeps it honest.
- **Follow one thing from start to finish.** One enrolment, from a tap on a phone to a decision. Every concept is something that happens to it, so the viewer always has a thread to hold.
- **Start where the thing is born.** The enrolment is written to the system of record before the platform sees it. Starting in the right place prevents a wrong mental model that later scenes would have to undo.
- **End on a human outcome and a human decision.** A new class opens and 140 more students get a seat. The agent recommends; people approve.
- **Use a fictional world based on real practice.** The university, people and numbers are invented, so the material can be shared anywhere, and any institution can see itself in it.

*Ask: whose story is this, and what changes for a person at the end?*

## 2. A world, not a slideshow

- **Test analogies against the hardest mechanism.** Rivers, libraries and pipes all failed at zero-copy sharing, because water and books can't be in two places at once.
- **Give each layer of the metaphor one job.** Light explains movement; the pictures it carries explain meaning.
- **Map every object to a mechanism,** in a table that lives next to the script. If an object has no mechanism, it's decoration: cut it.
- **Turn the place where the metaphor breaks into the twist.** The projector, which shows the original without a copy, became the film's dramatic moment.
- **Show things failing.** The wrong sketch (312% full, every test passing), the copy that goes stale, the record a test stops. A failure teaches the mechanism better than a success does. A success has edges too, so show who a route that works still misses: in *When strategy moves*, the automatic match finds most eligible households but misses tenants whose account is in a landlord's name, as real automatic schemes do, and the film shows them and their second route.
- **Vary the stories, not only the ideas.** A series whose openings all come from the same place and century starts to feel like one film. Plan the openings across a series together: different cultures and regions, the past, the present and imagined futures, and different kinds of story (an invention, a failure, an everyday system). Label an imagined future as imagined, and check a story from another culture against that culture's own sources.
- **Tell the opening story in four beats: scenario, problem, solution, benefits.** Say each beat outright, in that order, in words a newcomer follows: what was happening, and to whom; what went wrong or what people feared, and why it mattered to them; what was done, and by whom; and what got better, then the same in the film's data story. The author asked for it film after film, until it became the rule (AGENTS.md, *Story and world*). The first script of *Who may see it* said that a 1983 census law let each town hall "use the answers to check up on its residents, by name", and that hundreds of groups called for a boycott. It set the scene but never said the problem: someone who didn't know the story couldn't tell what checking up meant, or why anyone would boycott a census. *Off the shelf* has all four: surgical teams knew the steps that keep patients safe, but in a busy room often skipped them (scenario); patients paid with infections, mistakes and deaths that could have been avoided, and something had to change (problem); three times in every operation, a nurse read a short checklist aloud and could hold the team (solution); deaths after surgery nearly halved, because the list was agreed, in the room, and checked, which is what governance gives data (benefits). Each script lists its beats in an *Opening story* table, quoting the line that says each, and `site-tools/check_site.py` checks that every beat is there, in the narration's own words.
- **Say how the opening story carries the idea, and close it.** An opening from another place or time is there to carry the film's idea, and a viewer won't always see the link alone. Say it in one line at the end of the opening (*Now the questions appear*: the skipper's question mattered because he would act on its answer, and the phones brought it in time), let the main story borrow from it by name ("the skipper's test"), and come back to it before the end, traced with the film's own idea. Its first cut left the link unsaid and the story open, and the author read it as just a decision made from numbers.
- **Make the example show the point, and nothing that reads against it.** The author watched the Kenya opening of *When strategy moves* three times and couldn't see what it showed, and says it has happened with other films' examples too. Its first cut told how M-Pesa began, as a pilot for repaying loans that people used to send money home: true, but it read as if no one had set a strategy, in a film about a change in strategy. Lead with the decision the film is about (a mobile network sets a new strategy, to carry money as well as calls), put it on screen in one sentence, and have the narration say what it shows ("That strategy fits in a sentence. Making it real takes much more."). A true detail that makes the example read as something else goes to the rigour sheet, not the film.
- **Set guardrails early.** At most three zoom levels, only original art, and no styles that belong to other cultures, such as dot painting, which Australian cultural protocols protect.

*Ask: what would an expert say is wrong with this picture?*

## 3. Rigour

- **Keep a rigour sheet.** For each scene, write down the real technology and what the picture simplifies. It answers experts before they ask, and it's the checklist for the next update.
- **Check names and maturity when you publish,** not when you start. Products get renamed, and previews aren't generally available: say "preview" when something is.
- **Keep roles clear.** dbt writes and tests the recipes; Databricks runs, stores and governs. One sentence per role, repeated in picture and word.
- **Give a discipline's rules no product as their home when the discipline keeps them in its own tools.** Master data rules, such as when two records are one person, are a specification: master data tools often let stewards set them in screens, and in the simplest form they're YAML and SQL. *Who is it?*'s first cut matched people "in dbt" and kept the links "In Databricks", with their logos, and the author asked for no tool to be named as the rules' home. Say where they're kept in plain words ("where the data is transformed"), and tag code on screen as the simplest form.
- **Show whose component is whose.** Use official logos, unaltered, and credit the trademarks. License code and content separately (here MIT and CC BY 4.0), and exclude the logos.
- **Show a kind of product, not a product.** When a film shows a kind of product at work, as *A hub at work* shows a master data hub, call it by its kind ("a master data solution") and draw its screens for the film, in no product's style. Its first cuts named one product as the example, once, in words; the author asked for it to stay generic, since the film doesn't need to say which product it is (20 October 2026). Check each thing it does against real products in the rigour sheet, and end with questions to ask of any of them.
- **List your sources with the date you checked them.**
- **Check that a standard's word means what your picture says.** When the picture puts an industry model's word beside the business's, check its definition, not just its name: *Rules with a home* first paired a meter reading's "actual or estimated" with the IEC model's "reading type", which names what's measured; the model marks an estimate with the reading's quality, so the word is "reading quality".
- **Say where the standard puts what the film is about.** A series can follow a standard without teaching its changes, but when a film's subject is one of the standard's elements, people ask where it goes. *Rules with a home* drew every rule with a requirement's parallelogram and never said why, until the author asked where ArchiMate 4 defines those rules. The answer earned a chapter: what the standard calls the thing, what it has no element for, each link drawn as the standard draws it and named, and what it leaves to something else (how a rule is checked). The lab asks it, a scenario evaluates it, and a Pause and think question checks it in the film.
- **Take a list about people from its standard.** Gender or title choices written from memory leave people out: the first draft of *Lists everyone uses* had no non-binary. Copy the choices from the statistics standard the story's place follows (in Australia, the ABS standard of 2020), and name it in the rigour sheet.
- **Test the data; track the business.** A data test checks that the data is right: a dbt test, a data contract's quality rule, a SHACL shape. A rule about what the business does (a bill corrected on the next bill, a request answered in time) can be broken while every record is valid, so it isn't shown as a failing test: what's generated from it is a tracking table, an ordinary model listing the cases where the rule wasn't kept, for its owner's team to work through. *Rules with a home*'s second version showed retail's rule as a failing dbt test; the author, who builds such tables in practice, caught it. Ask of every check on screen which kind of rule it serves.
- **Copy code on screen from the project.** A test or a model shown in a film is the project's own file, with the same keys (a test's `arguments:`, as the project writes them); the rigour sheet says what the picture leaves out.
- **Say who owns what at one level, and keep it.** *Lists everyone uses* gave each role type its own owner, then called HR "the list's owner" two chapters later. Choose the level (the list, or each value) and keep it in every line, label and question.
- **Don't say a tool can't do what it can.** The first cut of *Meaning machines can read* said Genie couldn't read the stacking policy, a PDF; assistants read documents and web pages. The real problem is a policy and an old page that disagree, with no owner and no version, outside a managed glossary. Show that, and check what today's tools can do on the day.
- **Don't give a tool what it doesn't do, either.** The plan for *A hub at work* had an undo tray that held a decision for a minute before it took effect; no source describes one, so the film unlinks in one step and keeps every version, as the sources do. A safeguard the story needs but no product has becomes people's rule: in the same film, the stewards agree that more than 3 wrong in 50 blind checks stops automatic linking.
- **Show the source of truth, and the platform it feeds.** Platforms now carry a glossary's definitions next to the data (in Databricks, on each table's page, where Genie reads them). Show the managed source first, then where it reaches, and say which parts are in preview.
- **Take a film's dates from the project's data, and check them.** *Roles come and go* showed a contract's last day, 27 June, on HR's card, then the same day as the table's `valid_to`, which is exclusive, and it dated some rows before their ends were known. A last day becomes a `valid_to` the day after, and `recorded_at` is when the platform recorded the row's latest change. Where the example project holds the data, the film's `tools/check.py` compares every date the film shows with it before checking the frames.
- **Draw only the columns a table holds.** A table on screen is a claim about the model, and an expert reads every column. The first draft of *For this purpose only* put an email in the student system's results, which the film's own purposes keep apart; the table became the alumni list as the appeals team sees it, with the final grade hidden. Check each column against the model and against what the film teaches.
- **Close the rigour sheet's open items before recording.** A line that says "read it before recording" is a list for the day of the recording. *Who may see it*'s census law had been read only in a search index; before it was recorded, its sections were read again in the government's bill, in the same words, and the sheet says where.

*Ask: which line would I be unable to defend in front of the product team?*

## 4. Visual language

- **Use one design language.** Dark glass, luminous edges, thin line icons and one typeface. Mixing styles reads as mixing ideas.
- **Let colour carry meaning, and never break it.** Each source system has its colour; each business domain has its own; bronze, silver and gold are materials; red means stopped; green means passed.
- **Give a colour one job.** In *One person, many roles* each role has its colour, so the series' four parts, first painted in role colours, read as one role's films each. They now sit on plain paper, and a cross is drawn in red ink rather than in a role's colour.
- **Check what each shape reads as, and who it shows acting.** Viewers name what they see before the voice explains it. A strip of churchyard wall, drawn level to give the church flat ground, read as an oddly placed bridge over the lake; a second person holding a phone in the last scene read as the one typing Linh's address. Shape the ground to carry what stands on it, and give an action to one person.
- **Paint a road with the ground it crosses.** A road painted over the whole landscape at once runs across whatever stands in front of it: in *One person, many roles* the road to Uppsala crossed the farm's spruces, and the walker carrying the certificate walked over them. Paint each stretch with its own ground, clipped below that ground's line, so a brow or a tree in front hides it. Shrink whoever walks it as it goes away, and clip them behind what stands in front.
- **Show each line once.** The closing line faded out while the end card faded the same words in, so for a moment it showed twice. Carry it onto the card instead, to where the card's own copy takes over. Set a tag down before writing its words, too, so a line drawn behind it never strikes through them. The same goes for things: in *Which value wins* a rate book faded out of the harbour while its copy faded in beside Linh's rules, so for a moment there were two; one book is now carried across the cut.
- **Carry a value as a dot, and open it where it lands.** In *Which value wins*, values flying whole from five records into a sheet piled over the rows already filled. Each now travels as a small dot in its source's colour and unrolls into its tag where it lands, so the eye follows one dot at a time.
- **Fade a card's words before the card.** When a card folds away or steps back behind another, fade its words first: words still on a card on its way out read over whatever it passes.
- **Never fade one text into another in the same place.** Two words half drawn over each other read as a smudge: in *Roles come and go*, a date turning over showed both days at once. Let the old one go before the new one writes in.
- **Vary the content, not the style.** Six different, named data products per domain look like a real organisation. Six crops of one picture look like a template.
- **Make things look as they behave.** Live data should visibly change. Raw data should look rough: glitched, duplicated and mistimed. Clean data should look crisp.
- **Draw living things like living things.** Animals, plants, hands and the materials of the past (clay, paper, wood) made of circles, straight strokes and rectangles look rigid next to people drawn with care: viewers of *What's in a word* noticed at once. Give them tapering bezier outlines, overlapping forms, weight, a light side and a shadow side, and a little life from time: breath, wind, a gait. Keep data, systems and diagrams crisp; the contrast says which is which.
- **Hold things the way hands do.** At the agent's counter in *When strategy moves*, banknotes lay across the hands, slid under an open palm and stuck out on both sides of it, and the author saw papers overlapping the hands. Laid along the hands, the notes still looked wrong when the agent let them go over an open palm: the giver's hand came down over the taker's. Lay what a hand holds along it, no wider than the hand: on top of an open palm, or under the fingertips of a hand seen from its back, reaching out beyond them. To pass it, each hand holds one end, with the note between them and a gap between the hands, never one hand over the other. Hand it over while both hands are still, so it never jumps.
- **Pass things over a counter, not through it.** A letter handed across a counter is drawn behind the counter once it is on the far side, or it hangs in front of the counter's face. Seated people's hands hide below a table's edge, too, so in *The same problem, everywhere* every hand-over is staged between people standing.
- **Slide papers in over nothing anyone reads.** In *The same problem, everywhere*, printouts sliding onto the head of risk's desk crossed the board's card on their way, and its words showed through them. Each now comes up from below the desk, from a side with no words on it.
- **Dress people for their time.** In scenes about nature, prehistory and the ancient world, people could be the first humans developing language: draw them as early humans, in hide or woven clothing, bare-armed, with dignity and without caveman clichés. A suit sleeve pointing across a grassland breaks the spell. Modern clothes belong to present-day scenes.
- **Put paper under a white figure.** A person drawn as the white of the paper lets a painted street show through her. Paint a patch of paper under the figure first, so she reads as white wherever she stands.
- **Keep text clear of captions,** and check every scene against the caption area. Keep each caption to two lines, too: past about 140 characters a line wraps to three in the player, and its box rises over the picture (it covered a label on *Off the shelf*'s poster). Split such a line where the voice can pause, with the same words; the data governance series' `build.py` stops on one.
- **Make it read on a phone.** A phone held sideways shows the 1920-pixel frame about 850 pixels wide, so text smaller than about 28 px in the frame is too small to read there. Use fewer, larger words; when a picture is dense (a canvas full of notes, a table), move the camera in on the part the narration is about, and treat the whole picture, seen from afar, as context. Text that is texture rather than something to read (a scrolling list of systems, a canvas's printed headings) is marked as decoration. *The map before the data* was drawn to the rule from the start. The films made before it now meet it through a shared layer, `films/from-words-to-data/shared/src/phone.js`, which raises every piece of text that isn't decoration to 28 px after the camera's zoom; each film's scenes then make room for the larger words. Every player also draws its captions larger as it shrinks (`films/inner-life-of-data/source/src/engine3.js`, `drawCaption`). *The Inner Life of Data* came last, in English and Spanish: Spanish words run longer, so check both languages.
- **Place things by the size they're drawn at, not the size asked for.** Once text grows to 28 px, a card, a row or a label below it measured from the old size runs into it. Measure what's drawn (`phSz` in `phone.js`) and lay out from that; a card's height comes from drawing it once, unseen, and stacks below it start from there. A mark around a letter is fitted the same way, to the ink `measureText` reports, accents included: *Which value wins* ringed Linh's Ễ and ị at a fixed size, and once Manrope drew them, taller than the fallback font, the rings cut through the tilde and the dot.
- **Lay out a table from its widest text.** Columns set at fixed places ran into each other once the text grew to 28 px. Measure each column's widest entry at the size it's drawn, and space the columns from that.
- **On a timeline, zoom the years, not the camera.** When the dates in view change, let the line of years widen or narrow while the camera stays still: labels keep their size, and nothing drifts off the frame.
- **Hang tags above a timeline, not on its rows.** In *Roles come and go*, tags on the rows hid the shorter periods under them, a semester's contract among them. Hang each tag above the timeline on a pencil line from its period, laid out from the tags' widths, so no line crosses a tag or a label.
- **When the room runs out, take turns.** Larger words don't always fit side by side. Show cards one after another (each fades as the next arrives), keep only the line of code the narration is reading and the lines around it, shorten long names on the phone, and let tags wrap into rows by their width. Code is the hardest: show fewer lines, larger, rather than the whole file small.
- **Keep a name whole with a no-break space.** `wrapT` breaks lines at spaces, so a name that must stay on one line (*accounts and bills*) is written with `\u00a0` between its words. Write the escape, not the character: the two look the same, and *When strategy moves*' first helper for it replaced each space with a plain one, so nothing changed.
- **Decide what is texture, on purpose.** A period page behind the story, a picture seen from afar, a card half-hidden behind another and a background list are decoration: they set the scene and aren't read. Everything the narration names must read. Marking too much as decoration is the easy way out, and the report will still pass, so review the stills. A component drawn both up close and small (a bill, a rule's card) takes a `deco` option that reaches all of its text, so its small copies are texture on purpose and its close-up is still checked.
- **Labs and scenarios keep their sizes.** They're read up close and size their text for their real width, so the phone layer loads only in the films. *The Inner Life of Data*'s player also serves the labs, so there `src/phonefit.js` keeps the layer to the canvas the film draws its frame on; a lab canvas as wide as the frame stays as it was.
- **Check for overlaps at every moment, not only at the cues.** Stills at each narration cue missed labels hidden under a frame, a card crossing a head and film cards flying over each other in between. A still every 2 seconds, tiled nine to a sheet, catches them. `tools/legible.py --overlaps` measures the text each frame really draws, after the camera's zoom, every half second, and reports text under 28 px, text running off the frame and words drawn over other words; what's left is usually a moment in passing (a fade, a card flying in) that a still confirms. It checks the frame's sides, not its top: in *Teachers of data*, the whiteboard's headings sat cut off at the top of a close-up until a still showed it, so look at the top edge of the first still of every close-up.
- **Fade what the camera leaves behind.** As the camera moves in on part of a map, the names it leaves slide off the frame before they fade: in *Now the questions appear*, India's ran off the edge as the camera moved in on Kerala's coast. Start their fade as the move starts; `legible.py --overlaps` lists them as text off the frame.
- **Let things happen in an order that could happen.** Timing each motion to its own word can break cause and effect: in *Rules with a home*, the reading crossed the wall before the gate opened, because the gate waited for the words "the agreed list". Open the gate, then cross, and check the order in stills a second apart.
- **Re-read every state you reuse.** A scene carried over from the last film brings its settings with it: Tomás's note in *Rules with a home* kept the half-filled dot that meant "a draft" in *Now the questions appear*, though both rules had been confirmed. Ask what each state means in this film.
- **Choose the poster in a wordless moment.** Mid-sentence, a chapter is still building: the first poster of *Rules with a home* had the gate shut and the agreed list not yet drawn. A breather after the chapter's last line shows everything it built.
- **Take the poster from the opening story.** A poster is the picture on a film's page, on its cards and in its series' mosaic. Taken from the chapters that explain, the nine posters of *The same one, everywhere* each showed the same person beside a diagram, and the author found them alike. Each opening story has a place and a time of its own (a parish in 1686, a court in 1560, a ship at anchor), so posters taken from them look different from each other.
- **Draw the world past the camera's widest frame.** When the camera pulls back, the edges of a set come into view and read as a stage: Zofia's desk, in *A map people and agents can read*, ended inside the frame as the camera pulled back from her, and had to be drawn wider. Check the stills at both ends of every camera move.
- **Keep people wholly in or out of the frame.** When the camera moves in, a person at the edge either stays whole or leaves the shot: half a face reads as a mistake.
- **Draw a reaching arm again where it passes in front.** A person is drawn in one layer, so a hand that reaches over a desk, a screen or a plant goes behind them. In *Teachers of data*, Anil's pointing arm vanished behind the faculty's monitor and the plant on the sill, which the desk's layer holds. Draw the person, then the front layer, then the person again, clipped to the rectangle the arm passes through: the arm shows in front, and the rest of the body stays behind the desk.
- **Order labels as their lines land.** When several labels join one figure, stack them in the order their lines meet it, so no two lines cross. A value about the person, such as a country of birth, meets the person below the role bands, not on one.
- **Underline when rings would cross.** Rings round two lines that sit close together overlap; underline each, one after the other, instead.
- **Let a page give way before another opens over it.** A source opened over a rule's page let the page's words show through the paper: faint enough to pass `legible.py --overlaps`, which counts text above half opacity, and plain on a still. Fade what's underneath first, then open the new page in its place. Even under an opaque page, the check counts the words beneath as drawn over: it can't see paper. In *Teachers of data*, the sheet and the table's headings fade while the guide is open over them.
- **Change a paper's state in place.** When a sheet goes from sent back to signed, draw the signed copy with the same layout, keeping blank lines where the question was, and crossfade the two: nothing on it jumps, and the stamp lands where the old one was, as in *Teachers of data*.
- **Make a warning the same paper in a softer colour.** In *Teachers of data*, a change that warns comes back on the same sign-off sheet as one that's stopped, but signed, with what's missing in amber, not red. The viewer reads it as the same rule at a gentler step, not a new kind of thing.
- **Make each way of keeping a thing look like itself.** The same element shown as a page in a tool and as a text file first looked alike. The tool's page now reads as a form, labels in a column and values in the typeface, and the file as monospaced text.
- **Give a card that names a place a small picture of it.** In *Fixed where it starts*, a trail of four cards with only words on them left the data team's board bare, and asked to be read word by word. Each card now carries a small picture of its place, as the film already drew it (the wallet's card with its badges, the bucket and the mop, the platform's list on a screen, the form on a phone), and the thread runs over the cards from pin to pin, as it would on a real board.
- **Merge things in turn.** Three chips that become one row slide in one by one, each fading only as it arrives, so none is drawn over another on the way.
- **Shrink a dense picture as one picture.** To pull back from a whole composition into a small card, as the last chapter of *Kept, not cleaned* does with the series' line, draw it once into a kept picture and scale that, on a log scale so the shrink feels even; its words become texture on purpose as they pass under 28 px, and every frame stays fast. Under an end card, fade the scene's words out with the veil, so none shows through: the overlap check finds them.
- **Hold still while chapters cross.** Two chapters overlap for 0.8 s as one fades into the next, so anything carried across the cut, and the camera, keeps still for that time, or the crossfade shows it twice. In *Kept, not cleaned*, people also wait to arrive until the camera can frame them whole; and since the camera keeps its frame inside the picture, a thing near an edge is framed off-centre, so lay it out where the framing the camera allows shows it whole. When a chapter opens on the place the last one closed on, start its camera where the last one stopped: the last chapter of *The same problem, everywhere* opens close on the shelf, as the chapter before left it, then pulls back.
- **Give motion weight, and carry things across cuts.** Things that arrive with a small spring and settle, a camera that drifts slowly in every shot, and a thing shared by two chapters that travels across the cut instead of fading (a model, a file, a graph) read as animation, not slides. In the video, motion blur (several moments averaged into each frame) makes movement read as filmed; with too few moments, a fast label shows doubled copies instead of a smear. Skip film grain: noise doesn't compress, and a fine grain made one video more than twice as large (123 MB against 53 MB). Motion itself costs space too: drift and blur doubled that video against its still-framed cut (53 MB against 27 MB), which is still small. *In the weeds of data crafting* keeps these helpers in `films/analytics-engineering/shared/src/weeds.js` and `post.js`.
- **Open a diagram into the picture it stands for.** In chapter 5 of *One person, many systems*, one of the four small diagrams on Noor's wall opens out into the chapter's picture, instead of a cut to a new one: its frame lifts away, its small white figure grows into Linh, and each system's chip flies to its own card. The viewer sees the idea become the place, and each part keeps its meaning on the way.
- **Build components, not drawings.** The same vault, data tile, sketch and plaque drawn by one function appear in the film, the labs and the scenarios. Viewers recognise them, and a fix lands everywhere.
- **Make a request look unlike the answer.** In *For this purpose only*, a request and a door's purposes are both paper slips, so a request held up to a door could read as a second purpose. The request is held up a little askew, with a dashed edge, a shadow under it and the colour of who asks.
- **Give a period object its year, not its title.** A film made in English shows no words in another language, and an old book's title is words in another language: *Fixed where it starts* first printed Semmelweis's German title, his Hungarian journal's name and *Offener Brief* on his letters, as decoration. Draw a title as bars or strokes without letters, keep the year, and let a tag in English say what it is.
- **Mark text as decoration by the size the camera draws it.** A page seen whole is texture; the same page close up is read. *Fixed where it starts* marks its scorecard's words as decoration only while the camera's zoom draws them under about 28 px (`fw_scorecard`), so the wide shot passes for the right reason and every word of the close-up is still checked.
- **Frame what a hand works on above the captions.** A phone's keyboard is at the bottom of the phone, so a close-up that fills the frame puts the keys a thumb presses under the captions. *Fixed where it starts* lays the phone out close and frames it a little wider (`FW_PHV`), so every key the thumb presses, and the Paste bubble, stays above the caption band.
- **Round a tip with an arc, not a smoothed curve.** A smoothing curve through a few close points overshoots: the thumb in *Fixed where it starts* grew two small horns at its tip. Draw the sides, and join them round the tip with a half circle.
- **Let what people carry out of their frame.** A person is painted in a layer of their own, cut to the box round them, so in *Who decides* a change request held up beside Mei's face lost its far edge. Pass `wide: true` to `gv_behind` for anything held out from the body, or draw the thing after the person.
- **Light a person from outside their layer.** A figure's layer is finished like paper, with its grain multiplied over it, so a screen's glow drawn inside it came out as a grey smudge. Draw a glow after the figure, with the "screen" blend.
- **Fade a table a column at a time at the frame's edge.** Moving in on *Who decides*' register, the frame cut its last column through the middle of its words. Fade each word as the frame comes to cut it, and a column with its first word (`wd_fadeCut`), so a page keeps the rest of its words and a column goes together.
- **Fade decoration at the frame's edge too.** A list marked as decoration isn't there to be read, but the frame that cuts it still shows broken words: moving in on the checks in *Fixed where it starts*, the badge list beside them showed "arning" and "s" at the left edge for seconds. Neither check saw it, because both skip decoration, and `legible.py` also skips a word anchored more than 40 px off the frame, however far it runs into the picture. Fade a table's columns as the frame comes to them, decoration or not (`fw_fadeCut`), and look at a still of every camera move past a screen or a page.
- **Strike a note through once it's answered.** When Clara signs Jun's sign-off sheet, the note that sent it back is struck through, not wiped away: paper keeps what happened, and the viewer sees why the sheet came back.
- **Keep a paper's heading in the frame as the camera moves in.** A sheet's kicker ("Sign-off sheet", "Register of owners and stewards") is what tells the viewer which paper this is: move in to the rows the voice names, but stop where the heading still shows.
- **Keep bubbles and cards clear of the frame's edges.** In *Who may see it*'s full-size stills, a speech bubble 10 px from the left edge and a card 15 px from the right looked cut off. Keep them about 40 px in, after the camera's zoom.
- **Let an answered question mark go.** A gold question mark is a doubt: once the answer is written, it fades out completely. Left at a quarter, binder 6's looked like a doubt still open. A note written on paper is struck through instead (above).
- **Draw a screen's words after its desk.** The series' desks paint their monitors with a filled face, so whatever a screen shows must be drawn after the desk: in *An agent that checks*, Sam's two screens came out blank because their words were drawn first.
- **Show a close-up in the state its copies are in.** In *An agent that checks*, copies of the checklist hang with their steps lit green and their don'ts crossed, and the board's close-up first showed the page bare. A close-up is the same paper, nearer: same marks, same notes.
- **Bring a corner card in where it stays.** A card that slides in from past the frame's edge shows its words cut off on the way, and `legible.py --overlaps` flags them. Fade it in where it settles, rising a few pixels, and fade it out the same way.
- **Clear a screen before a title or end card.** Words left on a screen read through the card that comes in over them, faintly but plainly on a still. Fade what's on the screen in the 0.6 s before the card, its bar's title too: `gv_screen` draws the title itself, so pass none and draw it with the rest.
- **Measure a stamp against the words beside it.** A stamp is as wide as its word plus about 56 px, and turned a little, which `legible.py` doesn't measure (it skips turned text). On a narrow card in *An agent that checks*, "APPROVED" covered the end of "its classes and its rule". Lay out the card from both measured widths, with room between them.
- **Turn the head, not only the eyes.** The face's `gaze` moves the pupils too little to read at a distance: a person reading the desk in front of them needs `look` (eyes down) and a small `turn` too. With `flip`, a positive turn faces screen-left, so check the sign on a still.
- **Put a note where it answers, above the captions.** In *Change without surprises*, the note that says the board now meets "for a change that needs it" first sat at the calendar's foot, under the captions. It now goes over "once a month" in the calendar's name, the words it answers.
- **Let a reach meet what it reaches, and cut on it.** Yusuf pinning a form to the board first raised his hand well short of it. Hang the form where the arm can get to it, give the arm a pose made for the reach, and cut as the hand arrives, so the eye goes from the hand to the paper.
- **Let a tag that names one shot go before the chapter crosses.** The corner tag "Thursday" on the last shot of a chapter still showed while it faded into the next, over a different day. Fade it out before the chapter's last 0.8 s.
- **Turn a card into part of a form in turns.** When a gold card becomes the form's new box, fade the card as the box comes, then write the box's words; faded together, the card's words showed through the box's.
- **Leave a day's number showing under what marks it.** A card for the draft submission laid over its day on a calendar hid which day it was. It now sits under the number.

*Ask: if I removed every label, could someone still tell what is what?*

## 5. Narration, sound and pace

- **One idea per line, in short sentences.** Voice each line separately, and time the pictures to the voice, not the voice to the pictures.
- **Measure density, don't guess it.** `films/inner-life-of-data/source/tools/pace.py` reports words per minute, how much of the time the voice speaks and the longest quiet moment in each chapter. It also counts sentences that run into the next without a breath, and long stops inside a chapter. The v4 cut ran at 147 words a minute with the voice speaking 86% of the time: accurate, and relentless. The first breathing cut fixed the averages with 32 long stops, and felt stop-start. Averages hide rhythm.
- **Leave room to think, and spread it out.** Aim for about 120 to 135 words a minute, and at most 140 in any chapter, with the voice speaking about 75% of the time and at most 82% in any chapter. Leave about 0.8 s after every sentence, and 0.5 to 1 s more after each new named idea; a language that takes longer to say the same thing may need a longer beat in its densest chapters. Keep stops inside a chapter under about 2 s. The picture keeps moving during every pause. *The Inner Life of Data*'s [breathing cut](films/inner-life-of-data/breathing-cut.md) and [its review](films/inner-life-of-data/pacing-review.md) tell how these numbers were found; its fourth cut uses them.
- **Keep the camera calm.** Camera moves glide (a sine ease) and take at least about 1.4 s. Whip pans under a talking voice feel rushed; a frame that freezes in a pause feels stopped.
- **Move in only where the camera can centre.** A camera kept inside the frame can't bring a thing near the edge to the middle of the shot: moved in to twice the size, it can't centre anything within a quarter of the frame from an edge, so that thing lands off centre, or partly out of view. At the end of chapter 11 of *One person, many systems*, that left a card half out of view, so the tools now step back instead. Lay out what the narration names away from the edges, and check each move in a still.
- **Let the film flow; pause only where it matters.** *A Sharper Sketch* gives every sentence a beat of about 0.8 s, holds longer only after the ideas that need to land, and keeps three short wordless endings in the whole film: viewers found a breather in every chapter too many stops.
- **Give each film its own music.** Match the mood to the subject: the warm pads of *The Inner Life of Data* suit a journey through a platform; a film about modelling and design gets a slower, warmer ambience.
- **Vary the sound itself, not only the mood.** Viewers who watched more than one film found the sound repetitive after the first. Every film so far builds its music and effects from the same few synthesised sounds (pads, drones, and the same chimes, whooshes, ticks and bells in each `tools/audio.py`), so a new key or tempo still sounds like the last film. For the next film, change the palette: other instruments and timbres, another rhythm, a new set of effects, or a recorded or licensed score. Check by listening to two films back to back.
- **Nothing loops without a reason, and every sound is something on screen.** A viewer noticed sounds that kept repeating in the background and didn't match what appeared: clocks, pulses, repeated figures and random notes drift apart from the pictures and wear on the ear. Keep the music to sustained chords that change with the chapters, and fire each effect at the same moment, with the same word and offset, as the thing it belongs to in the scene. *Declare it, then build it* works this way. A repeated figure can carry meaning when each note belongs to something: in *When strategy moves*, a soft felt-piano note falls for each layer the change reaches, so the ear follows it down the map.
- **Hold music under a story, not under the explaining.** *Who is it?*'s first cut held a consort of viols under every chapter. The author liked it under the opening story and found it tense under the explanations, and asked for sounds and music from time to time instead. Hold chords where a story is told, and at the end; where the film explains, let the voice and the effects carry it, with music now and then: a chapter's chord spread on a harp as it starts, a role's note, a needle's steps. The series' `films/master-and-reference-data/shared/tools/quiet.py` fails when a chapter after the opening has music for more than half its length, or for 12 seconds without a break.
- **Keep voices on one note in tune with each other.** Two voices a little apart in pitch on one note beat: detuned copies for a chorus, a lute's double strings, viols that each drift on their own, a vibrato against a steady note. In equal temperament the partials of a major third beat too, 4 to 9 times a second. A phone's speaker drops the low notes and keeps those partials, so a chord that sounds warm on headphones trembles on a phone. *Who is it?* plays its lute with one string to a note and its dulcian and clarinet without vibrato, moves all its viols' pitch together, tunes the chord it holds alone at the end pure, puts the person's D a pure fifth under her A, and never sounds two notes a semitone apart together. In a phone's range, that took about half the trembling out of its end card, and `quiet.py` now finds no held notes that beat.
- **Set each new instrument against the one it takes over from.** At the same gain, a new instrument can come out much louder or quieter: the reed organ of *One person, many systems* came out 9.6 dB under the blooming chords of *One person, many roles*. Play each new instrument alone, measure its loudness against the one it takes over from (ffmpeg's `ebur128`), and trim it to match before the first mix. Then set the whole by the voice, as below.
- **Let rhythm follow the feeling.** Chords alone made every scene feel the same: an error landed as calmly as a success. Where the story turns to an error or a rush, a low, muted pulse rises under it and fades out when the problem is caught (`urgent()` in `films/from-words-to-data/shared/tools/music.py`): a short bass note on every beat, leaning on the semitone for an error or driving on the fifth for a rush, a soft drum on one and three and a muffled key between, all below about 1 kHz. Match the tempo to the scene: about 92 to 100 beats a minute for something going wrong, slower and uneasy for a slow drift, quicker for a deadline or a busy kitchen. Set it about 7 to 10 dB under the rest of the music, measured on its own, so its hits come through without taking over (a slow drift can sit lower): the first pulses sat 11 to 18 dB under, too faint to notice. Give the bass note a few harmonics, because a phone's speaker can't play a 55 Hz root; on a phone, the muffled off-beat key carries most of the rhythm. Keep it to those scenes; the calm chapters have none, so the contrast tells the viewer something has changed.
- **Keep effects low and soft.** The same viewer found high tones disruptive: chimes, pings, shimmers and hi-hats carry energy far above the voice. Knocks, muffled keys, soft paper and low felt notes, filtered below about 2.5 kHz, mark a moment without cutting through the narration. To soften a film already made, replace its bright effects by name with soft stand-ins that take the same arguments (`soft_palette()` in `music.py`: a chime or a ping becomes a low felt note, a tick a muffled key, a pop a wooden knock, a sweep a breath of air), so every cue keeps its time. If the instruments themselves still ring above the voice (a kalimba, glass pads, FM bells), low-pass the whole score last, music and effects but not the voice (`soften()`, at 2.6 kHz). When only one instrument rings high, take the top off that one alone: *Now the questions appear* plays its celesta low-passed at 2.6 kHz, like the nylon string, and needs no `soften()`. Compare the energy above 4 kHz, where the voice is silent, before and after, against a film that already sounds right: in a mix normalised to -16 LUFS, *The map before the data* measures about 0.0001 to 0.0003 of full scale (RMS), and the reworked films 0.0002 to 0.0005. Measured over the whole mix, the voice's own sibilants swamp it (*When strategy moves*: 0.022 with the voice, 0.00014 in its 81 s of silence), so find the silences from the timeline first.
- **Bring music in from time to time.** The author liked the music under the opening story of *Who is it?*, then found it annoying: "the sound is tense and vibrates", and "I'm keen to just bring some sounds and music from time to time instead of constant sounds". So *The same one, everywhere* plays music under each film's opening, where its story from real life is told, and afterwards only at a chapter's turn, under a moment on screen, or in a pause without words. In *Which value wins* the chords then sound about a quarter of the time, swelling in the pause between two chapters and let go as the new one's voice begins. Nothing there trembles or pulses: no `urgent()` pulse (the lesson above doesn't apply to that series), no vibrato, and no two held notes a semitone apart. Listen to the chord changes too: a note let go over two seconds while its neighbour a semitone away swells in rubs, beating 9 to 18 times a second, which a phone's speaker turns into a tremble. Let a voice that moves by a semitone go as the next note comes in, as one player would. When the chords are tuned pure, do the same where the next chord tunes a note the other way (in E flat, F is 9/8 over E flat beside B flat, but 10/9 beside C), or the two notes' overtones beat. And voice a pure minor chord open: F minor as F, C, E flat, A flat and C, close together low down, repeats as a whole about nine times a second, a flutter a phone's speaker brings out (its loudness moved by 9%); as F, F, A flat, C and E flat it moves by 1%.
- **One voice to a note.** Two or more voices slightly detuned on one held note beat at less than once a second, and a phone's single speaker turns that into a slow pulse. A reverb doesn't hide it. *Roles come and go* gives each held note of its choir one voice, with a little drift and vibrato for life, and its held notes swing about 1 dB, where three voices to a note swung 8 to 17.
- **Say it and show it at the same time.** Narration and picture together beat narration plus a wall of on-screen text. Keep on-screen words to labels.
- **Name things before explaining them.** A new term lands better if the picture shows it a moment before the narration explains what it does.
- **Say which one.** "It will take longer than the code" left the author asking: what code? A newcomer can't point at "the code", "the system" or "the data" unless the line or the picture has named it. Say which: "agreeing it will take longer than changing the systems".
- **Make intentional pauses look intentional.** A silence needs light and motion, or it reads as a glitch.
- **Let sound support meaning.** Music sits under the voice and lifts about 4 dB in pauses, slowly, so it doesn't pump; never cut the sound to silence, which reads as a fault. About sixty effects land on story cues. Mix to about -16 LUFS for the web.
- **Check a new palette's levels against a film that sounds right.** New instruments come out at their own loudness, and the final loudness pass can't fix the balance between them. With the voice as the yardstick, the music of *The map before the data* sits about 21 dB under the voice while it speaks, and about 18.5 dB under in the pauses. The first mix of *One person, many roles*, with its new watercolour palette, sat 26 dB under, too faint, while its one pulse sat only 3 dB under the music, too loud. Measure the music and the effects on their own, scaled as `tools/audio.py` mixes them, before rendering. The effects need it too: those of *Lists everyone uses* first sat 18 dB under its music, against about 12 dB in *One person, many roles*, and went unheard. Music that comes and goes needs the same check: the turns of *Rules with a home*'s third version, its chords no longer a bed, first sat 24 to 28 dB under the voice, and sit about 19 to 21 dB under once raised.
- **Tune held chords pure, or they tremble on a phone.** After the author found a constant bed of strings tense and trembling, taking out its vibrato and its doubled players a little out of tune wasn't enough: in equal temperament a held major third still beats four to eight times a second, and a phone's speaker, which drops the low notes, brings the beating forward. *Lists everyone uses* tunes every note pure over its home key, D, lets each chord pick between a note's two just tunings by which beats least (E is 9/8 over D a fifth above A, but 10/9 a fifth under B), and leaves the seventh partial out of its strings, since it beats against any minor seventh. To check, high-pass the music at 300 Hz and measure how much its loudness moves between 3 and 12 times a second: the old bed moved by 7 to 30%, the same chords played steadily in equal temperament still by up to 27%, and tuned pure, by under 3%.
- **In a series, read the other films' palettes before choosing one.** Films made side by side, each in its own thread, can land on the same instruments: the first mix of *Which value wins* used a reed organ, as *One person, many systems* and *A hub at work* had, and gave its sources and its person notes of their own. Read the palettes in the series' *Decided* table, and in the scripts of films still on their branches, and keep what the series carries from film to film (in *The same one, everywhere*: the mark on the clarinet, each role's note, and the person's D and A). It happened again: its second mix took a humming choir and a felt piano, which *Roles come and go* chose the same day on its own branch, before either film reached the table. So search every branch's `score.py` for an instrument before you choose it (`git grep -i harpsichord <branch>`); *Which value wins* now plays sackbuts and a harpsichord, which no other film uses.
- **Keep held chords steady on a phone.** A phone's one speaker turns slow beats into a pulse: two voices a hair apart on one note beat, and so do the overtones two notes share when they are a little apart and about as strong. The first cut of *A hub at work* held its chords on a reed organ with two reeds to a note, and they swung by up to 4.3 dB. Its synthesiser's second version tuned each chord's fifths pure, which only moved the beats: a note two chords shared was then a little different in each, and the two beat as one chord gave way to the next. Use one tuning throughout, equal temperament like the other instruments, and give a held voice few overtones, falling fast (here, odd harmonics only), so those two notes share are too unequal to beat; its chords now swing by 0.5 dB at most. Measure a held chord on its own, summed to one channel and smoothed over half a second, and run the series' `quiet.py`, which finds the beats.
- **Tune from one just scale to keep chords pure and shared notes equal.** Where pure chords suit a film better than equal temperament, take every pitch from one just scale, not from each chord's own root: tuned chord by chord, the first mix of *Rules that run* beat as *A hub at work* had (`quiet.py` found two). Its `fq()` tunes each note once, pure on D, so a note two chords share is the same pitch in both, and a chord built on the scale's pure intervals stays pure. A few of the scale's fifths are a comma off (on D: C to G and E to B, narrow; G-sharp to E-flat, wide): voice the chords around them.
- **Check the series' other palettes right before you mix.** A series' films are made in parallel: *Roles come and go* and *Kept, not cleaned* both chose a humming choir and a felt piano on the same night, and three films already had a reed organ. Read the instruments each sibling film's `tools/score.py` names, on its branch, and choose what none of them has; *Kept, not cleaned* moved to a flute choir, a celesta and tubular bells.
- **Clip what shapes noise.** Water burbling, made as noise shaped by the exponential of a slow noise, spiked 10 dB over the voice. Capping the shaping at three times its mean kept the burble and lost the spikes.
- **Find what beats, and why.** Two held notes a hertz or less apart swell and fade together, a slow wobble that a phone's one speaker makes plain. *Kept, not cleaned* had three causes. Its flutes' phase was summed in single precision: `music.tt()` gives float32 times, and `np.cumsum` adds in the type it gets, so a long note's pitch slipped by about a hertz at a time (sum with `dtype=np.float64`). A flute's twelfth beat with the fifth above it, which equal temperament tunes a little flat. And the cello's D and A were tuned equal, not pure, as a cellist tunes them. The sound check of *The same one, everywhere* (`films/master-and-reference-data/shared/tools/quiet.py`) lists each pair that beats, and when.
- **Change key on the notes both keys share.** Tuned pure, each from its own tonic, two keys disagree on the notes they have in common, so a note held across the change jumps. *Teachers of data* goes from Bogotá in G major to the university in E-flat major, and tunes E-flat from G (G × 4/5): G, C and D are then the same pitch in both keys, and the bassoon's G from the street is held, to become the third of E-flat under the university's first shot.
- **Make applause a crowd, not a beat.** Claps that land together read as a rhythm, and a rhythm under a story pulls the ear. In *Teachers of data*, each person at the crossing claps at their own pace, from their own start, with gaps drawn at random between 0.3 and 0.48 s, so the clatter has no pulse.
- **Start a sound with its shot.** A sound that begins before its picture belongs to nothing on screen. The cars of 1996 in *Teachers of data* roll up from far off, and their engines first started under the shot before; each now comes in at the cut (`car_in(..., cut=)` in its `tools/score.py`) and carries on from there.
- **Check a synthetic voice by ear and by numbers.** A naturalness model (such as UTMOS) and a speech recognizer catch what a pitch reading misses. Pitch-shifting a synthetic voice made it robotic; blending two voice styles made the Spanish voice more natural and closer to the English one.
- **Be open about the voice.** A synthetic voice is fine; say so. Captions come from the same script as the narration, so they always match.
- **When the picture times something itself, let the score replay it.** The leak in *Fixed where it starts* drips from a painter that times each drop from its number: a period, a phase for each joint, and a hash that skips some drops. Its score runs the same formula, drop by drop, so each drip sounds as its drop lands, and none sounds while a dissolve or a bucket fading in hides it. Change one, change both.
- **Tune held chords pure, and give each pitch one voice.** A glass harmonica holds its notes for seconds, long enough for an equal-tempered third to beat. *Fixed where it starts* tunes every note from its key's tonic in whole-number ratios, leaves out the chord that falls on the wolf fifth, and holds the notes two chords share rather than striking them again. Two held voices on one pitch, a handbell on the glass's note or a glass note struck again, add up with a fixed phase and can cancel into a dip, so a bell's note stays out of the chord under it. `tools/quiet.py` finds held notes that beat; it can't hear a unison that cancels.
- **Damp a ringing note at the cut.** A vibraphone's note rings for seconds: in *Who decides*, one struck at the end of a chapter rang on into the next one's call, and the music ran 13 s without a break, which `quiet.py` failed. Damp a long-ringing note when its chapter ends, as a player lifts the pedal (`until` in its `score.py`).
- **Hear each name as its people say it, before recording.** The voice reads a name by English spelling: it said Noor as "nor" in earlier films, and *andon* as "AN-dun". Print the sounds it will make (`Tokenizer().phonemize(text, 'en-us')` from `kokoro_onnx.tokenizer`) for every name and borrowed word, and respell them in `film.json`'s `say` until they're right: "Nuur", "ahndohn".
- **Hear what an acronym becomes, too.** The shared voice tool spells AI as "A I", and the voice reads that as "uh eye" (ɐ aɪ), while "AI" whole comes out as "ay-eye" (eɪˈaɪ). *An agent that checks* maps it back in `film.json`'s `say` (`\\bA I\\b` to `AI`); print the sounds of every acronym, as of every name.
- **Measure the pace again once the voice is recorded.** The film's own estimate of a line's length (2.7 words a second) is slower than the voice: *Who decides* was planned at 123 words a minute, and its voice, at about 190 while speaking, brought three chapters to 138 to 140. Lengthen the holds where an idea lands, not every gap, and only then score and mix. Then check every camera move timed in seconds rather than to a word: in *Teachers of data*, the voice brought the stewards' first name card in while the camera was still moving in, so it read small, and the move now starts so it arrives before the first name.

*Ask: where does the viewer get a moment to catch up?*

## 6. Media as code

- **Make every frame a function of time.** Then any moment can be rendered, reviewed and compared, like software.
- **Grow a painted thing with a transform, not with its size.** The watercolour of *The same one, everywhere* paints each card and figure once and keeps it, keyed by its size. A size that changes every frame paints and keeps a new one every frame, and a pencil outline whose wobble follows its size shimmers as it grows. Paint it once, at its final size, and scale the canvas instead.
- **Paint only what the frame can see.** A thing drawn in perspective grows without limit as it nears the camera. In *Change without surprises*, the cars driving out under the frame were painted, and their canvases kept, at every size they passed, up to 12,000 px wide, and `tools/check.py`, which draws every frame in one go, ran out of memory and hung without an error. Skip what the frame can't see, paint a thing far bigger than the frame only where it shows, and keep its canvases at a few sizes, each about 1.4 times the last (`cw_paint`).
- **Time the film to the narration.** Change a line and the whole film re-times itself: a new voice, a human narrator or another language needs no manual editing.
- **Time each moment to a whole word, and let the build check it.** A moment that waits for a word finds the first place its letters appear in what the voice reads, so "again" lands on "against", and a word that isn't in the line falls back to the line's start. Either way the picture and its sound come early, and nothing says so. The data governance series' `build.py` stops on both (`films/data-governance/shared/tools/cues.py`).
- **Review by looking and measuring.** Render stills at the moments the narration names something, check them, fix, and repeat. Say plainly what you can't check yourself, such as how the audio sounds.
- **Use cheap checkpoints before expensive rebuilds:** style frames, a five-second voice test, a forty-second sound sketch.
- **Render in resumable chunks,** and run one heavy job at a time.
- **Time a frame from every chapter before the full render.** A canvas filter set on the context (`ctx.filter`) applies to every stroke drawn after it: *When strategy moves* greyed its map that way, and its last 8 seconds took 41 s a frame against about 1 s elsewhere, which held the render's last chunk for hours. Draw such a part once into a copy, and filter the copy.
- **Sum a long note's phase in double precision.** From words to data's `flute()` sums its pitch over `music.tt()`'s times, which are single precision, and on a long note the pitch slips by about a hertz at a time, so it beats against the same note on the nylon string. *When strategy moves* plays its own copy, summed as float64, and gives its felt piano partials in whole-number ratios on one string, so one note's octave doesn't beat with the next. Its `tools/quiet.py`, the master data films' check, finds what an ear on headphones misses.
- **Change the sound without rendering again.** The soundtrack isn't part of `render.html`, so after a new mix `tools/render.py` reuses every finished chunk and only lays the new sound in: the new chords of *A hub at work* took 22 seconds, not a quarter of an hour.
- **Use one source for every output.** The same code makes the MP4, the web player, the labs, the scenario pictures and the posters.
- **Keep the live player light.** The site's player paints each place once, in the background, while the film plays. A picture that keeps the browser busy for seconds holds up the film, its soundtrack and the whole page. The trap is a blur set before many strokes: the browser blurs every stroke. One room of *Off the shelf* took seven seconds that way in a browser that draws without a graphics card, as the site's checks do, and its "Where next?" check failed. Paint such a picture sharp on a layer and blur the layer once. The data governance series' `check.py` times every place. Many places add up too: painted back to back, the prologue's 38 held the page for over ten seconds in the same browser, and its "Watch again" landed late. The series now paints each one when the page is idle, or after a second (`shared/src/finish.js`).
- **A fix in shared code is a new version of every film.** A series' films bundle its shared source, so the wash fixed for one film of *The same one, everywhere* changed every film's frames. Rebuild each film's player, render it again and share a new review copy, or the copy under review isn't the film that ships.
- **Draw it live for learning, and render a file for sharing.** The live player is small, sharp at any size and interactive (chapters, questions, labs). The video file is what platforms accept, plays offline and looks the same everywhere. Keep captions off its picture and ship them as `.srt` files beside it: one video then serves every language, and a caption fix needs no new render. A full render is also the strictest test: it draws every frame, not just the ones someone watched.
- **Release from a clean machine.** A workflow renders every language from the committed source, and refuses if the site's player or the voice timings don't match it. What people download is then what the site plays, and anyone can make it again.
- **Design for other languages from day one.** Keep words in language packs, let boxes size to the translated text, and check the longest language.
- **Start a language with captions.** A full version needs a new voice, new on-screen text and a new video. Captions alone keep the same film and the same video, and take one file: every film after *The Inner Life of Data* has Spanish captions this way. Keep each caption about as long as the English line, so it reads in the time the voice takes. Since 16 October 2026, captions and the site's pages are the only Spanish: no film's visuals or voice are translated, because that doubled the work for every change.
- **Start a page's app once its whole script has run.** The series' scenarios started drawing before the same file had defined their pictures, so the first situation on every visit had none; the others were drawn after a click, so it went unseen until a review of a new one. Start the app after the script ends (a resolved promise is enough), and have the browser check look for each situation's picture.
- **Keep the score's names for the score.** In every `score.py`, `SR` is the sample rate. Naming a new chapter `SR` (for *sources*) broke the whole mix with a type error far from the change. Give chapters other names. A time named like one of the score's own sounds does the same: *Who may see it* kept a moment as `tie`, the name of its string's sound, and the mix stopped at the next tie with "'float' object is not callable". Name a time for its moment (`knot`).
- **A score shares its names.** `tools/audio.py` runs `tools/score.py` with the public names of `music.py` (none starting with `_`), so a helper with a leading underscore isn't there, and a function the score defines with a shared name (`brush`, `celesta`, `tbell`) replaces the shared one for the whole score. Give a film's own instruments names of their own.
- **Keep an offset's remainder positive.** JavaScript's `%` keeps the sign, so an offset worked out from a seed or a position that can go below zero, such as a wash that starts left of the frame, moves a texture into the frame instead of past its edge, and leaves a strip without it. Use `((v % n) + n) % n`. The landscapes of *One person, many roles* had a pale strip down their left edge until work on *Which value wins* showed it.
- **Give a font added for a few letters the weights the page already gives that family.** A face added from code with other weights, such as `500 800` beside Google Fonts' one face per weight, takes the family over, and every other letter falls to the next font in the list. *Who is it?* added Vietnamese letters this way: on the site its page lost Manrope, and on a 320 px phone its Spanish header ran wider than the screen. Copy the weights of the family's faces on the page, and add nothing where they already carry the letters.
- **Clamp every alpha.** Canvas ignores a `globalAlpha` it can't use, such as `NaN` from a division by zero, and keeps the one before, so a thing meant to be hidden shows at full strength. Name an override's keys apart from the data's own fields, too, so one is never read as the other.
- **Fade a painted thing with one mask.** A wash faded slice by slice shows a seam at every slice. Paint it whole into a buffer, then fade it with one gradient.
- **Draw a tag after anything that may cover it.** A tag drawn before a panel that later slides over it vanishes without an error, so tags and labels go in the last pass.

*Ask: if a product is renamed tomorrow, how long does the fix take?*

## 7. From film to learning

A film makes people feel they understand; learning comes from using the ideas. The site follows three steps, one page each:

1. **Watch:** the film, with chapters.
2. **Take it apart:** one hands-on lab per chapter.
3. **Make the call:** scenarios that ask people to decide.

- **Give each page one job.** Keeping practice apart from the labs makes people recall rather than look up, and the change of page is a natural pause.
- **Give each lab one mechanism, and let people break it.** Take Zerobus offline, plant a missing ID, rename a mart column, change the original record. The consequence teaches; the explanation confirms it.
- **Keep the page to what people use.** Notes on how to read a film's colours and conventions went unread next to the film itself: the film has to explain itself.
- **Say what just happened, in words.** Every lab has a line under the picture that describes the result, which also serves people using screen readers.
- **Say the end once.** When a lab ends, its end box says what it showed, and the line under the picture stays for screen readers only. Shown twice, the same sentence reads as a mistake.
- **Reuse the film's pictures in the labs,** so recognition carries over from watching to doing.
- **Write scenarios as situations, not definitions.** "Every test passed, yet the class is 312% full. Why?" teaches more than "What is a conceptual model?"
- **Make every wrong option plausible, and explain it.** A wrong answer is the best moment to teach, so each option says why it's wrong.
- **Make a pass mean something.** A fix that turns a check green must be one its owner would accept. In *The navigator's test*, "owner: to find" passed the map's checks and taught the opposite of the film; a named owner, with the meeting that confirmed it, doesn't.
- **Vary the format:** choose, sort, order and spot the problem. Each asks for a different kind of thinking.
- **Link everything back.** Each lab plays its chapter of the film, and each scenario points to its lab. Trying the scenarios before the labs is fine: guessing first makes the answer stick.
- **Remember progress for the person, not about them.** Progress stays in the browser; nothing is sent anywhere.
- **Make every picture operable without a mouse.** Every canvas interaction also has a button.

*Ask: what will someone be able to decide after this that they couldn't before?*

## 8. From one film to a series

What *A Sharper Sketch* and *When things go wrong* added once there was more than one film.

- **Turn feedback into the next film.** *A Sharper Sketch* says openly that the earlier sketch was an oversimplification, and sharpens it. Correcting in the open teaches the very habit the film is about.
- **One film, one mechanism.** Park what doesn't fit (the physical build went to a later film for engineers), and keep a list of candidates for the series.
- **Choose a reference deliberately.** When there is more than one standard, name the one you use and why, then adopt, extend and record the differences.
- **Put people on every side of the problem.** Technical and business, producing and using the data: one person in each corner, the same guide in every film, and outline colours that say the side.
- **Test characters in code before the script,** with a character sheet of poses and expressions, and write the script within what it shows works.
- **Blameless and believable.** No villains, and small, plausible numbers: the dangerous wrong number is the one nobody questions.
- **Show the alternatives side by side,** so the one thing that differs between them is the lesson.
- **Draw what the audience already knows.** A dashboard in a familiar style beats an abstract picture that needs explaining.
- **Use the domain's own words, and cases people have lived.** A university has applicants, students, staff, researchers and alumni; it doesn't call them customers. A student sharing one family email with her brother was real in the example data but hard to picture, and a contract posted to an old flat read as out of date: offices don't post contracts any more. A graduate who applies again online and gets a second student number is something universities publish notices about, so check a case against what institutions actually publish, and never blame what is normal (separate staff and student logins). When a story needs an unusual case, say in one line how it comes about.
- **Let people work the way they do.** The plan for *A hub at work* ranked a steward's inbox by how many systems each case touched. Stewards start with the case someone is waiting on, and the products route cases rather than rank them, so the film starts there. Check how the people in a story order their day, not only what they decide.
- **Preview a series in a few parts.** Eight films named one by one took too long. Group them into a few plain parts (*One person, many roles* uses four: who someone is, roles and lists, sharing it safely, keeping it right) and show every film inside its part.
- **Close a series where it began, then hand it on.** The last film's last chapter redraws the series' line as its opening film drew it, writes each film's lesson inside its own card as the voice says it (lessons on extra tags round the parts read as a confusing stack), then sees the whole line from further off as one part of something larger, and names the next series. Where a film's title already is its lesson, write its takeaway instead, so the card doesn't say the same thing twice.
- **Write each decision down with its date,** in the treatment or the story outline, so the next session starts from it.
- **Films stand alone.** No numbers on screen, and none for the site's standalone films; open with a one-line recap, and place each film under the part of the overview it goes deeper on. A series is the exception: its films are numbered, always as "Film 2 of 7", and each film's page ends with *In the series*: the previous and the next film, and the whole series in order. Seven films without numbers were hard to keep track of.
- **Let the site count the films.** A series' films are made side by side and come out in any order. "Nine of eleven films are out", changed the same way by two pull requests, merges without a conflict and is wrong. Write no count by hand: the course bar counts the films, a film that is out leaves *Still to come* by itself, and a film that comes out before the one numbered before it keeps its opening story in its own `site.json`.
- **Quote earlier films exactly.** A late film that shows what an earlier one drew (a capability, a measure, a rule, a target) must use its words and keep it the same kind of thing. *A map people and agents can read* first wrote "connecting customers" where films 4 to 10 say "connect customers", gave film 8's measure a name of its own, and called *Why it moves*' ten-day outcome a regulator's target; each read as something new. Numbers keep their meaning too: twenty-one days was *Now the questions appear*'s network report, which stops the clock at approval, so it couldn't come back as the measure's value. Find each name, number and ID in the earlier film's script, and copy it. The exception is a change of standard: what the standard now draws differently, the later film draws the new way, as the learning modules teach it. The map's agent was first an actor, as *Who does it, and where meaning changes* drew the outage agent; ArchiMate 4 draws software in a role, and so do the series' labs.
- **Keep one number per rule across the series.** *Who is it?* tested the person queue against 7 days while *Kept, not cleaned* drew a 10-day limit, and learners see both films and the labs. Settle a shared number in the series' *Decided* table before another film draws it.
- **Turn every review finding into a check** (`site-tools/check_site.py`, `site-tools/smoke.py`, `tools/check.py`), so it can't come back.
- **Choose a palette after reading the scores of the films made beside it.** A series' films are made side by side, so a sound no film on the site uses yet may already belong to one still in its branch: the oboe first planned for *Fixed where it starts* plays in *Teachers of data*'s trio. Read every branch's `tools/score.py` before choosing, and write the choice in the script's *Decided* table.

*Ask: what did the last film teach us, and where is it written down?*

## 9. From a series to a course

What *From words to data*, seven films made together, added.

- **Follow one idea through every film.** The credential runs through all seven, from four offices' four numbers to an AI agent's draft, so each film picks up where the last one left it. A thread keeps a course from becoming a row of lectures.
- **Start each film in the past, with something still true.** A monkey's alarm call, a merchant's ledger, a library's card catalogue, a cash register: each opens a film with an idea that outlived its materials. History carries the concept; the present tests it.
- **Give every film its own sound.** One shared motif (four notes, in each film's key and on its own instrument) ties them together; everything else differs: a kalimba and a choir, an organ and a lute, a marimba against strings, library jazz, synth arpeggios, glass bells, strings and a clock. Seven films with one palette would sound like one long film.
- **Share tools, not copies.** One set of tools and one instrument library serve every film; each film keeps only its own words, pictures and score. A fix lands everywhere at once.
- **Write the narration first, for all the films.** Voicing every script before drawing any picture fixes the timeline, shows which films are too thin, and lets the pictures be made in parallel.
- **Generate what repeats, and check it's current.** With forty pages in two languages, the pages come from each film's data (`site-tools/build_series.py`), and the site check fails if a page isn't what the generator makes.
- **Check every frame, not every tenth of a second.** A ring whose radius dipped below zero for one frame stopped a full render; the checker now draws every frame the video will.
- **Bridge every detour back to the thread.** A philosopher's rabbit made "the grain" land out of nowhere until the next line brought it home: Mei's degree and two microcredentials are one graduate, or three credentials. Every excursion into the past ends on the credential.
- **Let the resolution follow the model.** Once the offices agree a definition, say which numbers counted things it leaves out; "none was wrong" contradicts the diagram on screen. And don't make agreement sound cheap: it takes people, trade-offs and time.
- **Connect look-alikes, rather than rank them.** A glossary, an ontology and a data model can all draw the credential, and viewers confuse them. The first cut of *Meaning machines can read* set the ontology beside the glossary and the semantic layer, but never beside the data model, the comparison most often got wrong; the next set the two side by side as rivals with different jobs, which made a practitioner ask which one to keep. The chapter that stayed, *One map, many views*, shows where they all come from: one architecture map, where each concept is written once, and the views drawn from it, each with its job. Show the source and the flow, and the differences explain themselves. Name each part where it first appears, too: *What's in a word* now labels its glossary and its conceptual model on the sheet.
- **Build labs from a few kinds.** Sort, count, pick, compose and step through cover most ideas; each lab is words plus one picture drawn with the film's own components. Every word a picture draws comes from the language pack, and text is sized for the lab's real width.
- **When every film builds one thing, give the series one set of labs.** *The map before the data* adds to one map in every film, so it has one labs page and one scenarios page for all eleven: a lab for each film's one mechanism, and scenarios that say which film they come from. What the films leave unsaid can be taught there: the series' standard changed to ArchiMate 4 mid-series, and a lab and five scenarios show and test what changed, while the films simply use it. Check every answer against the films as built, not only against the plan, and settle any difference with the film's own thread: a first draft gave generating power an owner the films never found, and the lab and film 11 first drew the map's agent two ways, until both drew it as version 4 does, as software in a role. A lab never fills a gap the films leave open.
- **Lay each language out at its own length.** Spanish runs longer than English, and some of its words are long (*electrodependiente*, *comercialización*). Lay every picture out from the measured words: boxes that grow to three lines on a phone, a long word broken between syllables, a two-line heading balanced so no word is left alone, and a label that takes its own row when it can't sit beside a title. Review every lab at 320 and 390 px in both languages, played through to its end.

*Ask: if someone watched only one film, would they still want the next?*

## Checklists

**Before the script**
- [ ] The two-audience promise is written down.
- [ ] One thing to follow, from where it's born to a human outcome.
- [ ] The metaphor is tested against the hardest mechanism, and each layer has one job.
- [ ] A table maps every object to a mechanism.
- [ ] The opening story's four beats are said outright: scenario, problem, solution, benefits. Someone new to the story can say from the narration alone why it was a problem and what the fix gave people, and the script's *Opening story* table quotes the line for each.
- [ ] The opening story's link to the idea is said in one line, the main story uses it, and the film comes back to it at the end.
- [ ] The opening's example shows the film's point plainly: the narration says what it shows, and no detail in it reads as the opposite.
- [ ] What practitioners do first comes first, and an exception is named as one.
- [ ] The roles, words and cases are ones the domain uses and viewers have lived; an unusual case says how it happens.
- [ ] Every check shown serves the right kind of rule: tests for what the data must be, a tracking table for what the business must do.

**Before rendering**
- [ ] The rigour sheet covers every scene.
- [ ] Style frames and a short voice test are approved.
- [ ] `tools/pace.py`: about 120 to 135 words a minute (at most 140 in a chapter), the voice speaking at most 82% of any chapter, about three wordless endings in the whole film, no sentence followed by less than 0.5 s, and no stop of 2.5 s or more inside a chapter.
- [ ] Stills are checked at every named moment, with nothing under the captions, every shape reading as what it is, and one person doing each action.
- [ ] What is far sits behind what is near: a road or a path goes behind the brows and trees in front of it, and whoever walks it grows smaller and passes behind them.
- [ ] Hands hold what they hold as hands do: along the hand, on top of an open palm or under pinching fingertips. Two hands passing something hold one end each, never one hand over the other, and it never jumps from one to the other. Stills a few frames apart show it.
- [ ] Text reads on a phone: about 28 px or more in the frame, or marked as decoration, with no words drawn over other words (`tools/legible.py --overlaps`).
- [ ] One language on screen. A film made in English shows no words in another language: they read as forced. Show the glossary's synonyms, or nothing, and leave other languages to the captions.
- [ ] Every "the code", "the system" or "the data" in the narration says which one.
- [ ] Every claim that a tool can't do something is checked against what today's tools do.
- [ ] Every name, number and ID taken from an earlier film is copied from that film's script, and means what it meant there.
- [ ] No product is drawn or named as the home of rules a discipline keeps in its own tools, and code shown for them is tagged as the simplest form.
- [ ] The sound: effects below about 2.5 kHz, and a rhythm only where the story turns to an error or a rush.
- [ ] Music is held only under a story and at the end; under the explaining it comes now and then.
- [ ] Nothing trembles or pulses: no vibrato, no two voices a little out of tune on one note, and no two notes a semitone apart held together.
- [ ] A held note, measured on its own, swings no more than about 2 dB: more is two voices beating, which a phone's speaker turns into a pulse.
- [ ] `tools/check.py`: every moment of the film draws without an error. Stills only sample a few moments.

**Before publishing**
- [ ] Product names, maturity and sources are checked on the day.
- [ ] Captions are generated in every language.
- [ ] Logos are unaltered and credited, and the licences are stated.
- [ ] The poster is looked at after `tools/publish.py` draws it: its moment comes after every fade has settled, so no two texts show in one place. *Who is it?*'s first poster caught a date changing into another.
- [ ] The labs and scenarios still match the film: same names, numbers and colours.
- [ ] Tested on a phone, in dark mode and with the keyboard only.
- [ ] The videos are released by the release workflow, and watched once from the release.

| 1 | Which part of the story does this film take? | The pilgrimage to Ise in the 1700s. Ordinary people might not travel as they pleased, but a pilgrimage was hard to refuse, and people said "once in a lifetime, to Ise". A farmer who had never left his village didn't know the road, and the journey cost more than one household could spare; so villages formed pilgrimage groups (*Ise-kō*), every household paying in and two or three a year, chosen by lot, going for all; priests from Ise, the *oshi*, visited the villages every year with charms and calendars, lodged the pilgrims at Ise and guided them to the shrines; guidebooks (*dōchūki*) listed every station. Nobody was made to go, and in 1771 more than two million crossed the Miyagawa into Ise in a few months, counted at the ferry. It stands for a platform teams choose because the way is laid out for them. The plan's line was "pilgrims and merchants chose the roads because they were easier; guidebooks and prints followed": the film keeps the pilgrims, the guidebooks and the prints, and leaves out merchants, who travelled for trade and needed no persuading. Checked against Kotobank (*Sekai daihyakka jiten*, *Nihon daihyakka zensho*, *Seisenban Nihon kokugo daijiten*, *Dejitaru daijisen*, Yamakawa, Britannica, *Mypedia*). | *Once in a lifetime*, *Teams, as customers*, *A guide, and someone to ask*, *Counted at the ferry*, *The road, chosen* |
| 2 | What does the film leave unsaid? | The years of the other great waves, 1705 and 1830, with more pilgrims still; the names of the comic tale (*Tōkaidōchū hizakurige*), its author and the printmaker (Hiroshige), drawn only as sheets in the series' own style; the oshi's other gifts and their business, since lodging pilgrims was also their living; the travel passes. The number of pilgrims in an ordinary year is said as "hundreds of thousands", since the sources give from 300,000 to 700,000. What the journey cost, and how many households a group sent, weren't found, so the farmer is the film's own example. What the story didn't achieve is for the series' last film. | *Once in a lifetime* |
| 3 | Is the data story believable? | Yes. Eleven tickets and six weeks for a first data product is common before a platform offers self-service, and so are the teams that go around it, a database on a team's own server and spreadsheets; the film says it blamelessly: the platform was the safe way, but not yet the easy one. A template that makes a whole repository from one form is how practitioners offer a golden path, and two days to a first data product once it exists is believable for a small, well-defined product. Eight teams of eleven choosing it within the year, and none told to, is a good result, not a perfect one. | *Six weeks*, *A path already laid*, *Two days*, *Counted at the ferry* |
| 4 | Who asks, who reviews, and who owns? | A year ago network's team asked the platform team in tickets, a technical team's requests to another; on the path a team fills in one form instead. A domain team writes only its logic, in a pull request the data team reviews; no business user reviews a pull request, and no owner or steward does. Each data product is owned by its domain, shown with its owner's gold tag: network, metering, field services. The contract for a data product's readers comes with the template and is agreed team to team. | *Six weeks*, *A path already laid*, *Two days* |
| 5 | Are the numbers real? | No, invented: eleven tickets, six weeks, eleven domain teams (six on screen: network, metering, field services, retail, generation and finance), March's two days, eight teams of eleven by December, two to four days, nine in ten, and the guide's times for each step. | Throughout |
| 6 | Doesn't leaving the path break the rules? | No. A team may leave the path where it needs something else, as the CNCF's white paper asks of a platform (*optional and composable*), and that part is then its own to run; the platform's own checks still hold off the path, every request checked where the data is, as *Security all the way through* showed. What a team gives up is the template's help. | *A path already laid* |
| 7 | Why does the voice name no framework? | Plain words: the film teaches *Team Topologies*' platform as a product, the CNCF white paper's attributes of platforms (platform as a product, self-service, documentation and onboarding, optional and composable, secure by default) and its maturity model's adoption, from push to pull, without naming them; "guardrails, not gates" is practitioners' own phrase. They are named in the script, checked on the day. | The script |
| 8 | Why hand over to *Services with customers*? | The plan says so: that film, in *Architecture that keeps up*, shows how to write down who each service is for and what they need. The voice names it once, and a card says *another series*. | *Teams, as customers* |
| 9 | Is the practice map right in ArchiMate? | The path is one of the data platform's technology services, *a new data product, from the template*, beside its connectors, monitoring, masks and log, and the catalogue; it serves each data product made from it, application components each owned by its domain; in the motivation layer, a goal, *teams choose the platform*, realised by two outcomes, *eight teams of eleven* and *a first data product in days*. An outcome realising a goal is allowed in ArchiMate 3.2; the licensed version 4 specification wasn't read, so this is worth a look. | *The data practice on the map* |

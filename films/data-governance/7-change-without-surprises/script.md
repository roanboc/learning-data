# Data governance · Change without surprises: script

*The script of the seventh film of the data governance series, *Governance you can use*: about 4:40, in seven chapters, in English, painted in watercolour. The times are estimates until the voice is recorded; then the narration lives in `source/src/narration.js` and the pauses in `source/src/breath.js`, and where they differ from this page, the source wins. ## The promise

Every line passes two tests: anyone who has changed something other people depend on can follow it, and an experienced data engineer or governance lead finds nothing wrong in it. Sweden switched sides of the road in one morning, and no one was surprised, because everyone knew what would change, when, and what to do. A change to data that people depend on needs the same three things. A board that meets once a month, with a form answered from memory, can't know what a change reaches; the project's lineage can, and a check writes it into the pull request: the models downstream, the tests, the things people use, and who to ask. The check finds who a change reaches; people decide what it breaks, and changing what existing data means is breaking, even when no column changes. So add instead of changing: a new column breaks no one. A new version, beside the old one with a date, is the last resort, for a change that can't be added. And the people a change reaches hear it before it goes in, by name, in their own words, with a date and help. **Change control is a step in the change, not a meeting.**

## The story in one paragraph

At ten to five on the morning of 3 September 1967, every car still on Sweden's roads stopped, crossed from the left side to the right, and at five drove on. Parliament had decided four years before, and a commission had prepared ever since: new signs went up on the right and stayed covered until one night, a booklet reached every home, the change's sign was on milk cartons, and thousands of buses got doors on the other side. The change was huge, and it surprised no one. At the university, the word *award* has just changed: microcredentials of at least 6 credit points now count, and the new words went to their owners by name (*One word, one home*). Now Jun has to change what the models count. The binder's policy 19 says changes are assessed for impact before they're made; it runs as a change board once a month, with a form answered from memory, and the next one meets after the draft submission. Instead, the change carries its impact: a check reads the project's lineage and writes into Jun's pull request what the change reaches. His first draft relabels the big microcredentials as awards, and reaches nine models, 39 tests and three things people use: Planning's census dashboard, Finance's forecast and the wallet app. Planning and Finance didn't ask for it: both use the credit learners have towards an award, part of which comes from microcredentials, so their numbers would quietly move. No column changed, so the check had nothing to stop; Mei sees that *microcredential* would mean something else, and that's breaking. Jun adds instead: every microcredential stays a microcredential, and a new column says which ones count as awards. The check calls it additive: nothing anyone reads changes until they choose the new column, and the one thing people use downstream today is the wallet app. A new version, beside the old one with a date, is kept for a change that can't be added, and the check stops a breaking change without one. Jun writes to the wallet team before it goes in, in their words, with a date and his help. The draft submission reads the new column from Wednesday, and Planning's census report from its next run, so the two numbers will agree. The change goes in when everyone it reaches is ready: on Tuesday, before Wednesday's draft submission runs (*The facts the films share*). Yusuf takes policy 19 off the shelf: it runs in every pull request now.

## Rigour to check before recording

Checked on 21 October 2026; the sources are listed below.

- **Dagen H, the hour.** On Sunday 3 September 1967, every vehicle on the road stopped at 04:50, changed to the right, and waited, "the ten minutes' standstill" (*de tio minuternas stillestånd*, Swedish Wikipedia, *Högertrafikomläggningen*); at 05:00 it drove on. A general traffic ban held across the whole country from 01:00 to 06:00 ("Ett generellt trafikförbud rådde i hela landet från klockan 01.00", M Sverige, 1 September 2017), with longer ones in some towns (Stockholm's from 10:00 on Saturday 2 September). So "every car still on Sweden's roads": only the few allowed were on them.
- **The decision and the commission.** The Riksdag decided on 10 May 1963, by 294 votes to 50. Statens högertrafikkommission (HTK) was set up on 28 June 1963 to plan and carry out the switch, and closed in 1967: "four years". The referendum of 16 October 1955 had gone 82.9% against, of valid votes; the film doesn't mention it (one source says "of the population", which is wrong).
- **Signs.** New signs for the right were put up beside the old ones and covered; in the night to 3 September the covers came off (Norrbottens museum, 1 September 2017; Smålandsposten, 31 August 2017, has 20,000 people at work on the day "to uncover new signs and cover old ones"). Counts differ: about 300,000 (Norrbottens museum), about 350,000 (a 1966 estimate, in English Wikipedia) and 360,000 (Smålandsposten), so the film gives no number. Smålandsposten's 130,000 reminder signs (one source) aren't in the film.
- **The booklet and the cartons.** A 32-page booklet, printed in 8 million copies, went to every household in Sweden the week before (Norrbottens museum). The change's sign was on milk cartons, and even on underwear (English Wikipedia; Smålandsposten's *mjölkpaket* and *H-kalsonger*). The film draws a plain letter H, not the 1967 logo.
- **Buses.** About 8,000 buses with doors on the wrong side had to be rebuilt or replaced (Smålandsposten); English Wikipedia gives over 1,000 new buses and about 8,000 rebuilt; Swedish Wikipedia about 7,500 buses in all. "Thousands" holds across them.
- **Afterwards.** In September and October 1967, half as many people died on Sweden's roads as in the same months of 1966 (M Sverige; Norrbottens museum's monthly figures), and accidents rose again in 1968 and 1969. The film claims only that the change surprised no one, not that it made the roads safer.
- **The name.** *Dagen H*, "H Day", the H for *högertrafik*, traffic on the right. Said once ("Dahgen Hoh" for the voice), never written on screen.
- **1967's streets and clothes.** Police directing traffic in dark uniforms, peaked caps and white gloves. Stockholmskällan's record of the information film of 1967–68 shows the switch at Slussen, with the police directing it by radio at five in the morning; Swedish Wikipedia has the police at Kungsgatan, Stockholm, on 3 September 1967. Check the drawings against period photographs before painting. The crossroads is a town's, not a particular one.
- **The university's story** follows *The facts the films share* (21 October 2026). The glossary's *award* changed on Monday of Yusuf's first week.

## The promise

Every line passes two tests: someone who has never measured data quality can follow it, and a data quality lead or a data steward finds nothing wrong in it. It opens the module, so it sets the module's first idea, Joseph Juran's: quality is fitness for use. The same data is good enough for one use and not for another, so start from the uses: who uses the data, for what, and what goes wrong, for whom, when it's wrong. For each use, name the few things it can't do without (its critical data) and ask six questions of them (DAMA's dimensions: complete, unique, valid, consistent, accurate, on time). Then agree a level for each use with the people who use it, owned by the person who answers for the data, and write it down beside the data. Governance comes before tooling: the tools record what people have agreed. **Ask what it's for before you ask if it's good.**

## The story in one paragraph

Duisburg, 1569. The mapmaker Gerardus Mercator publishes a new chart of the world, made for sailors. At sea, a pilot steered by compass, on a bearing: one fixed angle to north, held hour after hour. But on the charts of the time, a straight line between two ports wasn't a bearing a ship could hold: steer the angle the chart showed, and the ship slowly left the line, and on a long crossing it could miss the coast it was making for, and its crew spend days more at sea, short of water. Mercator stretched his chart towards the poles, a little more at every degree, so every course on one bearing is a straight line: a pilot could rule a line between two ports, read its angle and steer it. Thirty years later, Edward Wright worked out the stretch exactly, and most charts for sailors are still drawn this way. The price was chosen on purpose: far from the equator, every country swells, and Greenland looks as big as Africa, which is fourteen times larger. Right for steering, wrong for comparing countries: one chart, made for one use. At the university, in December 2025, six ceremonies in the Great Hall presented 2,031 graduates. The graduation list, which says who graduates, at which ceremony, with which award, and the name printed on each certificate, went to the printer at 5 pm on Thursday 13 November, about four weeks before the first ceremony, and passed every check on it. Still, 41 certificates were printed again: 26 had a wrong name, 9 a wrong award, and 6 graduates were missing, each about 120 dollars with its courier, and a graduate might cross the stage holding an empty folder. The same list had seated the hall well, every ceremony's count right to within a few seats. Andrés Peñaloza, who will cross the same stage next December with a Master of Data Analytics, is held in the student system as Andres Penaloza, without his accents: right for a seat, and not his name on a certificate. In January 2026, Malia Fonoti, the graduations officer, who uses the list most, Mei Tanaka, in the registrar's office, who answers for its names and awards, and Jun Park, who builds it, sit down with it. They don't ask if it's good. They ask who uses it, for what, and what goes wrong, for whom, when it's wrong: the seating plan needs each ceremony's count within 2%; the certificates need every name, award and date exactly right, zero wrong; the printed programme needs the same names as the certificates; and the government's count of awards, which Planning sends, needs each award counted once, exactly. One list, four uses, four levels. For each use, they name its critical data and ask DAMA's six questions; seating leans on complete, a certificate on accurate. Malia agrees the ceremonies' levels and Planning the government's count, and Mei owns them all. Jun writes it down beside the data: each use as a quality rule in the list's data contract, in the Open Data Contract Standard; its critical columns marked as critical data; every check in dbt naming its dimension and its owner; and on Databricks, each critical column carrying a governed tag. If quality keeps failing anyway, look first at your governance, then at your tools. Mercator's chart said what it was for, in its title: for the use of sailors. The graduation list now says what it's for, too.

## The reference, and practice

The module's reference is DAMA-DMBOK's data quality chapter as the map, with Danette McGilvray's *Ten Steps* and Thomas Redman's practice as the spine, and the quality movement underneath (the module's *Decided* table records why). For this film, four bodies of knowledge, each named once and none taught:

| Body of knowledge | What it adds | Where the film uses it |
|---|---|---|
| Joseph Juran, *Quality Control Handbook* (1951) | Quality as fitness for use: judged by the people who use a thing, for what they use it for | *One list, four uses*: "The quality pioneer Joseph Juran called quality fitness for use." |
| DAMA's six dimensions (DAMA UK's *The six primary dimensions for data quality assessment*, 2013; DAMA-DMBOK, second edition, 2017) | Six plain questions to ask of data: complete, unique, valid, consistent, accurate, on time; and critical data, the few elements a use can't do without | *What each use needs*: the six questions, and each use's critical data |
| ISO/IEC 25012:2008, the data quality model | Fifteen named characteristics of data quality, inherent and system-dependent | Named in one line: "An ISO standard names fifteen; six are enough to start." |
| The Open Data Contract Standard (Bitol, a Linux Foundation project; the project pins v3.1.0), and dbt's and Databricks' docs | A quality rule written beside the data, with its dimension, its severity and its business impact; checks that record their owner and dimension; a governed tag on each critical column | *Written beside the data* |

**Where the film adopts, extends or differs.** It adopts Juran's definition and DAMA's six dimensions as questions, and DAMA-DMBOK's critical data elements. It extends them with the practice the module's plan asks for: each level agreed with the people who use the data, and owned by the person who answers for it (Mei), as McGilvray's and Redman's work puts data's customers first. It differs on weight: ISO/IEC 25012's fifteen characteristics, and the longer lists of dimensions that MIT's Total Data Quality Management and Wang and Strong's study found, are named in one line, because six questions are enough to start. Where a quality rule is often taught as a check in a tool, the film makes the rule a use's level first, written in the data contract, and the checks in dbt and the tags on Databricks follow it: governance before tooling.

**Practice (the module's plan and the author's words, 5 November 2026):** if teams struggle with the basics, "you probably need to improve either your governance or your tooling", so the film says it once, governance first. A practitioner will recognise the data story: a table that passes every check and still lets down one of its uses, because nobody wrote down what each use needs; and a level agreed in a meeting that lives on in someone's head. The film's levels are believable ones: a seating plan can absorb a few seats either way; a certificate can't absorb one wrong letter.

**What the film leaves to others.** How a check warns or stops, and whom it tells, is *Rules that run*'s and *Too good to be true*'s, which the film names; a data contract between systems is *Silent change*'s; measuring the list by hand and profiling it are *A hundred records*'; accuracy against a reference outside the data is *Valid isn't true*'s; timeliness is *Late is wrong too*'s.

## Rigour to check before recording

Each source with the day it was read.

- **Mercator's chart, 1569.** Wikipedia, *Mercator 1569 world map* (read 5 November 2026): published at Duisburg in August 1569; titled *Nova et Aucta Orbis Terrae Descriptio ad Usum Navigantium Emendate Accommodata*, "properly adapted for use in navigation", corrected "to be more useful to sailors", so that courses of constant bearing (rhumb lines) are straight lines; on the plane charts of the time, "the straight lines emanating from the compass roses are not rhumb lines so that they do not give a true bearing"; Mercator increased the latitude scale towards each pole "in proportion to the lengthenings of the parallels"; later, "Edward Wright and others showed how ... the calculation of the latitude scale" could be done. The film's "Ad usum navigantium · For the use of sailors" shortens the title's middle words, and says "sailors" for *navigantes*.
- **Wright.** Edward Wright's *Certaine Errors in Navigation* (1599), which set out the stretch as a table of the latitude scale: thirty years after 1569. "Most charts for sailors are still drawn this way": nautical charts are drawn on Mercator's projection, as Wikipedia's *Mercator projection* says (read 5 November 2026).
- **The crossing.** "On a long crossing, it could miss the coast it was making for, and its crew spend days more at sea, short of water": what the plane chart's error could cost, said in general, not as one voyage. The route drawn, Lisbon to St John's, is the film's example, not a voyage from the sources. An assumption for the author. Its numbers, worked out in `src/chart.js` from the two ports (Lisbon 38.72° N, 9.14° W; St John's 47.56° N, 52.71° W): the straight line ruled on the plane chart reads about 78.5° west of north; held as a bearing, it reaches St John's longitude at about 45.3° N, 2.3° (about 137 nautical miles) south of the port, and passes south of Newfoundland; the bearing that reaches St John's, ruled on Mercator's chart, is about 74.4° west of north. The chart spans 66° W to 4° E and 33° to 60° N, stretched about the parallel of 46° N.
- **Greenland and Africa.** Africa about 30.4 million km², Greenland about 2.17 million km²: 14 times (Wikipedia, *Africa* and *Greenland*, read 5 November 2026). On the film's chart, Greenland is drawn about as large as Africa at the chart's own scale, as Mercator's projection draws it.
- **Juran.** Joseph M. Juran, *Quality Control Handbook* (1951), quality as fitness for use. The film says it without a year.
- **DAMA.** DAMA UK, *The six primary dimensions for data quality assessment* (2013): completeness, uniqueness, timeliness, validity, accuracy and consistency, asked in the film as six questions; DAMA-DMBOK, second edition (2017), for critical data elements. ISO/IEC 25012:2008, the data quality model, names fifteen characteristics.
- **The Open Data Contract Standard.** Its *Data Quality* page, latest (read 5 November 2026, and again on 10 November 2026): a rule's `type` (text, library, sql, custom), `dimension` (accuracy, completeness, conformity, consistency, coverage, timeliness, uniqueness), `severity`, `businessImpact` ("Consequences of the rule failure") and `customProperties`, and a property's `criticalDataElement`; the project pins v3.1.0. A published open standard; no status to date.
- **dbt.** *Add data tests to your DAG* and *meta* (docs.getdbt.com, read 10 November 2026): data tests, the `data_tests:` key (with `tests:` still an alias), and `meta` set in a test's `config`. Neither page marks what the film uses with a release status.
- **Databricks.** Azure Databricks' *Governed tags* (updated 13 October 2026) and *Apply tags to Unity Catalog securable objects* (updated 26 October 2026), read 10 November 2026: governed tags are account-level tags with a tag policy for who may assign them and which values they take, set on Unity Catalog objects, columns among them, and seen in Catalog Explorer. Neither page gives governed tags or column tags a release status; the tags page marks tagging *external metadata* objects as in Public Preview and automated tag assignment as beta, neither of which the film uses or shows. Catalog Explorer is drawn for the film, at the level of what it shows, with Databricks' official logo; no screen is captured.

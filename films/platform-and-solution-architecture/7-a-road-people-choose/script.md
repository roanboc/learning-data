# Platform and solution architecture · A road people choose: script

*The script of *A road people choose*, film 7 of 8 of *Platform and solution architecture*, the fourth series of the topic *Enterprise architecture*, in ten chapters, in English, drawn in the look of *The map before the data*. Written on 12 November 2026 from the author's brief of 11 November 2026 and the series' plan (`../proposal.md`), which the author confirmed that day. Made while the author was away; this film's questions and assumptions are in `docs/assumptions/05-enterprise-architecture/4-platform-and-solution-architecture/7-a-road-people-choose.md`. It carries no draft label (the four series' shared *Decided* table). The narration is in `source/src/narration.js`; this file gives its reasons.*

## The promise

Every line passes two tests: someone new to data work can follow it, and a platform lead, an enterprise architect or a data engineer finds nothing wrong in it. Its idea: a data platform is a product, and the teams that build on it are its customers, so it works when they choose it, not when they're told to use it. A platform can be the safe way and still not the easy one: tickets to wait on, a pipeline written from nothing, and the rules learned by being refused, so teams in a hurry go around it. An order would make teams use it, not want it. So the platform team goes to the teams, to see what they need to do and what gets in their way, and makes the right way the easiest one: a path already laid, from a template, with one form and no tickets; the rules built into it, met as a team works, as guardrails, not gates; a guide written from teams' questions, and someone to ask. A team writes only what's its own, its logic, and may leave the path where it needs something else, that part then its own to run. And the platform team counts what tells it the platform works as a product: how many teams choose it, how long a first data product takes, and what teams say.

**Takeaway:** "Make the right way the easiest way, and count who chooses it."

## The story in one paragraph

Japan, the 1700s: ordinary people may not travel as they please, but a pilgrimage is hard to refuse, and people say: once in a lifetime, to Ise, to the great shrine. From Edo, that's the Tōkaidō most of the way, then the road south to Ise. But a farmer who has never left his village doesn't know the road, where to sleep, how far to the next station, what it all costs, and the journey costs more than one household can spare. Villagers need a way to go that they can follow without knowing the road, and a way to pay for it. So villages form pilgrimage groups: every household pays in each year, and each year two or three, chosen by lot, go for everyone. Priests from Ise, the oshi, visit those villages every year, bringing charms and calendars; at Ise they lodge the pilgrims in their own houses, and guide them to the shrines. And guidebooks list every station on the way, how far apart they are, the inns and the sights. Nobody is made to go, but the way is laid out, paid for together, and someone is expecting them at the end; so ordinary people go, hundreds of thousands in an ordinary year, and in 1771 more than two million cross the river into Ise in a few months, counted at the ferry. Later, a comic tale of two travellers on their way to Ise becomes a craze like none before, and prints of the road's stations follow. At the utility, a year ago, network's team wanted its first data product on the platform, planned outages for the call centre: it took eleven tickets, each waiting its turn in the platform team's queue, a pipeline written from nothing, and a first pull request sent back for the catalogue's rules; six weeks. Two other teams gave up: field services runs a database on its own server, and metering works from spreadsheets. Nobody did anything wrong: the platform was the safe way, but not yet the easy one. Ravi Menon could order every team onto it, but an order makes teams use it, not want it, and the quick way finds a way around. So he treats the platform as a product and the teams as its customers; his team sits with each domain team, and three things come up again and again: waiting on tickets, not knowing where to start, and learning the rules by being refused. The template every new job starts from grows into one for a whole data product: one form, no tickets, and minutes later a repository with all a data product needs; the team writes only its logic, in a pull request the data team reviews, and may leave the path where it needs something else. The rules are built into the path; a guide lists every step and whom to ask, and a question in the platform's channel is answered the same day. In March metering's team tries again, on the path: its checked meter reads are in the catalogue in two days, and its spreadsheets are retired. By December eight of the eleven domain teams have chosen the path, and none was told to; a first data product takes two to four days, nine in ten teams would tell another to use it, field services' server is switched off, and metering's team adds a check to the template, so every team gets it.

Where the film adds to them: it says nobody did anything wrong when two teams went around the platform, and takes them back as customers, not as rule-breakers; it lets a team leave the path where it needs something else, with that part then its own to run; and it counts teams that chose the path without being told, not teams on it. It names no product.

Practice, from the author's brief and the series' plan: the platform as a product, with templates, paved paths, self-service and guardrails, and its adoption measured; the data story is the plan's, a domain team shipping its first data product in two days from the template, not six weeks. The domain team writes its own logic and the data team reviews the pull request (AGENTS.md, *Owners and stewards never review pull requests*); the template carries a contract for the data product's readers, agreed team to team. The platform team's tickets in *Six weeks* are a technical team's requests to another, not a business user's ask.

## Sound

Chosen in the four series' palettes list on 12 November 2026 and made in `source/tools/score.py`: a violin, as it was played about 1600, gut-strung and bowed plainly in its low and middle notes (G 3 to E flat 5), each note swelling a little in with the bow, held level with a breath of the bow's hair under it, and let go, no vibrato and no slide; with a mandolin, the small gut-strung mandolino of about 1700, plucked with the fingertip, single notes, round and short, never the tremolo and never a strummed chord. The site has both, and no film pairs them, and no film of this topic plays either (the palettes list's pair rule). The series' mark, *the relay*, in A flat: the violin plays A flat, B flat and E flat rising and lets the E flat go as the mandolin takes it an octave down and carries it to A flat, under the title and the end card.

# Locks before the data · Who is it, really?: script

*The script of the film that opens* Locks before the data*, the first series of the module* Data protection and privacy*, written from the module's brief of 5 November 2026 and made on assumptions while the author is away, as he asked that day: zero trust; people, workloads and agents, each an identity; rights from roles, never people; joiners, movers and leavers; a confidentiality agreement signed before any access; a chapter on thinking like an attacker before building; NIST's Cybersecurity Framework 2.0 named once. The ideas film names no product, no cloud and no tool.*

## The promise

Every line passes two tests: anyone who has ever signed in to anything can follow it, and a security engineer finds nothing wrong in it. A request to a data platform is an order, and an order should count only when it proves who sent it. In the Warring States a commander trusted the match of two bronze halves, not the messenger who brought them; a platform can trust the proof each request brings, not the network it comes from. So each person, job and agent gets a name of its own and a person who answers for it, its rights come from a role, never from a person, and the people in each role follow the university's records as they join, move and leave. And since a proof can be stolen, as one was in 257 BC, each identity gets only the least it needs, and Sam thinks like an attacker before he builds.

## The story in one paragraph

China, about 2,300 years ago. Seven kingdoms are at war, each with armies on its borders, days from the capital, and a king's orders travel with messengers. The most dangerous is an order to raise troops: if anyone could bring one, a forged letter or a messenger's lie could put an army on the march, for an order nobody gave, and the commander at his post sees only the man who brings it. The answer is a tally, a small bronze tiger cut in two along its back: the king keeps the right half, the commander the left, and an order to raise troops counts only if the halves fit together. One, now in a museum in Xi'an, carries its rule in forty characters of gold: for fifty soldiers or more, the halves must match; only a beacon fire lets the commander act without it. So the power to send soldiers stays with the king, however far the order travels. Then, in 257 BC, the tally's weak spot: Lord Xinling, a prince of Wei, has the king's own half taken from his rooms, and at the camp the halves match. The general, Jin Bi, still doubts, and is killed; the army marches on an order the king never gave. A match proves someone holds the half, not who they are.

## The reference, and practice

The module's reference holds (its proposal, *The reference*): DAMA-DMBOK's data security area for what is protected and why, so the module reads as one with data governance, and zero trust (NIST Special Publication 800-207) for how. NIST's Cybersecurity Framework 2.0 is named once, to place the films on one map. For this film's question, who a request is and what it may do, the thread took these, named here; the film teaches what they agree on, and names only the framework, once.

| Body of knowledge | What it adds | Where the film uses it |
|---|---|---|
| NIST SP 800-207, *Zero Trust Architecture* (August 2020) | No trust granted for a request's network location; every request authenticated and authorised, per session, by a policy that weighs identity, device and behaviour; least privilege | *Nothing trusted without proof*; *Think like an attacker* (Jin Bi's doubt, kept) |
| NIST Cybersecurity Framework 2.0 (26 February 2024) | Under Protect, its first category, *Identity Management, Authentication, and Access Control* (PR.AA): identities for users, services and hardware (PR.AA-01), authenticated (PR.AA-03), with permissions that follow least privilege (PR.AA-05) | *A name of its own*, the one line that names it |
| DAMA-DMBOK 2, *Data Security* | Access by role, owners who decide who may use their data, confidentiality agreements and the identity lifecycle as data management, not only IT | *Rights from a role*; *Joining, moving, leaving* |
| The *Threat Modeling Manifesto* (2020) and its four questions | "What are we working on? What can go wrong? What are we going to do about it? Did we do a good enough job?"; impersonation ("spoofing") is the first threat in the STRIDE list most teams start from | *Think like an attacker*; *A name of its own* answers the last two |
| OWASP *Non-Human Identities Top 10* (2025) | Jobs and services that run on a person's identity or a long-lived secret; one identity shared by many; identities with no owner | *Three kinds of who*; the job on Jun's token |
| ISO/IEC 27001:2022, Annex A (5.15 to 5.18, 6.5, 6.6) | Access control by role, identity management through the lifecycle, access rights given and taken away, confidentiality agreements, duties that hold after someone leaves | *Joining, moving, leaving* (the six lines) |

**Where the film differs, on purpose.** Zero trust is taught as a habit, not an architecture: the film draws no policy engine or enforcement point, and says "not trusting no one, but trusting nothing without proof" for "never trust, always verify". It doesn't teach role engineering, attribute-based rules or the STRIDE list: *People and groups* builds the groups, and *Power you switch on* the specialists' great power. Agents get one line and a pointer to *AI agents in data work*, which teaches how an agent gets its own identity.

**Practice.** What practitioners recognise: a scheduled job that runs on someone's personal token because that was the quickest way to start, and that breaks, or keeps far too much power, when that person moves; rights given person by person, until nobody can say why 41 people can read a whole table; movers who keep every right they ever had; leavers whose accounts outlive them; and security bolted on after building, when the four questions would have cost an hour before. The fix starts with names and roles before any tool, and the owner of the data says who may use it.

**The author's practice** (3 November 2026): every contributor signs a confidentiality agreement before any access; rights come from roles; agents never get admin accounts. The film says it in its own words: "First, they sign a confidentiality agreement: six lines, in their own voice." and "So rights go to a role, never to a person."

## Sound

A new palette, checked on 10 November 2026 against every `tools/score.py` and `music.py` on main and on all 49 other branches, and against the module's palettes list (`palettes.md` in the project's working folder): **a crwth and a regal**, made for this film in `tools/score.py` (`music.py` has neither).

- **The crwth leads.** The bowed lyre of Wales, played from the Middle Ages to about 1800, and again today from replicas: six gut strings over a flat bridge, four bowed in pairs tuned an octave apart, so every note sounds with its octave twin, two strings that must agree, as the tally's halves must. Bowed softly, no vibrato, a few notes at a time, never a tune of its own tradition.
- **The regal is the gatekeeper.** The small organ of the 1500s whose beating reeds sit in short pipes: nasal and soft, the bellows drawn gently, no tremulant. Monteverdi gave it to Charon, the ferryman who asks who would cross, in *Orfeo* (1607). It holds a few chords under the opening, at chapter turns, where the story comes back, and under the end card.
- **The mark.** One note split into its two strings, the king's high one alone on the right, the commander's low one alone on the left, then bowed together, G 3 and G 4 with the regal's G major, where the halves fit and where a request proves who it is: twice in the opening, then four times (zero trust; Jin Bi's halves that matched; the tiger come back; the end).
- **Keys.** Tuned pure from G, so no chord beats: G major, with C major, D major, E minor and B minor, which are pure here too; E minor for the problem (an order nobody gave, a token anyone could copy). A minor isn't pure here, so A is played only in D major.
- **Music** under the opening story (*Two halves* and *When the halves match*, to the title), then only at a chapter's turn, under a moment on screen or in a pause without words; never a bed under the voice. No chapter is urgent, so nothing pulses: the army on the march is a breath of air and its torches, never a drum.
- **Effects, each tied to something on screen,** timed by the same word as the picture: paper as a card or a badge is laid down; a tap as a tag or a tick comes in; a low knock for a cross or a coral slip; a breath of air as a line is drawn or something passes; a dry wooden clack for the bamboo slips; the camp fires a low warm texture. One at a time, soft, below about 2.5 kHz.
- **Mix** to -16 LUFS. `tools/quiet.py` passes: after the opening the music sounds 20% of the time and comes in 21 times, once every 18.2 s, at most 4 times in any 30 s; its loud moments -27.0 dB, its loudest notes -26.2 dB, the effects' -46.3 dB; 187 effects, at most 4 beginning in one second; nothing beats, no figure repeats, the room moves no held note by more than 0.1 dB, and nothing pulses.

## Rigour to check before recording

Checked on 10 November 2026; the sources are listed below.

- **"China, about 2,300 years ago." "Seven kingdoms are at war."** The Warring States period, 475 to 221 BC, with seven great states (Qin, Chu, Qi, Yan, Han, Zhao, Wei). The Du tally dates from 337 to 325 BC, and Lord Xinling's theft from 257 BC: about 2,350 and 2,280 years ago.
- **Orders by messenger, on bamboo slips.** The period's documents were written on bamboo and wooden slips, tied together; the film draws the order as slips, with no words on them.
- **Dress.** Long robes crossed at the collar and belted, hair in a topknot, a small black cap for a king or a lord and a cloth for a messenger, and armour of small lacquered plates laced in rows: as the period's own evidence shows it, in the lacquered leather armour from the tomb of Marquis Yi of Zeng (433 BC, Hubei Provincial Museum), the Chu silk painting of a lord riding a dragon (Hunan Museum) and, a little later, Qin's terracotta warriors (late third century BC). No flags, seals, writing or symbols of the kingdoms are drawn, and no one is shown hurt.
- **The tally.** A tiger tally (*hufu*) is cast in bronze in two halves, cut along the tiger's back; the inscription of the Du tally says "兵甲之符，右在君，左在杜": the tally for troops, the right half with the ruler, the left at Du (the post). The film's "the king keeps the right half, and the commander takes the left to his post" is that. The Du tally's ruler was still titled lord (Qin's ruler took the title of king in 325 BC); the opening's "king" is the Warring States' ruler in general.
- **The museum's tiger.** The Du tally, in the Shaanxi History Museum in Xi'an, is the left half, the one kept at Du: about 9.5 cm long and 4.4 cm high, a tiger running, with 40 characters inlaid in gold in nine columns (Chinese Wikipedia, 虎符, which dates it to 337 to 325 BC; China Daily, 25 August 2021, from the museum: "the left half of a bronze two-piece tiger-shaped tally"). The film draws it flipped, as the commander's half. Sources differ on when it was found (1957, 1967 or 1973), so the film says only "now in a museum in Xi'an". Its inlay is drawn as strokes too small to read, never as characters.
- **The rule on its flank.** "凡兴士被甲用兵五十人以上，必会君符，乃敢行之。燔燧事，虽毋会符，行": whenever soldiers are raised and armoured, fifty or more, the ruler's half must be matched, and only then may they act; in the matter of beacon fires, even without the match, act. The film says "For fifty soldiers or more, the halves must match, and only then may they march" and "Only a beacon fire, an attack at the border, lets him act without it."
- **"The power to send soldiers stays with the king."** The museum's own reading, through China Daily: the tally reflects "highly centralized military leadership" in Qin.
- **The siege and the theft.** Sima Qian, *Records of the Grand Historian* (*Shiji*), chapter 77, the biography of the prince of Wei (魏公子列傳): in the twentieth year of King Anxi of Wei (257 BC), Qin besieged Handan, the capital of Zhao; the king sent his general Jin Bi with 100,000 men, then, threatened by Qin, halted him at Ye ("留軍壁鄴", "kept the army walled at Ye"). The tally was kept in the king's bedchamber (王臥內), and Lady Ru, his favourite, took it for the prince ("如姬果盜晉鄙兵符與公子"), so "had the king's half taken from his rooms". At Ye, the prince forged the king's command; "晉鄙合符，疑之": Jin Bi matched the tally, and doubted, saying "今單車來代之，何如哉": now you come in a single chariot to take my place, how is this? Zhu Hai, who came with the prince, killed him with an iron hammer; the film says only "He was killed", and shows no killing. The prince picked 80,000 men and attacked; "秦軍解去，遂救邯鄲，存趙": Qin's army withdrew, Handan was saved, and Zhao survived. Chu's army also came to relieve Handan, which the film leaves out with the Records' own telling. Lord Xinling (Wei Wuji) was a son of King Zhao of Wei and the half-brother of King Anxi: "a prince of Wei" (the chapter's own title calls him 魏公子). The theft is known from Sima Qian, written about 160 years later; the film tells it as the Records do.
- **"The oldest trick: being someone else."** Impersonation is the first threat (spoofing) of the STRIDE list, and the oldest in the stories of tallies, seals and passwords alike.
- **The four questions.** The *Threat Modeling Manifesto* (2020), as said: "What are we working on? What can go wrong? What are we going to do about it? Did we do a good enough job?" The film asks them before building, as the manifesto advises, and answers the last two in *A name of its own*.
- **Zero trust.** NIST SP 800-207: "Zero trust assumes there is no implicit trust granted to assets or user accounts based solely on their physical or network location"; access is granted per session, by a dynamic policy that weighs the identity, the device and behaviour, with least privilege. The film's "checked against what that identity may do, and what's usual for it" and Jin Bi's five questions (who, from where, on which device, asking for what, and is that usual) are those signals in plain words. "Not trusting no one, but trusting nothing without proof" is the film's own wording for "never trust, always verify".
- **"A stolen password works as well inside the wall as out."** Why the wall stopped being enough: stolen credentials are among the commonest ways in, and inside a flat network they reach everything (NIST SP 800-207, *Introduction*).
- **NIST's Cybersecurity Framework 2.0** (26 February 2024): Protect's categories are, in order, *Identity Management, Authentication, and Access Control* (PR.AA), *Awareness and Training*, *Data Security*, *Platform Security* and *Technology Infrastructure Resilience*; PR.AA-01 "Identities and credentials for authorized users, services, and hardware are managed by the organization", PR.AA-03 "Users, services, and hardware are authenticated", PR.AA-05 access permissions that "incorporate the principles of least privilege and separation of duties". So "puts it first under Protect: every user, service and device known, checked, and given the least it needs".
- **Three kinds of who.** People, workloads and agents: the framework's users, services and hardware, with agents as the newest kind of workload, each with an owner (OWASP *Non-Human Identities Top 10*; the module's rule that a job or an agent never runs on a person's identity). *AI agents in data work* teaches how an agent gets its own: its films are named in a line, never taught again.
- **Rights from a role.** Role-based access, least privilege (PR.AA-05); the roles are the university's, from *Who does what, end to end* (*Governance on the map*), and an owner decides who may use their data, from *Who decides* (*Governance principles*). The 41 people and the nine who had moved are the film's own numbers, a composite of what access reviews find.
- **Joiners, movers and leavers.** The identity lifecycle, driven from HR's records as the source (ISO/IEC 27001:2022, Annex A 5.16 and 5.18): an account from day one with no access, a mover's old rights taken as the new ones are given, a leaver's account off on the last day.
- **The confidentiality agreement.** The author's practice (3 November 2026): every contributor signs one before any access; ISO/IEC 27001:2022, Annex A 6.6 (confidentiality agreements) and 6.5 (duties that hold after someone leaves). The six lines are the film's own plain wording; signing again every year is the module's assumption, logged for the author.

The token is a sealed card, never written out. "One workspace, open to the internet" stays so until *Walls, doors and keys*.

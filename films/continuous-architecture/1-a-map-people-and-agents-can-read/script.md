## The promise

Every line passes two tests: anyone who has kept a map, a model or a glossary that went stale can follow it, and an experienced enterprise or data architect finds nothing wrong in it. A map lives while people use it, keep it true and can pass it on. The more it's used, the more it's at risk: kept in slides and in a tool only one person edits, with its applications typed in by hand from another system, its copies drift, and a stale map is worse than none, because people trust it. So it moves into an enterprise architecture tool that everyone can reach, with a database and a graph. Much of what it holds is already kept somewhere else: strategy in documents, goals and initiatives in the project management tool, applications and servers in the IT service management tool. The tool brings each one in with its source's ID and keeps it in step; the rest (capabilities, rules, decisions and the links between everything) is written in the map itself, and the business glossary fits too, each term a business object defined once by the business. Keeping a map true is a data integration job as well as a modelling one. Each element is kept once, with a stable ID that everything else points to, a name, a definition, an owner, its relationships named as ArchiMate names them, its evidence with a date, and whether its owner has confirmed it; decisions are kept too, each saying why. A small map could keep all this as text files in a repository, the simplest approach; with many sources and many people in their roles, a utility needs the tool, and the ideas are the same. Every version is kept; a change is proposed as a draft and approved by the owner of that part, and checks run on the map itself. Every view is generated from it, one way, down to dbt's YAML and Databricks' catalogue, so nobody edits a view. Then an agent can read it: given a request, a change or a column, it finds the decision, the owners or the rule, and cites IDs and evidence so a person can check. The agent is on the map too, with its rights written down: it reads and drafts, and never confirms; people confirm. The opening story carries the idea, and says so: in Babylon, from 652 BCE, one of the city's scholars watched the sky each night and wrote down what he saw; the sky repeats itself more slowly than any watcher's working life, so what one watcher knew was lost when he stopped, and they kept a diary of the sky, always in the same order, each watcher carrying on from the last, for nearly six hundred years; astronomers still read it. The film names its test after them, "the watcher's test": could the next one read it, and carry on? **A map stays alive while people use it, keep it true, and can pass it on.**

## The story in one paragraph

In Babylon, in 652 BCE, one of the city's scholars watches the sky each night, and writes down what he sees. For Babylon's king and its temples, the sky sent signs: an eclipse of the moon could warn of the king's death. But the sky repeats itself slowly, some planets only every seventy or eighty years, longer than any watcher's working life, so what one watcher knew was lost when he stopped; they needed a record that outlived him, one the next could read, and carry on. So the scholars kept a diary of the sky, night after night, always in the same order: the moon, the planets among the stars, the weather; at each month's end, the river's level, the prices of six goods from barley to wool, and what had happened in the city; and when clouds hid the sky, the watcher wrote that too: I did not watch. Half a year went on each tablet, and the next watcher carried on from the last. They kept it for nearly six hundred years, under Babylonian, Persian, Greek and Parthian kings; the youngest diary we have is from 61 BCE. From it, later scholars used the sky's cycles, eight years for Venus and eighty-three for Jupiter, to predict the coming year's planets and eclipses, and astronomers today still read Babylon's eclipse records, which show the day growing longer by about 1.8 thousandths of a second a century. At the utility, Tomás's map has the same weakness: if only he can keep it, it stops when he does; kept one way, by everyone, it could outlive anyone's job. The watcher's test: could the next one read it, and carry on? Tomás's last note asks how to keep his map alive. It's used every week now, to sort requests, to give rules a home and to answer the minister, and that's when a map goes stale: it lives in slides and in a modelling tool only Tomás edits, its applications were typed in by hand from the IT service management tool and many have changed since, its copies drift in other people's decks, and the glossary and the catalogue keep their own words. A stale map is worse than none, because people trust it; if Tomás left tomorrow, nobody could carry it on: it fails the watcher's test. So he moves it into an enterprise architecture tool that everyone can reach, with a database and a graph. Much of what it holds is already kept somewhere else: strategy in documents, goals and initiatives in the project management tool, applications and servers in the IT service management tool. The tool brings each one in with its source's ID and keeps it in step, so nobody retypes it; the rest is written in the map itself, capabilities, rules, decisions and the links between everything, and even the business glossary fits, each term a business object defined once by the business. Keeping a map true is a data integration job, as well as a modelling one. Each element is kept once, with a stable ID, a name, a definition, an owner, its relationships, and his two rules from day one, its evidence with a date and whether its owner has confirmed it; decisions are kept too. A small map could keep all this as text files in a repository, the simplest approach; a utility, with many sources and many people in their roles, needs the tool, and the ideas are the same. Every version of the map is kept, with who changed what and when; a change is proposed as a draft, and the owner of each part it touches approves it, while checks run on the map itself and find, every time, that generating power still has no owner. Everything people read is generated from the map, one way: the ArchiMate views, the glossary, the conceptual model, the ontology, the tests and tracking tables, and the heat map, and, where data people work, each dbt model's owner, domain and rule IDs, and each Databricks table's owner, tags and description; a fix goes to the map, and every view follows. Then an agent can read it. Given a request, it asks who will decide what with the answer, then finds the decision, the capability and the owner, or nothing; given a change, it walks the layers and lists the owners who must agree; given a column in the catalogue, it finds the rule behind it, with its owner and its reason; and every answer cites IDs and evidence, so a person can check it. The agent is on the map itself, software filling a role, with its rights written down: it may read everything and draft changes, it may not confirm one, and where two sources disagree it asks the owner; its owner is Tomás. When the regulator changes the retail code, the agent finds the one rule whose reason is that clause, retail's half of day one's rule, drafts its new words and lists what they touch, the billing team's tracking table and two views; the head of retail confirms it, and from the next morning the tracking table shows each bill not corrected within ten working days. Next spring, Zofia starts as a data engineer: a badge, a page of acronyms and a list of systems, as Tomás had, and this time a map. She asks the agent who owns the readings data and which rules it keeps, gets Grace and two rules, each with its reason and a link, and by the afternoon has drafted her first change, for Grace to confirm. In Babylon, no one kept the diary alone: each watcher read the last tablet and carried on, and, written one way, it outlived every watcher who kept it, and every king they served. At the utility, Grace confirms Zofia's change, and the map passes the watcher's test; by the summer, thirty people keep their parts of the map, and their owners confirm each change, so if Tomás left tomorrow, the map would carry on. Keep each element once, in one tool, with an ID, an owner and its evidence, and make every view from it; review every change, let agents read and draft, and let the owners confirm. ## Rigour to check before recording

- **The diary of the sky.** Babylon's scholars kept what modern scholars call the astronomical diaries, and the tablets call *naṣāru ša ginê*, "regular watching". The oldest datable tablet is from 652/651 BCE and the youngest from 61/60 BCE; systematic watching may have begun earlier, under Nabonassar (747 BCE), which the film doesn't say. Short daily notes were gathered into standard diaries, each of half a year (six months, or seven with a leap month). Night by night they record the moon, the planets in relation to stars near their path, and the weather; at each month's end, the river's level (the Euphrates at Babylon), what one shekel of silver bought of six goods, barley, dates, mustard (cuscuta), cress, sesame and wool, and what had happened; where clouds hid the sky, the scribe wrote that the sky was not watched. Most of the several hundred tablets that survive are in the British Museum, translated in A. J. Sachs and H. Hunger, *Astronomical Diaries and Related Texts from Babylonia*, volumes I to III (652 to 61 BCE). Checked on 23 November 2026 in Livius' article on the diaries and the Wikipedia article, which cite that edition, and in Mathieu Ossendrijver's lecture notes on Babylonian astronomy (Freie Universität Berlin).
- **Who watched, and why.** The watchers are drawn as scholars on a roof, one at a time, as silhouettes in the series' own style, never in the style of Mesopotamian art: the tablets don't say how many watched, or from where. A scribe's tablet in one hand and stylus in the other follows Assyrian reliefs. That the sky sent signs to the king and the temples, and that an eclipse of the moon could warn of the king's death, is the omen series *Enūma Anu Enlil* and the letters of Assyrian and Babylonian scholars to their kings, which also tell of a substitute king set on the throne while the danger lasted; the film doesn't show it. The words on the tablet are in English, illustrative of what the diaries record, never a translation of one tablet, and no script is imitated.
- **The kings.** "Babylonian, Persian, Greek and Parthian kings" names the kings of Babylon by the four lines that ruled it while the diaries were kept: from 652 BCE, kings of Babylon (the first, Šamaš-šuma-ukin, an Assyrian prince on its throne; from 626 BCE, Nabopolassar's line), then the Persians (from 539 BCE), the Macedonian and Seleucid Greeks (from 331 BCE) and the Parthians (from 141 BCE). The bands on the line are drawn to those dates.
- **The cycles.** Goal-year texts, written from about 236 BCE, predicted a coming year's planets and the moon's phenomena by looking up the diary one period back: 8 years for Venus, 71 or 83 for Jupiter, 46 for Mercury, 59 for Saturn and 47 or 79 for Mars; almanacs gave the coming year's planets and eclipses. "Some planets only every seventy or eighty years" is Jupiter's and Mars's longer periods. The arcs on screen are drawn, not to scale (Ossendrijver; John M. Steele's and Hermann Hunger and David Pingree's surveys, as those notes cite them).
- **The day growing longer.** F. R. Stephenson, L. V. Morrison and C. Y. Hohenkerk, "Measurement of the Earth's rotation: 720 BC to AD 2015", *Proceedings of the Royal Society A* 472 (2016): records of eclipses, Babylon's timings among the most accurate before telescopes, show the mean solar day growing longer by about 1.8 milliseconds a century, less than tides alone would make it (2.3). The curve on screen is drawn, not their data. Checked on 23 November 2026.
- **What the film leaves out.** A diary records Alexander's death, in Babylon, in 323 BCE; *Kept alive* tells that moment, so this film doesn't. The diaries' names for the stars, their units and their hours are left out too.
- **The watcher's test** is the film's own name for its test. "A map stays alive while people use it, keep it true, and can pass it on" is the film's own summary, built on the series' insights 9 (every claim traces to evidence, and every decision records why) and 12 (a model is true when the people who own it confirm it).
- **An architecture description.** ISO/IEC/IEEE 42010:2022 describes one as stakeholders and their concerns, viewpoints and the views that follow them, model kinds, and the correspondences between them. "Each one answers the questions of the people who read it" is a view addressing its readers' concerns; the film doesn't name the standard. TOGAF's architecture repository is the idea behind keeping it all in one place.
- **The map's tool, and its sources.** Enterprise architecture tools keep the map in a repository that many people reach, each in their role, and keep it in step with the systems that already hold its records, matched on each source's ID; examples checked on 20 October 2026: OrbusInfinity, from Orbus Software, whose ServiceNow integration imports applications and servers from the CMDB and keeps objects and relationships in sync; SAP LeanIX; Ardoq; Bizzdesign Horizzon; Sparx Systems Enterprise Architect; and Avolution ABACUS. The film names none of them, and its window is drawn as no product in particular. Applications typed into a map by hand, which then drift from the configuration database, are a common way maps go stale; seeing enterprise architecture as a data integration job, as well as a modelling one, is the author's own practice (his review of 20 October 2026). CI-00417 is an illustrative configuration item. Where the map's records come from (strategy in documents, goals and initiatives in the project management tool) is illustrative of a utility, not a rule.
- **The glossary in the map.** In ArchiMate, a business object is a concept the business uses, so each glossary term is held as one, with its definition and the owner who defines it, as *Meaning machines can read* holds it; the glossary people read is still a view generated from the map (decided on 16 October 2026). The example dbt project copies its definitions from the glossary, with their `source:` (DEC-PRJ-11).
- **Or kept as text.** Keeping a model as text in a repository, changed through pull requests that owners review, with checks on every change, is what architecture-as-code and docs-as-code teams do; Structurizr does the same for the C4 model, generating its diagrams from a text model. The film shows it once, as the simplest approach for a small map: the file is drawn as YAML, but the film chooses no format. Decision records follow Michael Nygard's architecture decision records (2011), as in *Today, and where it's going*; DEC-007 is that film's record 7, shortened to fit. The IDs are the film's own scheme, a prefix for each kind and a number, never reused: CAP capability, STP step, DOM domain, OBJ business object, RUL rule, MSR measure, OBL obligation, DEC decision, ROL role, APP application component and TEC technology. The counts in the side panel, and the graph's nodes in chapter 3, are illustrative.
- **Named as ArchiMate names them.** The films use ArchiMate 4 (April 2026) and don't say what changed. A rule is a requirement; the step that carries it out realizes it, and it's associated with the business object it governs, so its page lists "realization" and "association", ArchiMate's own names. In version 4 a step is a process in the common domain, whoever does it. The relationships carry no multiplicity: cardinality stays in the logical model (decided on 16 and 17 October 2026).
- **Tomás's two rules** are *Day one*'s, word for word: every note names its source, and a note is a draft until its owner confirms it. Here they become two lines on every element, evidence (a document or a person, with a date) and status.
- **The checks on the map** are the film's own three; any team can run them on every change, in its tool or, for a map kept as text, as tests that fail a pull request. Generating power has had no owner since *What it must be able to do*, and connecting customers was red there, at 34 working days. The change request's evidence cites the measure from *Now the questions appear*, MSR-002, at 18 working days in August 2027, counted Grace's way (24 when she defined it), still short of the ten-day outcome of *Why it moves*: amber, not green. OBL-006 says the measure is reported to the regulator, so Ama approves that line.
- **One map, many views.** The views follow the decision of 16 October 2026, from *Meaning machines can read*: the glossary, the conceptual model, an ontology in OWL for reasoning, the tests on the data and the tracking tables for business rules are generated from the map, one way, so they agree. Nobody edits a generated file; in practice it says so in a header, and a check fails if it differs from what the map would generate, as this site's own `check_site.py` does for its pages.
- **dbt.** From dbt 1.10, custom properties such as an owner, a domain and rule IDs belong under `meta`, inside `config` (dbt's docs, "Upgrading to v1.10", and its deprecations page). dbt's own groups also carry an owner; the film keeps the owner the map gives, in `meta`.
- **Tests and tracking tables.** Since 20 October 2026 the films keep dbt's tests for whether the data is right; a rule about what people do, such as correcting a bill in time, is tracked in an operational table, never shown as a failing test. Here, the billing team's tracking table, built from RUL-022, shows each bill past its window each morning.
- **Databricks.** In Unity Catalog, a table's full name has three parts (catalog, schema, table), and Catalog Explorer shows its owner, its tags, its description (its comment) and its lineage, which Unity Catalog captures down to the column (Databricks' docs, "What is Catalog Explorer?" and its page on data lineage). The page on screen is drawn, not a screenshot. Checked on 18 October 2026.
- **How an agent reads it** isn't shown. Today, agents commonly reach tools and data through the Model Context Protocol (MCP), an open standard that Anthropic gave to the Agentic AI Foundation, under the Linux Foundation, in December 2025. dbt Labs publishes an MCP server that lets an agent read a dbt project's models, lineage and tests, and Databricks' managed MCP servers, for Unity Catalog functions, Vector Search and Genie spaces, are in preview (Public Preview or Beta, depending on the page). An agent that reads the map needs only read access to it and to the catalogue, and its drafts go through the same change requests as anyone's. Checked on 17 October 2026.
- **The agent on the map.** ArchiMate has no element for an AI agent. *Who does it, and where meaning changes* drew the outage agent as an actor, with its rights written down (the series' insight 11), and said later films would come back to it. This one draws both agents as ArchiMate 4 draws software that works beside people: an application component, and the map's agent assigned to a role, as a person would be. Version 4 makes it "much easier to model processes in which people and machines work together", such as "enterprise architects and AI assistants", with role and collaboration as generic concepts (Bizzdesign, *What really changes in the ArchiMate 4 specification*, checked 18 October 2026). The film doesn't say what changed; the series' learning modules do. The role, map drafter, and the agent's rights are invented, the IDs are illustrative, and Tomás, who answers for the agent, is its owner. Its rights sit on the map beside everyone else's. *Keeping it true*'s rule is "The agent recommends. People approve. Everything is versioned."; this series has said "confirm" since *Day one*, and here the agent drafts.

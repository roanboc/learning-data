*The script of *Growing people*, film 4 of 4 of *Ownership and performance*, the last series of the module *Leading data people*, and the module's last film, in six chapters, in English, drawn in the module's sunlit plaster. Written on 10 November 2026 from the module's brief for it (`leading-data-people/briefs.md`, section 13, in the author's words), with the module's `openings.md`, `palettes.md` and `numbers.md`. ## The promise

Every line passes two tests: someone new to leading people can follow it, and an experienced data manager finds nothing wrong in it. The film's idea is the everyday work of growing people: the person writes their own goals and the manager agrees them, with real work to grow on; feedback comes every week, small, about the work and what it changed, never about who the person is; the manager asks for feedback on herself, and acts on it; the credit goes to the team, in public; and a growth conversation looks forward, to a next step with more to own. The team's measures never become one person's targets, and feedback is never a grade once a year. As the module's last film, it closes on the module's promise: data work needs new skills, and leading the people who do it is a skill too.

**Takeaway:** "Their goals, weekly feedback about the work, the next step."

## The story in one paragraph

Japan, in 1872: the new government sets out to give every child a school. Teaching is new work, with new subjects and new methods, and few teachers are trained for it; by 1877 four schools in ten have a single teacher, and nearly one in three a single classroom. A teacher alone with a class can't see what each child has understood, nobody sees the lesson to help, and when a lesson loses the children they fall behind, and the teacher may never learn why. Teachers open their classrooms to each other: from the first year new teachers learn by watching lessons, groups form to learn together, a visitor watches the children (how they answer, whether they're interested, whether the questions suit them), and a reflection session follows. In the 1880s the groups begin lesson study, *jugyō kenkyū*: choose a goal, plan a lesson together, one teaches while the others watch the children, then improve it. It spreads across Japan, where today 99 in 100 primary teachers say they took part in the past year; after 1999 a book carries it to many countries, and in a trial in the United States, among 39 teams split at random, the students of those who used it for about three months, with research on how children learn fractions, understood fractions better than those of teams who trained another way. At the university, a year from now, Ivy Reyes joins the data team on Monday 1 March, in her first job, after a degree in statistics. Helena Novak, the data manager, doesn't hand her a list of goals: Ivy writes three of her own, agreed on Friday 5 March (the whole route of one question's data by Easter, a pull request merged by May, a data product of her own by mid-year), and Helena gives her real work to grow on, Graham Ellis's question about the teaching rooms. The first goal is met on Thursday 25 March. Every Tuesday they meet for thirty minutes, about her, never a status report. Helena's feedback has three parts, the situation, what Ivy did and its impact: on Tuesday 13 April about her review of the agent's draft, checked against Graham's own word for "used"; on Tuesday 25 May about a change she kept to herself for four days, which left Jun one afternoon to review it. Helena asks for feedback on herself, and in June Ivy tells her that answering her questions at once stops her looking; from then on, Helena first asks what she has tried. On Thursday 24 June the University Council puts off the new teaching building, and at the next review Helena reads Graham's thank-you note to the team and says who did each part. From Thursday 1 July Ivy owns *Teaching space use*; on Tuesday 20 July, all three goals met, her mid-year conversation looks forward; and on Tuesday 14 December, nearly six months an owner, her next goal is one she chose: to coach the next graduate, in 2028.

## The reference, and practice

The brief names three references, each named once; the film teaches what they share: feedback helps when it keeps attention on the work and what it changed, and harms when it turns to the person. The opening teaches the same from the other side: in lesson study the visitors watch the children's learning, not the teacher, and the lesson is planned together, so the talk afterwards is about the lesson.

**Where the film adopts, extends or differs.** It adopts Kluger and DeNisi's finding (feedback can harm, and helps least when it turns to the person), the three parts of situation, behaviour and impact, and Scott's two halves of hard feedback, without their theory, models or quadrants. It extends them from the brief's practice: feedback is weekly and small, in the person's own half hour, while the work is fresh, never saved for a review once a year; goals are the person's own, agreed with the manager, and the team's measures never become them; feedback goes both ways, and the manager acts on what she hears; credit goes to the team, by name, in public; and a growth conversation looks forward, to a next step the person chose. It differs from the habit of the yearly appraisal: there's no grade and no rating against colleagues, and the mid-year conversation looks forward more than it scores.

**Practice.** A practitioner will recognise the graduate who keeps a change to herself to make it perfect, and the reviewer left one afternoon; praise that feels kind and teaches nothing; a manager who answers every question at once, so the person stops looking; a thank-you note addressed to the manager for the team's work; and a first data product that a new analyst grows into owning.

## Rigour to check before recording

Checked on 10 November 2026; the sources are listed below.

- **Japan, 1872.** The Fundamental Code of Education (*Gakusei*) of 1872 laid the foundations for new schools, new teaching methods and the training of teachers; the government founded the first normal schools and translated the first teaching manuals in "the new 'science' of education" (Sarkar Arani, Fukaya and Lassegard, 2010, quoting Lincicome, 1995). Its plan was universal education (Britannica, *Gakusei*): "sets out to give every child a school". Enrolment took decades to follow; the film says only what the government set out to do.
- **1877.** "Out of a total number of 19,345 schools, 8,332 (43 percent) employed one teacher"; "6,170 schools were equipped with only a single classroom" (Sarkar Arani et al., 2010, from Inagaki, 1966): four in ten, and nearly one in three (32%).
- **Classrooms opened.** Classroom visits "occurred on a regular basis from 1872 as part of teacher training for novice teachers"; "visiting teachers examined the responses and behaviour of pupils to determine the degree of their interest in the class, and the suitability of the questions asked"; reflection sessions (*hanseikai*); regional learning groups in the first decades of Meiji; and in the 1880s, "lesson study groups" (*jugyō kenkyūkai*) (Sarkar Arani et al., 2010). The cycle (a goal, a lesson planned together, taught while the others watch the children, improved for next time) is lesson study as Lewis and Hurd describe it (as Fujii, 2014, gives it); the film says it in the present tense.
- **Today.** "99% of elementary and 95% of secondary Japanese teachers report being involved in lesson study within the prior year" (Lewis, 2013, p. 3, as Duez, 2018, gives it). The film says "99 in 100 primary teachers", the most recent figure the sources give, as "today".
- **1999.** *The Teaching Gap* (James Stigler and James Hiebert, 1999) began lesson study's spread abroad (Duez, 2018). The film says "a book about it", without its title.
- **The trial.** Lewis and Perry (2017): 39 teams of educators across the United States, assigned at random to lesson study supported by a fractions resource kit (research on how children learn fractions) or to one of two control conditions; a cycle took about 91 days on average; the students of the lesson study teams gained more in fractions knowledge (effect size 0.49, as Duez, 2018, reports it). The film says "some" used it, never how many, and "teams who trained another way" for both controls; its picture draws the 39 teams in ink, with no count for either group.
- **The references.** Kluger and DeNisi, 1996: 607 effects, 23,663 observations; feedback improved performance on average (d = 0.41), but over a third of the interventions made it worse, and the effect weakened as attention moved from the task towards the self. The Center for Creative Leadership's situation, behaviour, impact. Kim Scott, *Radical Candor* (2017): care personally, challenge directly. Each is named once, with its year where the voice gives one.
- **The university's numbers.** The module's `numbers.md`: Ivy joins on Monday 1 March 2027, her first job, after a degree in statistics; her first goals, written in her first week and agreed on Friday 5 March; the first met on Thursday 25 March, the day before Good Friday (Easter Sunday 2027 is 28 March), with her one page; Wednesday 7 April, the agent's draft that took a booked hour for a used one, where Graham's "used" means people in the room; the one-to-one, 30 minutes every week, Ivy's on Tuesdays; Thursday 24 June, the University Council defers the building; *Teaching space use*, hers from Thursday 1 July, refreshed every Monday by 07:00, counts per room per hour, never a person; her mid-year conversation on Tuesday 20 July; her end of year on Tuesday 14 December.

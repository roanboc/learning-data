# Small steps, measured · Measures that matter: script

*The script of *Measures that matter*, film 2 of 4 of *Small steps, measured*, the fourth series of the module *Data management*, in seven chapters, in English, drawn in the module's sunlit plaster. Written on 10 November 2026 from the module's brief and its shared numbers, with the leading data people module, which tells how the team reads the same page together.*

## The promise

Every line passes two tests: someone new to data work can follow it, and a data manager, a product owner or anyone who has run a balanced scorecard finds nothing wrong in it. Its idea: measure a data team by what people get, not by how busy it is. A few measures, in groups, each with a baseline taken before anything changes, one definition, a reader and the decision it serves; a quality measure beside every speed measure; a trend, never one number lit red or green; counted from the work, confirmed by the person served, and never a target for one person.

**Takeaway:** "Few measures, each with a baseline and a decision."

## The story in one paragraph

In New York in 1952, about one baby in fifty dies in its first four weeks, at least half for lack of oxygen, while everyone in the delivery room looks after the mother and each doctor judges the newborn by eye, in words that mean something different to each. Virginia Apgar, an anaesthetist, proposes a score anyone in the room can take, the same way every time: five signs at one minute, each zero, one or two. Tried on over a thousand babies and published in 1953, it makes a baby in trouble show at once, and lets doctors compare what they do; today most hospitals score every baby at one minute and again at five. In July 2027, Helena's data strategy promised the team's measures on one page. She starts from what people get and from the decisions the measures will serve: eight measures in three groups, delivery, data management and value, as Kaplan and Norton's balanced scorecard groups its measures. Each has a baseline (41 working days from ask to first use, in February), one definition in the catalog's glossary, a reader and a decision, and sent back sits beside days to first use. The page shows lines, not lit numbers: on 12 August promises met stand at 82 percent, but nine pieces of work are open against a limit of six, and the team finishes before it starts. As in the delivery room, where doctors scored their own babies higher, every number is counted from the work, first use is confirmed by the person who asked, and every measure is the team's. By December: 14 days, half as much sent back, 86 percent of promises met, 910 people a month.

## The reference, and practice

The module's spine holds (TOGAF's method, ArchiMate's words; `../../a-plan-in-ninety-days/proposal.md`). This film names, once each:

| Body of knowledge | What the film takes | Where |
|---|---|---|
| The balanced scorecard (Robert Kaplan and David Norton, *Harvard Business Review*, 1992) | A few measures in groups, read together, so a gain in one place can't hide a loss in another. Its four perspectives (financial, customer, internal process, learning and growth) become three groups one data team can own: delivery, data management and value | *Three groups, few measures*: "In 1992, Robert Kaplan and David Norton called it a balanced scorecard:" |
| Goodhart's law (Charles Goodhart, 1975; the common phrasing is Marilyn Strathern's, 1997) | When a measure becomes a target, it stops being a good measure: so the numbers are counted from the work, first use is confirmed by the person who asked, and no measure is one person's | *For the team, never a target*: "Goodhart's law, from 1975, as it's often put" |
| Data governance's measures, from the work (*Teachers of data*) | Measured from each night's checks and from people, not reported by the people measured | *Busy isn't better*: "Data governance already measures itself this way, from the work, as Teachers of data showed." |
| The leading data people module | How the team reads the page together, every second Thursday, and what it did on 12 August; its film isn't on main yet, so the film names the module | *For the team, never a target*: "How the team reads its measures together is told in the leading data people module." |

Where the film differs: the balanced scorecard is a whole organisation's tool for managing its strategy, with four perspectives and a map of how they lead to each other. The film keeps its core (few measures, in groups, balanced, tied to what the organisation is for) and shrinks it to one team's page in three groups. It adds what people who run measures in practice insist on, none of which the scorecard spells out: a baseline before anything changes, one definition each in the glossary, a reader and a decision for every measure, a quality measure beside every speed measure, and a trend rather than a number lit red or green. Goodhart wrote about monetary policy; the film takes only the phrasing everyone knows, and says it's "as it's often put".

Practice, from the module's brief and the data teams it draws on: start from the decisions a measure will serve, and ask of each one what you'll do differently when it moves, and who decides; keep the page to a few measures, and let the rest be counted off it; take the baseline before you change anything; write one definition, in the glossary, before the first number; pair speed with quality, since speed alone rewards rework; read the trend, not the month; count from the work, never from what the people measured report; and never turn a measure into a person's target. Every one of these is said in *On Monday*.

## Script

### 1 · The first minute · 0:00–2:28
New York, 1952: the delivery room, the baby in its cot; Dr Virginia Apgar, there for the mothers. A sheet of the deaths of the first weeks, and of the first day, hardly fallen in twenty years; at least half from a lack of oxygen. Everyone looks after the mother; the junior doctor alone at the cot. Each doctor's own words for how the baby is, each meaning something different; a baby in trouble missed. Something has to change. 1952: Apgar's card, five signs at one minute, each scored 0, 1 or 2; ten, a baby doing well; tried on 1,021 babies, published in 1953. A low score seen at once by everyone in the room; two bars after a caesarean, five and eight; many hospitals by the early 1960s, most today, at one minute and again at five; newborn deaths, about 20 in a thousand in 1950, under four by 2016, for many reasons. Then the team's room: busy every day, and a few measures, taken the same way every month, can show whether people get what they need. The title, with the module's mark.

### 2 · Busy isn't better · 2:28–3:13
July 2027: the strategy promised the team's measures on one page. Three counts that only go up (requests closed, hours worked, reports built) while the people who asked are still waiting. Helena starts from what people get, and from the decisions the measures will serve: one question for every measure, "when it moves, what will we do differently, and who decides?" Governance already measures itself from the work, as *Teachers of data* showed.

### 3 · Three groups, few measures · 3:13–4:05
The balanced scorecard, named once: a few measures, in groups, so a gain in one place can't hide a loss in another. Helena's page on the screen, its three groups coming in as they're named: delivery (days from ask to first use, sent back, load), data management (products with an owner, and their promise met), value (benefits against each case, decisions informed, people using the products each month, and two questions, twice a year). Eight measures; Apgar needed five signs. Anything else can still be counted, off the page.

### 4 · A baseline and a decision · 4:05–5:07
The four things each measure gets. Days from ask to first use: its baseline from February, before anything changed, 41 working days; its one definition in the catalog's glossary; its readers, the team every second Thursday and the data council every month; its decision, where to take the waiting out of the work. Beside it, sent back, one piece in eight in July: without it, speed could hide rework. The page itself, a small governed product that Helena owns.

### 5 · The trend, not one number · 5:07–6:14
June: 24 days. Each measure a line across the months, its baseline beneath it, never one number lit red or green. Thursday 12 August: promises met at 82 percent, up from 58 in March; beside it, load at nine against a limit of six, its line amber above the limit; the team finishes before it starts, and the load is six by mid-September. Value's lines: the University Council's decision of 24 June, from the rooms' data; the old warehouse's saving, 270,000 Australian dollars a year from January, owned by the director of digital services.

### 6 · For the team, never a target · 6:14–7:04
Goodhart's law, as it's often put. Back in the delivery room: doctors scored their own babies several points higher, so someone else took the score. Helena's measures, counted from the tracker and the platform's own logs; first use confirmed by the person who asked, never by the person who built it. The team of four, side by side: every measure the team's, never one person's number, never a target for anyone. How the team reads its measures together is the leading data people module's.

### 7 · On Monday · 7:04–8:06
Back in New York: five signs, taken the same way, by someone else, and every baby seen in its first minute. December on the page: 14 days, not 41; half as much sent back; promises met at 86 percent; 910 people a month; the old warehouse retired on 17 December, its saving starting in January. On Monday: list the decisions your measures will serve; keep them few, in groups; take a baseline before you change anything, and write one definition for each; put a quality measure beside every speed measure, and show the trend; keep every measure for the team. The end card: "Few measures, each with a baseline and a decision."

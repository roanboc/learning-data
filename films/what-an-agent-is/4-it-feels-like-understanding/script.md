## The promise

Every line passes two tests: someone who has never used an AI agent can follow it, and a psychologist who studies trust in automation, a spacecraft navigator, or a data engineer who works with agents every day finds nothing wrong in it. *A model, reasoning, hands and a loop* showed what an agent is made of. This film shows what working with one does to us. A fluent answer, a green tick and a long run of success give us the feeling that we understand what happened, and that we're safe, while we couldn't say what it read, counted or checked, not even roughly; so we stop checking. And a person and an agent can each be sure they understand the other while meaning different things. Three habits turn the feeling into knowing: say what you mean and hear it said back; check against something the agent didn't make, and read what its hands did; write every doubt down with an owner, and let a person decide. **Feeling sure is not knowing.**

## The story in one paragraph

In December 1998, NASA sent the Mars Climate Orbiter to study the weather on Mars. Small thruster firings pushed it slightly off course on the way, and the builders' software in Colorado worked out each push and sent the numbers to NASA's navigators in California, who steer the craft. The teams had agreed on newton-seconds; the file gave pound-seconds, each about four and a half times bigger, so every push was counted at less than a quarter of its size. Each team was sure the numbers meant the same to both. From April the navigators saw that something didn't fit and raised it in emails, but after thirty years of success orbiting Mars felt routine, and the doubt was never settled. On 23 September 1999 the orbiter passed behind Mars 170 kilometres lower than planned and was never heard from again. On the next orbiter, Mars Odyssey, the team wrote both units on every hand-off, had every critical number checked independently and added a second way to find the craft; it was still at work twenty years later. Today, at the university, Anil Kurian plans the Faculty of Health's student advisers, one for every 240 students, and asks Jun how many students the faculty has. Jun asks the agent: in seconds, 2,160 students, 63 more than last year, its check passed, evidence attached. It feels like understanding, but Jun couldn't say, not even roughly, what it read or counted. The agent had been right for months, so when Anil wrote that the advisers' lists looked long, they both let it go. The faculty planned nine advisers and needed twelve, and 720 students never had their call. The lists held 2,880 people. The agent had counted student load, the table the university reports to the government, where two half-time students count as one: full-time equivalents, not people. Jun meant people, the agent took the table's meaning, and neither asked; its check compared its total with that same table, and the record of what its hands did showed it all along. So the team changes three things, as Odyssey's did: every count names its unit and the agent reads the question back; the answer is checked against the faculty's own list, and Jun reads what its hands did; every doubt goes in the team's log with an owner, and a person decides. This term the agent reads it back, counts 2,880, the faculty's list agrees, and every student gets their call.

### 5 · One word, two meanings · 4:36–5:35

**Narration.** At the end of the term, Anil counted the advisers' lists: 2,880 students. The agent had counted from student load, the table the university reports to the government. There, two half-time students count as one, and half the faculty studies half-time. So the table said 2,160: full-time equivalents, not people. Jun meant people; the agent took the table's meaning. ## Labs (candidates)

The module's labs come later, as one set (the briefs); these are candidates for it.

1. **Explain it.** Take a number an agent gave you at work. Before you open its evidence, write in three lines what it read, what it counted and what it checked. Then open the record of what its hands did, and compare.
2. **Say it, hear it back.** Write a question for an agent with its unit in it ("people, each counted once"), and an instruction to read the question back and wait for a yes before it counts. Try it with a word that has two meanings in your own data.
3. **Check against something else.** For one count you report, find a second source the agent didn't make. Write the plain rule that compares them, and who looks when they don't agree.

## Rigour

Checked on 3 November 2026, against the sources listed below, before the voice was recorded.

- **The opening, the mission.** NASA launched the Mars Climate Orbiter on 11 December 1998; its objective was "to orbit Mars as the first interplanetary weather satellite" and to relay for the Mars Polar Lander (Phase I report, p. 6). The narration keeps the weather: "to study the weather on Mars", "the first weather satellite at another planet". "Years of work": the mission was identified in 1995 (Phase I, p. 9) and lost in 1999.
- **The opening, the pushes.** During the nine-month journey, thrusters fired to unload the reaction wheels (Angular Momentum Desaturation, AMD), "10-14 times more often than was expected", because the orbiter's single solar array was lopsided (Phase I, p. 6). The wheels keep the craft pointing; the firings let them keep doing it, so "small thrusters kept it pointing the right way" is the plain version, and the firings perturbed the path. The ground software "Small Forces" (SM_FORCES) worked out each firing's impulse and wrote it to the AMD file, which the navigators used (Phase I, pp. 6 and 16). The builders were the prime contractor's team in Denver, Colorado (Phase I, p. 9); the film says "the builders", in Colorado, and names no company (AGENTS.md, *A fictional world*: no real company as the subject). The navigators were the operations navigation team at JPL, Pasadena, California.
- **The opening, the units.** The interface specification required newton-seconds; the file gave pound-seconds; "the trajectory modelers assumed the data was provided in metric units per the requirements" (Phase I, p. 6); the effect was underestimated by a factor of 4.45, since 1 pound of force is 4.45 newtons (Phase I, pp. 13 and 16). So each push counted at 1/4.45, about 22%: "less than a quarter of its size". "Each team was sure the numbers meant the same to both" is the brief's reading (the plan: "two teams each thought the other used the same units"); the report says the navigators assumed metric, and nothing in it says the builders knew of the difference.
- **The opening, the doubt.** "Throughout spring and summer of 1999, concerns existed at the working level"; discrepancies were "only informally reported" and "not resolved" (Phase I, p. 13); from April 1999 "it became apparent that there was some type of mismodeling" (p. 18); "the team relied on e-mail to solve problems, instead of formal problem resolution processes" (p. 21). "Orbiting Mars is routine": "JPL's navigation of interplanetary spacecraft has worked well for 30 years ... a widespread perception that 'Orbiting Mars is routine'" (Phase I, p. 28, observation 8); the Phase II report files it under overconfidence. The envelopes are drawn for April to July, months of "spring and summer"; the report gives no count of emails.
- **The opening, the loss.** The engine burn for orbit began on 23 September 1999; the carrier signal was last seen at about 09:04:52 UTC, as the craft entered occultation behind Mars, and never reacquired (Phase I, p. 6). "At the time of Mars insertion, the spacecraft trajectory was approximately 170 kilometers lower than planned" (p. 7): the planned first periapsis was 226 km; the estimate after the fact, 57 km (pp. 13 and 14). The narration says only "170 kilometres lower than planned" and "never heard from again"; the report says it was destroyed in the atmosphere or left Mars.
- **The opening, the fix.** The board: "mistakes occur on spacecraft projects. However, sufficient processes are usually in place on projects to catch these mistakes before they become critical to mission success" (Phase I, p. 7), said in chapter 6 as "mistakes happen, and checks are there to catch them". Odyssey's team, after the losses of 1999, among its risk reductions: "Identified parameters critical to mission success and did an independent verification of these parameters"; "Listed both imperial and metric units on documentation for hand-off between systems and subsystems"; "Added delta differential one-way range measurements, called 'delta DOR,' that provide an independent measurement of the orbiter location relative to Mars" (Arrival press kit, October 2001, p. 13, *Lessons Learned*). "A second way to find the craft" is delta DOR in plain words. Odyssey, launched on 7 April 2001, was the next NASA orbiter sent to Mars after the Climate Orbiter.
- **The opening, the benefit.** Odyssey's orbit insertion burn: 24 October 2001 UT, the evening of 23 October in California (press kit, p. 6); the narration says "In October 2001". In April 2021, JPL called it "the oldest spacecraft still working at the Red Planet" and the "longest-lived spacecraft at the Red Planet", with over 18,000 relay sessions (JPL, 7 April 2021); in 2014, "the longest-working spacecraft ever sent to Mars" (JPL, 12 February 2014). The narration says "twenty years later it was still at work, the longest-lived spacecraft at Mars", true in 2021, and claims nothing about its state in 2026.
- **The opening, drawn.** The orbiter's single solar wing is drawn because it's the reason the thrusters fired so often; no logo, flag or mission patch; the people are composites in 1999 clothes; no real person is drawn or named. The board's member list is not used.
- **A fluent answer.** The agent and its parts are *A model, reasoning, hands and a loop*'s: read-only tool cards, a check, an answer with evidence (the series' rule: read-only when reading is enough). The 2025 study: Steyvers and colleagues, *Nature Machine Intelligence*: "longer explanations increased user confidence, even when the extra length did not improve answer accuracy", and users "overestimate the accuracy of LLM responses when provided with default explanations". The narration's "more confident in an AI's answers" is the study's "user confidence" in the model's answers. The two bars are drawn without numbers: the study's own figures are not shown.
- **Explain it.** Rozenblit and Keil, *Cognitive Science*, 2002: people rated their understanding of devices (among them a zipper, a flush toilet, a cylinder lock and a piano key) on a 7-point scale, then wrote step-by-step explanations, then rated again, and their ratings dropped; Study 1 had 16 Yale graduate students and Study 2, 33 Yale undergraduates; "The illusion is far stronger for explanatory knowledge than many other kinds of knowledge, such as that for facts, procedures or narratives." The narration says "they rated themselves lower" (on average), not "everyone". The film's own seven dots copy the study's 7-point scale; the drop from five to three is the film's, to show the direction.
- **Right every time.** Parasuraman and Manzey, *Human Factors*, 2010, reviewing the research: complacency "occurs under conditions of multiple-task load" and "is found in both naive and expert participants and cannot be overcome with simple practice"; automation bias "occurs in both naive and expert participants, cannot be prevented by training or instructions, and can affect decision making in individuals as well as in teams". "When an aid has been right for a long time" is the 1993 study the review builds on: Parasuraman, Molloy and Singh found that people caught an automation's failures far less often when it was constantly reliable than when its reliability varied, and only when they had other tasks; with monitoring as their only task, detection stayed good (NTRS record of the paper). So the narration's "above all when they're busy" and "experts too, and training alone doesn't prevent it" each have their source.
- **One word, two meanings.** "Student load" counts full-time equivalents, as universities report load to governments (the series' world: the census-day number the university reports to the government); the film's numbers are invented and add up, and the faculty's people are the series' census-day count for Health, 2,880 (the faculties of *Many agents, and what emerges*, of the series' 18,412 students): 1,440 full-time + 1,440 half-time = 2,880 people = 2,160 full-time equivalents; 2,160 / 240 = 9 advisers; 2,880 / 240 = 12; 2,880 − 9 × 240 = 720 students without a call; last year's 2,097 + 63 = 2,160. Grounding: Clark and Brennan, 1991, *Grounding in communication*: people work together to establish that what's said is understood, by acknowledgements, repetitions and questions. The 2024 study: Shaikh and colleagues, NAACL 2024, *Grounding gaps in language model generations*: language models "generate language with less conversational grounding, instead generating text that appears to simply presume common ground", and training on preference data reduces their grounding acts. The narration's "presume we already agree" is that line in plain words.
- **What its hands did, not what it says.** Turpin and colleagues, NeurIPS 2023: a model's step-by-step explanation "can be plausible yet misleading", leaving out what drove its answer; it's the reason the film's second change says "read what its hands did, not only what it says", and the record card is the tools' log, not the agent's account. The narration doesn't cite it by name, to keep the chapter to one idea.
- **The module's rules.** The read-back and the unit beside every count are context, written where the agent reads; the catalog's "Census day" card is the series' census-day count, people, each counted once. The plain comparison is a rule run every time, deterministic first, not another agent; "a person decides; the agent's tick is only for reference": agents are never approvers. Nothing reaches production in the film. The team's log is the team's shared memory, outside a single chat.
- **People.** Anil Kurian plans the Faculty of Health's places and keeps its call list for its student advisers (the governance films' shared facts); here he plans its advisers. Jun, in the data team, built the data project; his initials, "JP", are the series' Jun Park. No student, adviser or 1999 person is named.

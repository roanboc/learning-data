## The promise

Every line passes two tests: anyone who has looked for the meaning of a word at work and found three can follow it, and an experienced data governance lead finds nothing wrong in it. Most organisations don't have one business glossary. They have several, each made for a good reason by one team: siloed, disagreeing, hidden from everyone else, or bought and never opened. Several glossaries are the same as none, because nobody knows which to trust, so everyone keeps their own. The fix is to keep one, chosen because it can be trusted and every tool can read it, not because it's the biggest: each term with a definition, an owner, a status and the date it last changed. The others are retired, or made to read from it, and every copy is made from it one way, says where its words came from, and is checked. A business glossary isn't a data dictionary or a catalog. When a word changes, a change notice lists what the new words touch and who owns each part, and the word's owner signs it; a number in a meaning is a rule too, kept in one place, as a business rule every report reads. And the glossary keeps what people agreed; it doesn't make the agreement. Then reports agree, a change reaches everyone it touches, and people can trust the numbers. **Many glossaries are no glossary: keep one, and sync every tool from it.**

## The story in one paragraph

In 221 BC, the king of Qin conquered the last of the warring states and became China's first emperor. But each old state had its own script, its own measures and its own cart widths: a measure of grain held more in one state than in the next, so taxes and prices were in doubt. The empire needed the same script, measures and cart widths everywhere, so it set them by law. Now a measure of grain had to hold the same everywhere, so taxes and prices could be trusted. The empire even decided which words its officials used: "the king's order" became "the emperor's edict". Far to the south, the county office of Qianling kept a list on a wooden board: each old word, and the new word to use instead. In 2002, archaeologists found the board in an old well at Liye, with thousands of the county's own records, and those records use the new words almost every time, so a word meant the same in the county as in the capital. The board was a glossary, kept where the work was done, and used. A university has the same problem: one word, defined differently in many places, and it needs one glossary that everyone trusts, and every tool reading from it. Today, Mei, who owns what *award* means, has found it defined in five places (the last line of *Who decides*). Yusuf finds five glossaries: the business glossary on the platform, with the words Mei approved, which the data team copies from; Planning's spreadsheet; an appendix to the registrar's policy; the faculties' dashboards, each with its own description; and a glossary tool bought in 2019, with 600 terms. Between them, three different awards. Each was made for a good reason; two can only be found by the people who made them; the biggest was last opened fourteen months ago; and when *award* changed on Monday, only one of the five changed. Yusuf brings the terms' owners together, and they keep one glossary: not the biggest, but the one they can trust and every tool can read, the glossary on the platform, beside the data: like the Qin board, it sits where the work is done, so it gets used. The others are retired, or made to read from it: the 2019 tool's terms with an owner move across and the rest are archived; Planning's spreadsheet links to each term; the registrar's policy says "as defined in the business glossary"; the faculties' dashboards show the glossary's words, read from the platform. The data team's copy already worked this way (*Written once*): it says where its words came from, and it's checked against the glossary every night. On Monday night the check found the change, and sent a change notice: like the Qin board, it shows the old words and the new ones, and it lists what the new words touch and who owns each part; Mei signed it, and for once saw where her words are used; Jun saw a number in the words, and it was kept in one place, as a business rule every report reads. Monday's words were argued out by Mei and the faculties in a workshop: the glossary keeps the agreement, it doesn't make it. The people who kept the other glossaries are thanked, and their best definitions move into the one that stays. Policy 12 said reports count by the glossary's definitions: now each word has one home, and every tool reads from it. Like the Qin board, it's used: reports agree, a change reaches everyone it touches, and people can trust the numbers.

## Rigour to check before recording

Checked on 21 October 2026, and on 27 October 2026 for the series' first pack; the sources are listed below. The files and tools the first pack leaves off the screen are named here, for *Meaning on the platform*.

- **221 BC.** The *Shiji* (Sima Qian, chapter 6, the Basic Annals of the First Emperor of Qin), under the year 221 BC: 一法度衡石丈尺。車同軌，書同文字。 Zheng (2024) translates: "He standardised the measurements of weight, capacity, and length. Carts all had the same axle width, and writings all had the same script." The same passage says he changed the name of the people from *min* to *qianshou*, "the black-headed". "The last of his rivals" is Qi, conquered in 221 BC; the title the king took was *huangdi*, emperor. "Each old state had its own script, its own measures and its own cart widths" is what the unification replaced; Zheng notes that standardising had begun before 221 BC, within the old states (Chu's manuscripts were already fairly uniform), so the film says the states had their own ways, not that nothing was shared. The ruts the carts roll in are the usual reason given for one axle width; the narration doesn't claim it.
- **"Taxes and prices were in doubt."** The First Emperor's edict of 221 BC, cast or stamped on the empire's weights and measures (bronze weights, bronze edict plates, and pottery measures such as the round one with its 40-character edict in the National Museum of China, the measure the seller has), orders that the measures "not one, and in doubt" be made clear and one: 法度量則不壹歉疑者，皆明壹之 (zdic, 秦詔版; Li 2017: the edict regulated lengths, volumes and weights). "In doubt" is the edict's own word, 疑. That a measure of grain held more in one old state than in the next is the general case, not a number: which of the seller's two measures is the bigger is the picture's choice. Land tax was paid from the harvest, in grain, and assessed by officials (Yang Zhenhong's study of Qin and Han tax slips, as reported by *Archaeology*, 2021), so a measure that differed changed the tax. "The empire needed the same script, measures and cart widths everywhere, so it set them by law" is the edict's reason in plain words, with the *Shiji*'s three (above): the edict is a law, issued the year the empire was made one. "So a word meant the same in the county as in the capital" is what Zheng's counts show the county did (below).
- **"Now a measure of grain had to hold the same everywhere, so taxes and prices could be trusted."** What got better is the edict's own aim, in plain words: the measures that were "not one, and in doubt" were all to be made clear and one, 皆明壹之 (zdic, 秦詔版), and the empire made its standard weights and measures carrying the edict, such as the round pottery measure the seller has (Li 2017). "Had to" says what the law required, not that every measure in the empire was exact; "could be trusted" is the doubt the edict names, taken away.
- **"Which words its officials used."** Liye 8-461, the "wooden tablet of nomenclature changes" (*gengming fang* 更名方). Zheng (2024): found in the excavation begun in 2002 at Liye, Longshan county, Hunan, the site of a Qin county town, among more than 38,000 slips and tablets, most from a disused well; 27.4 by 12.5 by 0.6 cm, pieced together from six fragments, the top right missing; two rows of entries; 54 entries identified; 35 of them change proper names, titles and appellations, 14 set which graph writes which meaning, 2 set graphic forms, and 3 avoid taboo characters. The Liye documents date from 222 to 210 BC. Staack (2023): the well, J1, had 18 layers, and the documents are thought to come from the archives of Qin's Qianling county. The two rules on the card are entries BVI, 以王令曰以皇帝詔 ("'By the royal command' is now changed to be 'by the imperial instruction'", Zheng's translation; the film says, in plain words, that "the king's order" became "the emperor's edict", and the card on the board says the same), and BXXI, 王獵曰皇帝獵 ("the royal hunting excursion" becomes "the imperial hunting excursion"; the film says "the king's hunt" and "the emperor's hunt"); the Hunan Provincial Institute of Cultural Relics and Archaeology's article quotes both, and 王犬曰皇帝犬, the king's dogs becoming the emperor's.
- **"A county office kept a list."** What the board was is argued: Hu Pingsheng (2009) reads it as a compilation of standards for the county's clerks and scribes, many of whom had grown up in a Chu-influenced area and didn't know Qin's writing and terms; Barbieri-Low (2022) as an aide-mémoire for a working scribe, compiled from imperial instructions and ordinances (both as cited by Zheng). The film says "a county office kept a list on a wooden board", which fits both, and doesn't say it was sent from the capital. "Archaeologists found the board in an old well, with thousands of the county's own records": the board was among the more than 38,000 slips and tablets of the excavation begun in 2002, most from the disused well J1 (Zheng), the archives of Qianling county (Staack); "thousands" is the safe word, as some are blank or broken. That the board was found with the records is why the find matters: the county's own writing can be checked against it.
- **"Almost every time."** Zheng's counts in the Liye documents published so far (volumes 1 and 2): the new word for the people, *qianshou*, 13 and 21 times; the old words, *min* and *baixing*, 0 and 1 (Table 4); and the new form of 皇 in 11 of 12 cases. His conclusion: "a remarkable degree of adherence to the gengming fang guidelines". The tallies on screen are 34 and 1. The word for the people is not on the board: Zheng lists it among the standardised terms "absent from the gengming fang", ordered in 221 BC (the *Shiji*), so the film says the records use "the new words", not the board's, and the tally card says "the new word for 'the people'". The board's own rules were followed too: its rule that 吏 writes "official" and 事 writes "work" (entry AVII) holds "in every instance" in the Liye and Yuelu texts, and its new form of 皇 in 11 of 12 cases, the twelfth being the board itself, where the old form is glossed (Zheng, re-read 27 October 2026). After the Qin fell, the early Han texts from Zhangjiashan went back to *min* (34 times): the film stops at Qin.

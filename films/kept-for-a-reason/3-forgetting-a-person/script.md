# Kept for a reason · Forgetting a person: script

*The script of* Forgetting a person*, Film 3 of 5 of* Kept for a reason*, the third series of the module* Data protection and privacy*: 9 minutes 12 seconds, in eight chapters, in English, in the module's Night glass look. Written on 5 November 2026 from the module's briefs, on stated assumptions while the author was away (his 11:53 UTC message; each assumption is a row of the film's [*Decided* table](decided.md), for his review). It's an ideas film: it names no product, cloud or tool; *A person asks* shows the same request in Databricks, dbt and GitHub, and *Deleted means deleted* the platform's deletes.*

## The promise

Every line passes two tests: someone who has never thought about their own data can follow it, and a privacy officer, a data protection lawyer and a records manager each find nothing wrong in it. The film teaches what most privacy laws share, never one country's law: people can ask to see the data held about them, to correct it, to have it erased and to stop a use of it; to answer, an organisation must find every place a person's data lives, by every number and form of their name and through lineage, before anyone asks; and what can't be erased is said, with its reason and its end date. The takeaway: a person can ask; you must be able to answer.

## The story in one paragraph

Barcelona, 1998: by order of Spain's labour ministry, a newspaper prints a notice that a lawyer's property is to be auctioned to pay his social security debts. He settles the matter. Years later the newspaper's archive goes online, and by 2009 a search for his name, Mario Costeja González, shows links to the notice, as if it were news. The newspaper won't remove a page it printed lawfully. In 2010 he complains to Spain's data protection agency, which lets the newspaper keep its page but tells the search engine to remove the links; the search engine goes to court, and on 13 May 2014 the European Union's Court of Justice rules that even true, lawful information can in time become wrong to list under a person's name, unless the public has a strong reason to find it. The page stays; the link from his name goes; and anyone in Europe can now ask a search engine directly: over two million requests since, for eight and a half million links, each reviewed by a person, a little over half removed, and each refusal given a reason. At the university, Linh Nguyen's tutoring contract ends on 20 November 2026, and four days later she asks what the university holds about her, and to be taken off the tutors' mailing list. The university checks it's her with her signed-in student account and nothing more; finds every place her data lives, by her six numbers, the three ways her name is written and lineage, on a map it kept before anyone asked; sends a copy that says why each part is held, where it came from, who else gets it and until when, without other people's data; replaces her yes with her no on the person and keeps the no, so the list rebuilt each semester can't add her back; and says what stays, and why: pay records seven years after each contract ends, her degree and results for good, her research data as the project's rules require.

### 2 · Four sentences

**Narration.** Most privacy laws give people rights over the data others hold about them. Each fits in a sentence anyone can say. Show me what you hold about me. That's wrong. Correct it. You don't need that any more. Delete it. Stop using it for that. Since 2018, Europe's GDPR, the best-known privacy law, gives all four, each with its limits, and calls the third the right to be forgotten. Not every law gives all four, or in the same words. The university's own rules give all four to everyone it holds data about, with an answer within thirty days. Nobody has to say why they're asking. A person can ask. The university must be able to answer.

## Sound

A new palette ([`source/tools/score.py`](source/tools/score.py)), checked on 5 November 2026 against every `score.py` on main and on every branch, and every palettes list in the project's files (`palettes.md`, where this film's row is): **humming tops**, tin pump tops of the 1800s, each tuned to its own note, that hum only while they spin: pressed, a top spins up into its note; kept at speed, its note holds; left alone, it slows, its note sinks a little (about a semitone, as it nearly stops) and fades, and it falls still; and a **cello**, bowed softly and close, plain, no vibrato, low and middle, its top rolled off: the person who asks (Costeja, then Linh), and the answer, a phrase that falls to rest. Every chord is tuned pure, in just intonation from F: D minor for the problem, F major for the answer, B flat major as the ruling turns, C as the map waits for a request; no G minor, and no G with D or B flat, which aren't pure in this tuning. A top's note sinks as it slows, so a top is never left to slow over a held note with a harmonic in the range it sinks through (an F or an A under the F top): the two would beat.

- **Under the opening.** The cello's low open fifth, D and A, a breath, under "Barcelona, 1998". As the notice is printed, the notice's top (F) is pressed and hums through the lawyer's line, the cello's D minor under it; through the years it's left alone, slows and falls still. The search spins it again; it slows as the matter is said settled; a client and a neighbour spin it again, softer; he asks, the cello's D; at "It says no" it's let go, and there's silence under "He wants the old notice to stop following him, and nobody will let it". ## Rigour to check before recording

Checked on 5 November 2026. The source notes, with quotes, are in `sources.md` beside this script in the project's files.

- **The opening, Spain.** Two notices, of 19 January and 9 March 1998, in a daily newspaper published in Barcelona (La Vanguardia, not named in the film), "an announcement for a real-estate auction organised following attachment proceedings for the recovery of social security debts owed by Mr Costeja González" (Court of Justice, press release 70/14); printed by order of the Ministry of Labour and Social Affairs, "to give maximum publicity to the auction in order to secure as many bidders as possible" (the agency's resolution of 30 July 2010, quoted by ECIJA and Wikipedia; "Spain's labour ministry" in the film). "The attachment proceedings concerning him had been fully resolved for a number of years" (press release). A version of the edition was later put online (Wikipedia); the year isn't sourced, so the film says "years later". In 2009 he asked the newspaper, which refused, the notice having been published by the ministry's order (Columbia Global Freedom of Expression). His complaint to the Agencia Española de Protección de Datos, 5 March 2010; its resolution of 30 July 2010 rejected the complaint against the newspaper (lawfully published) and upheld it against Google Spain and Google Inc., asking them to withdraw the data from their index (press release). Their appeals to the Audiencia Nacional ("Spain's court"), which referred questions on 27 February 2012; the Advocate General's opinion, 25 June 2013; the judgment, 13 May 2014, Case C-131/12. The film's "even true information, lawfully published, can in time become wrong to show under a person's name" is the press release's "even initially lawful processing of accurate data may, in the course of time, become incompatible with the directive"; "no longer relevant, or excessive" shortens "inadequate, irrelevant or no longer relevant, or excessive in relation to the purposes for which they were processed and in the light of the time that has elapsed"; "unless the public has a strong reason to find it, such as a role in public life" is "unless there are particular reasons, such as the role played by the data subject in public life, justifying a preponderant interest of the public"; "sixteen years on, there's no such reason" is the judgment's paragraph 98 (sensitive for his private life, first published 16 years earlier; no particular reasons appear, though that was for Spain's court to establish); "anyone in Europe can now ask a search engine directly" is "the data subject may address such a request directly to the operator of the search engine".
- **Left out of the narration.** The property was a house he owned with another person, who isn't named anywhere in the film. In 2015 Spain's agency refused his request to remove links to comments about the case itself, as of public interest (resolution TD-00568-2015, by its file name; Stanford CIS blog): his own right had its edge too. The search engine's name, and the newspaper's, are left out under the rule against naming a real company as a subject.
- **The benefits.** "Over 12,000 people have lodged requests to be 'forgotten' by Google on the first day the search giant offered the service" (AFP, Sydney Morning Herald, 31 May 2014). The search engine's own transparency report, read 5 November 2026: since 29 May 2014, 2,218,678 requests to delist, for 8,544,810 URLs; of those reviewed, 4,045,040 delisted (54.2%) and 3,412,381 not (45.8%); each request has a manual review, and if a URL isn't delisted, the person gets "a brief explanation". The film: "over two million requests, for eight and a half million links", "a person reviews each one", "a little over half come down", "each refusal gives a reason".
- **The rights.** The principle most privacy laws grew from: the OECD's Guidelines of 1980, the Individual Participation Principle (paragraph 13): to know whether data is held, to have it communicated, to be given reasons if that's denied and to challenge it, and to have data erased, rectified, completed or amended. GDPR (Regulation (EU) 2016/679, applying from 25 May 2018), named once as the best-known privacy law: access (Article 15: the purposes, the recipients, the period, the source; a copy, which must not adversely affect others), rectification (16), erasure, titled "right to erasure ('right to be forgotten')" (17, with its exceptions in 17(3): a legal obligation; archiving, research and statistics; legal claims), objection (21, with no exception for direct marketing), and consent withdrawn as easily as given (7(3)); an answer within one month, extendable by two (12(3)). The film's four sentences are access, rectification, erasure and objection; "not every law gives all four, or in the same words" is true of the laws outside Europe. Left out: restriction (18), portability (20), decisions made by machines (22), fees and extensions. The university's own rules, all four rights to everyone it holds data about and an answer within 30 days, are invented (the series' decision).
- **Is it really her?** The EDPB's guidelines on the right of access (01/2022, version 2.1): "as a rule, the controller cannot request more personal data than is necessary to enable this authentication" (paragraph 65); controllers may use the authentication procedures that already exist between them and the person (66). The film's reason against a passport copy (one more thing to keep safe) is its own, in plain words. The request is logged on 24 November 2026 and due 24 December, as *A person asks* sets it (Request 2026-117, answered 7 December).
- **Every place she is.** The EDPB's guidelines: the controller searches "all IT systems and non-IT filing systems based on search criteria that mirrors the way in which the information is structured, for example name and customer number" (executive summary), extended to other identifiers such as user names, and to processors (paragraph 125). Her six numbers, and her name written three ways (NGUYỄN Thị Linh, Nguyen Thi Linh, L. Nguyen), are the master data films' and the example project's; her person record links them (*The same one, everywhere*). The map kept before anyone asks, with an owner for each place, and lineage, are common practice (the author wasn't asked; `assumptions.md`).
- **More than a copy.** Article 15(1) and (3); the EDPB: the controller renders "illegible those parts that may have negative effects for the rights and freedoms of others". The copy's rows follow the series' facts (*A reason to keep it*: contracts and hours on a contract, pay records and tax on a law, the mailing list on her consent of January 2023). HR deletes the 2023 form's marital status and photos on 6 November 2026, so neither is among her records.
- **Off the list.** Consent withdrawn as easily as given (Article 7(3)); the practice of keeping a "do not contact" record rather than deleting the person (the UK ICO's direct marketing guidance, read through search results only); the series' version (*A reason to keep it*): her yes on the person is replaced by her no, with its date, so the list, rebuilt each semester, can't add her back. Her row leaves the list on Wednesday 25 November (*A person asks*, *Deleted means deleted*).
- **What stays.** The university's retention schedule (invented; *How long, and then what* owns it): pay records (contracts, hours, pay) kept 7 years after each contract ends, by law; academic records (degrees, results) for good, a public task; research data as the project's rules require; signed by the records' owners (Ingrid Holm for HR's rows, Mei Tanaka for the registrar's) on 14 October 2026, kept by data governance (Yusuf Demir). Her last contract, T-26188, ended 20 November 2026, so its pay records are kept until 20 November 2033. "Where the answer is no, it says why, and how to challenge it": the OECD's paragraph 13(c).
- **Not shown.** One country's law; the deletes on the platform (*Deleted means deleted*); the request's proof (*A person asks*); any product.

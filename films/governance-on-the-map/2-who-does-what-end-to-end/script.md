## The promise

Every line passes two tests: anyone who has asked for something and never heard back can follow it, and an experienced enterprise architect, business analyst or data governance lead finds nothing wrong in it. In Madrid in 1892, Ricardo Palma, a writer from Lima, asked the Royal Spanish Academy to put more than three hundred words used every day in the Americas into its dictionary, and most were turned down, among them the word for "to budget". People looked in the dictionary to see if a word was right, so words left out counted as wrong, and the millions who used them had no way in; seventeen years later, Palma was still asking a friend what had become of "to budget". Since 1951, the academies of the Americas have worked as one association, soon joined by Spain's, and today they make the dictionary together: members propose words from where they live, a commission drafts each change, the Spanish Academy approves it, and every other academy gives its view before it goes in. Twenty-three academies now make one dictionary, free online, for more than six hundred million people; each year's update lists what changed, and "to budget" is in it. It's the same with data: when anyone can ask for a term, and every step has someone to do it, everyone can use the same words. At the university, three faculties count a completion three ways, so Health looks nine points behind, and Clara, in Planning, asks for the term to be defined. Noor and Yusuf follow her request from start to finish: ask, draft, check, decide, publish, follow. Each step is done by a role, and actors fill the roles: people, and one piece of software, the agent, with its rights written down. Each step reads something and writes something for the next, and each of those is information with a home of its own. The chart of who's responsible for what is now drawn from the map, so when someone is away, the chart follows. And the glossary is run as a product: an owner, its users, a promise, a list of what changed each week, and counts of who reads each term, so terms nobody reads are retired. Clara can see where her request is without chasing anyone, and a service followed end to end gives anyone a way in, a step for every role, and words everyone can use. **Anyone can ask. Everyone can see where it is.**

## The story in one paragraph

Madrid, 1892. Ricardo Palma, a writer from Lima, joins the meetings of the Royal Spanish Academy, which makes the dictionary of Spanish. He brings more than three hundred words used every day in the Americas, and asks for them to go in. Most are turned down, among them the word for "to budget". People look in the dictionary to see if a word is right, so words left out count as wrong, and the millions who use them have no way in. Seventeen years later, Palma is still asking a friend in Madrid what became of "to budget". Something has to change. In 1951, the academies of the Americas form an association, and Spain's academy soon joins their work; today they make the dictionary together: members of each academy propose words from where they live, a commission drafts each change, the Spanish Academy approves it, and every other academy gives its view before it goes in. Twenty-three academies make one dictionary, free online, for more than six hundred million people; each year's update lists what changed; and "to budget" is in it. It's the same with data: when anyone can ask for a term, and every step has someone to do it, everyone can use the same words. In January, at the university, Clara, in Planning, compares how many learners complete their awards, faculty by faculty, and finds three faculties counting a completion three ways: Business when the last mark is in, Science when the examiners confirm the marks, Health when the award is conferred, so the same learners look nine points less likely to complete in Health. Before, a question like this went round by email, and nobody decided; now there's a service for it, *Define or change a term*, decided within ten working days, and Clara asks. Noor and Yusuf follow her request from start to finish. Mei, the steward, drafts what completion means, with an example; the agent checks that the draft has everything a term needs, and lists what it touches; the registrar, the owner, decides; Mei writes it once, on Noor's map, which publishes it to the glossary; and every copy follows, one way. Each step is done by a role, and whoever fills it is an actor: a person, a team, or here, software, the agent, which may read and write its note, and may not decide or change a word. The academies work the same way: their members change, and the steps stay. Each step reads something and writes something for the next (the request, the draft, the agent's note, the decision, the term and its copies), and on the map each is information with a home of its own. The chart of who's responsible for what, half of it "to be confirmed" on Yusuf's first day, is now drawn from the map, so when Mei takes three weeks' leave in February, the chart follows, and nobody retypes it. The glossary is run as a product: its owner is Yusuf, for data governance; its users are people, reports, tables and agents; its promise is that every term has an owner, a definition, an example and a status, and a change is decided within ten working days; it lists what changed each week and counts who reads each term, so nine terms nobody read this year are retired, each with its owner's yes. All along, Clara could see where her request was; it was decided in eight working days, and the nine points between the faculties are gone. Palma had to ask a friend what became of his word; Clara can see where hers is.

**Practice.** What practitioners recognise: a definition asked for by email goes round until someone gives up; a request nobody can see gets chased, and the person who chases most gets served first; a responsibility chart typed by hand is out of date the week someone moves on; a steward named on a chart with no time to do it is a name, not a role; an agent that drafts and flags saves time, and one that decides isn't trusted; a glossary nobody owns fills with terms nobody reads; and a weekly list of what changed is how people learn to trust it.

**The author's view** (20 October 2026), which this film follows: governance designed "as a capability in enterprise architecture terms and the elements end to end (services, processes, business glossaries as business products, relevant DG roles and actors, information assets, models, etc)". And on 27 October 2026, on people: tools that give "guidance, visibility and feedback around the impact of their work"; here, Clara sees where her request is, and the owner sees what a change touches before deciding.

## The look

*Watercolour*, the series' look since 17 October 2026, shared with the series on master and reference data, so the two look like one university.

- **Pencil and wash.** Every place is painted on paper, and people are drawn as the master data films draw them, with a soft wash of their side behind each one.
- **Only the university's places,** all of them the series' shared places: the floor of offices behind glass, Planning's room, Mei's desk in the registrar's office, the data team's room, the registrar at her desk (drawn as in *Who decides*), Noor's room and her map, Yusuf's office. The film paints its own places for the opening: a meeting room of the Royal Spanish Academy, at night in 1892 and by day since, a schoolroom in Lima, a corridor and the academy's tall door, Palma's desk in the National Library of Peru in 1909, a meeting table in Mexico City in 1951, and today, two members' desks and a bus stop.
- **Madrid and Lima, painted the same way,** with the place and year in the corner: a long table under oil lamps, bookcases, members in dark frock coats with high collars; Palma in his fifties in 1892, with a full grey moustache, and at seventy-six in 1909. A warm umber wash behind each person of the past. Nothing on screen is in Spanish: words on the slips, in the notebook and in the dictionary are drawn as lines of writing too small to read, with an English tag where the story needs one, such as "to budget", and the corner names the place. No style, pattern or music borrowed from Spanish or Andean culture: the story is told in the series' own voice.
- **Colour carries meaning,** as in the series: gold is governance's decisions, and the business side; cyan the technical side, and dashed cyan the agent, which is software, never a face; green what runs in the work; grey what can go. Noor's map keeps its bands' colours from *The map before the data*.
- **No files, code or tool screens.** Where the work happens on a screen, the film shows the university's own pages: a request card, a draft, a note, a decision, the glossary's list of what changed, a page of one's requests. Applications are named for what they do: the glossary, the data team's copy, the dashboards, the assistant that answers questions in plain words.
- **One diagram-like picture: Noor's map,** where the service, its steps, roles and information go up as cards. Everything else is people doing things: a slip passed along a table, a request carried from desk to desk, a name turned on a card.

### 4 · Roles, not people · 3:13–3:54

**Narration.** Each step is done by a role: the one who asks, the steward, the reviewer, the owner. Whoever fills a role is an actor: a person, or a team. Actors come and go; roles stay. ## Rigour

Checked on 28 October 2026, before recording and again when the film was made; the sources are listed below.

- **Palma in Madrid, 1892.** Ricardo Palma (1833–1919), the writer of the *Tradiciones peruanas*, directed the National Library of Peru from 1883 to 1912. In 1892 he was named the official delegate to the gatherings in Spain for the fourth centenary of 1492; he used the journey to bring the Royal Spanish Academy hundreds of neologisms and words of Peru and the Americas, and from 20 October 1892 to 10 March 1893 he attended some fifteen or twenty of its sessions, in an "ardent battle, in favour of more than three hundred words in everyday use in America" (Tanner, 2002, quoting Palma's *Recuerdos de España*). The academy refused many of them; another account says the proposal was rejected almost completely. The film's "joins the meetings of" is his attending the sessions as a corresponding member; "Most are turned down" is the accounts' refusal. He'd sent the academy words before, and in 1889 wrote that it had accepted nearly three hundred of his four hundred slips: the film tells only 1892.
- **"To budget", seventeen years later.** In April 1909, Palma, then 76, asked his friend in Madrid, Daniel Granada, to propose the word *porcentaje* (percentage), and to tell him what had become of *presupuestar* (to budget), "a term he had proposed some 17 years before": "Do they accept it, or do they insist on rejecting it?" (Tanner, 2002, from Palma's *Epistolario*). The academy turned *porcentaje* down that December, and Palma wrote that the dictionary was "a sanitary cordon between Spain and America". *Presupuestar* is in the dictionary today (*Diccionario de la lengua española*, online, checked on 28 October 2026: two senses, to work out a venture's costs and income, and to include an item in a budget). The film says "the word for 'to budget'", in English, and puts no Spanish on screen; the Spanish captions say *presupuestar*.
- **Why the missing words were a problem.** The dictionary was read as the norm of correct Spanish, so a word it left out was taken for a wrong one; Palma's own words for the harm are the "sanitary cordon", and his view that "languages are eminently democratic", so the academy should accept "the impositions of the speaking majority" (*Papeletas lexicográficas*, 1903, as Tanner quotes it). "People look in the dictionary to see if a word is right, so words left out count as wrong. And the millions who use them have no way in." says this in plain words. The schoolroom and the people using the word are the film's pictures.
- **The academies, together.** The Association of Academies of the Spanish Language (ASALE) was founded in Mexico City in 1951, at a congress from 23 April to 6 May called by Mexico's president, Miguel Alemán; the Royal Spanish Academy wasn't there, took part in the association's permanent commission, and has taken part in its congresses since the second, in Madrid in 1956. The film's "Spain's academy soon joins their work" says this, and its picture of 1956 is a card set down at a table. ASALE has twenty-three academies today, in Spain, the Americas, the Philippines and Equatorial Guinea (its own brochure). The dictionary has been the work of the Royal Spanish Academy and ASALE together since its 23rd edition (2014). How a word goes in: the academies' members propose words of the area where they live; the academy's commissions draft each proposal of an addition, a removal or an amendment, with the help of its Institute of Lexicography; the plenary approves it; the proposals then go to the other academies, through ASALE's Permanent Commission, for their view, so the change can be finally approved; and approved changes go into the next update. The film's "A commission drafts each change, the Spanish Academy approves it, and every other academy gives its view before it goes in." says this in plain words. The online dictionary is free; its updates come once a year with a list of what's new (update 23.8, on 10 December 2024, had 4,074 changes; 23.8.1 came in December 2025, ahead of the 24th edition). The 24th edition was announced for the autumn of 2026; on 28 October 2026 no announcement of its publication was found, and "each year's update" holds either way.
- **More than six hundred million.** The Instituto Cervantes, *El español en el mundo 2025*: about 635 million people can speak Spanish, about 520 million of them as their first language. The film says "for more than six hundred million people".

## In short

- **What:** a new series, *People first* (*Las personas primero*), opens the module. Four films, set in February 2027, the month before Ivy Reyes joins: *People before roles*, *Wellbeing is in the work*, *No two alike* and *How, more than who*.
- **The frame:** four questions a leader asks before the work. Who is here? What do they need? How do they differ? How do they work together?
- **The references:** no single framework. The backbone is ISO 27500, the international standard on the human-centred organisation. The stance is Ubuntu, with its cousins from the Andes. Each film rests on the best-tested research for its question.
- **Personality:** real, and worth a film. Decided by the author on 12 November 2026: the five broad traits are the reference, a way to understand that a team is different people with different traits, all of them valuable for working together. What matters is that everyone knows how to collaborate. Never a tool for assessing anyone.
- **The stories:** the rope bridge of Q'eswachaka in Peru, made new each year by four communities, for three films; a day in Confucius's school, in China about 2,500 years ago, for the film on how people differ.
- **The fourteen films:** none is voiced or drawn again. *Many hats, one picture* stays as the author validated it.
- **From the author's own papers:** four papers from his master's studies hold six ideas the module hadn't touched. Five fit inside these four films. The sixth, more than one way to lead, becomes a film of its own, which he asked for on 12 November 2026; its plan comes first.

**Confirmed by the author on 12 November 2026:** the series and its four films; the frame; the stories; what the films take from his papers; and what changes in the module. How personality is shown he had decided earlier that day. > "The feedback I have about the leading data people is that we are not really starting with people. As mentioned, people are the most important asset in all, so the series needs to start with just people, collaboration. Get inspiration from the Ubuntu and other people-first frameworks before we start with the hats, skills and so on. People before roles is important, their wellbeing and their personal traits. We have few no nothing about it and needs a much better framework. I have even checked the personality traits in https://www.16personalities.com/teams so we are aware that there are personalities and they also influence the way we collaborate. | What the author asked | Where it goes |
|---|---|

| Ubuntu and other people-first frameworks, before the hats | The stance of the whole series, named in *People before roles*. Ubuntu's own film, *The team comes first*, stays where it is |
| People before roles | *People before roles* |
| Their wellbeing | *Wellbeing is in the work* |
| Their personal traits; personalities shape how people collaborate | *No two alike*, with the evidence checked (below) |
| A much better framework | *The frame*, below: four questions, each with its sources |

**What the module has, and what was missing.** People are in every one of the fourteen films. *The team comes first* teaches Ubuntu in practice, says that the people's wellbeing comes first, and shows it with the night calls one person answered alone. *When someone falls behind* looks for the cause before it judges. But the module opens with *Many hats, one picture*, which is about roles and skills. No film asks who the people are before what they do. None says what wellbeing at work is made of. None says how people differ, or what a personality test can and can't tell a team. None shows a team agreeing how it works together.

**Where it stops.** Skills are the next series. Listening, explaining and saying no are *Working with people*. Feedback, goals and performance are *Ownership and performance*. Helena's ninety days, told from the manager's side, are the module *Data management*. The series isn't a course in psychology or mental health: a leader isn't a clinician, and the films say so. It says nothing about hiring.

### What the author's own papers add

The same day the author shared four papers from his master's studies, written in 2015 and 2016: "there were other documents I worked on with proposals and my perspectives. Good to consider as they shaped my management style." They are read here as his view then, which he may see differently now. Nothing personal from them is shown or named: they shape the emphasis only.

Much of what they hold, the module already teaches: managing and leading as two sides of one role, coaching, improvement in small steps, the interests behind a position, one list with a rhythm for planning, a weekly talk with each person that asks how they are, and no ranking of people. Six ideas it hadn't touched:

| | The idea in the papers | What the module has | Where it could go |
|---|---|---|---|
| a | **More than one way to lead, chosen for the person and the moment.** The papers build on Daniel Goleman's six ways of leading. Two of them, giving orders and setting the pace by one's own example, harm a team when they are most of what a leader does, and the author saw the second most among technical leaders. Coaching is used least. His own proposal: learn the ways one at a time, each in a meeting the team already keeps | No film on ways of leading. *Growing people* teaches feedback and goals; *Less, on purpose* says what a meeting is for | **A film of its own,** which the author asked for on 12 November 2026 (below). In this series, one line of *No two alike*: the teacher held one student back and urged the other on, and a leader changes her way too |
| b | **Feelings belong in a hard conversation.** Say what you saw without judging it, what you feel and need, and what you ask: Marshall Rosenberg's non-violent communication. The author's own lesson from a negotiation was that arguments alone weren't enough | No film speaks of feelings | *How, more than who*: the disagreement about a number |
| c | **What makes people care isn't what stops them being unhappy** (Frederick Herzberg's two kinds of factor). Fair pay, sound tools and clear rules stop people being unhappy; the work itself, doing it well, being seen for it and growing are what make them care | No film speaks of motivation | *Wellbeing is in the work*: the three needs are the second kind |
| d | **Ask the whole team, show them the answers, and ask again.** The author put the same questions to everyone in a team of his, in writing, showed them the results and what he decided, and planned to ask again | Half an hour a week with each person (*The team comes first*), and "What slowed the work?" asked aloud every second Thursday (*Measure the work, not the people*). Nothing in writing, where the quiet answer too | *Wellbeing is in the work*, after Marta's week |
| e | **A leader negotiates a pace the team can keep:** a schedule it can meet, no weekends as the plan, a rhythm for planning, time to improve. Prepared, with what each side wants, what both want, and the least the leader can accept | *Saying no, and what instead* teaches interests, options and fair criteria, and leaves out the best alternative. No film shows a leader negotiating the team's pace | *Wellbeing is in the work*: Helena agrees Marta's order with the people who asked. The author's table for preparing a negotiation could become a lab for *Saying no, and what instead* |
| f | **Leading without a title.** Anyone can lead from where they are, and needn't wait for a manager to do it | *Owning the outcome* shows it for one person's own work | *People before roles*: a chart shows who manages, not who people turn to |

One observation in the papers is this series' own reason, made in 2015: in practice, the product's results and even the team's tend to come before each person in the team.

**A film on ways of leading** is new work, outside this series. The author asked for it on 12 November 2026: "add the film for management styles". Its plan is written first, for him to confirm, and where it sits is for that plan. *The new skills* is the likeliest home: it promises that "leading the people who do it is a skill too", no film there teaches it, and no voiced line there counts the films. *Leading a data team* says in its voice that it follows four ideas, so a film added there would mean voicing a line again. As a fifth film here, it would bring a skill into the series the author asked to keep to people.

**Smaller, and outside this series,** for the author to choose: a simulation from one paper, in which firms that cooperate ride out bad luck better than firms that compete, could become a lab beside *The team comes first*, labelled as a toy model; ethics seen as care for a web of relations (Fritjof Capra), which sits close to this series' stance, has no film; and a personal plan could be an optional page beside *Growing people*, offered and never asked for.

**Not carried over.** Two practices from the papers: extra hours as the answer when a team falls behind its own promise, and opening a negotiation above one's goal, to leave room to give. The author's own negotiation argues against the first. Against the second, the module teaches promises plain enough to check, inside one organisation (*Saying no, and what instead*).

**How sure the sources are.** Goleman's six ways come from a consultancy's study of 3,871 executives, published in a business review and not a research journal: a map that practitioners find useful, not a tested law. Herzberg's split in two is debated; that the design of the work shapes how people feel about it is well supported (Humphrey and others, 2007, 259 studies). Non-violent communication rests on practice and small studies. That teams whose members share the leading do better is supported across 42 samples (Wang and others, 2014): more for how a team works together than for what it delivers, and more when the work is complex, as data work is. Herzberg and Rosenberg are read so far only in secondary sources.

## The frame: four questions before the work

The author asked for a much better framework. No published one covers this ground, so the series uses a small frame of its own, built from the sources below: four questions a leader asks about the people before asking anything about the work. Each film answers one.

| | The question | The film | What answers it |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | **Who is here?** | *People before roles* | A team is people before it is roles. Ubuntu and the Andean *minka*; ISO 27500 |
| 2 | **What do they need?** | *Wellbeing is in the work* | Three needs everyone has at work, and the balance between what the work asks and what helps. Self-determination theory; job demands and resources; the World Health Organization; Safe Work Australia |
| 3 | **How do they differ?** | *No two alike* | By degree, not by type, and every mix of traits is of use to the team. The five broad traits, with their limits; a teacher's two answers, from the Analects |
| 4 | **How do they work together?** | *How, more than who* | How a team works counts for more than who is in it, and the how can be agreed. The studies of teams |

Then the hats, the skills and the work: the rest of the module.

The order is what people share, then how they differ, then how they combine. It also puts first what a leader can change: the work, and how it's arranged. A person's traits aren't a leader's to change.

## The reference, and its alternatives

No single framework covers people at work, so the series names several, once each, and teaches what they share. None is a syllabus.

| Body of knowledge | What it adds | Here |
|---|---|---|
| **ISO 27500:2016**, *The human-centred organization: rationale and general principles*, and **ISO 27501:2019**, its guidance for managers | The one international standard on what putting people first means for an organisation. Seven principles: capitalize on individual differences as an organizational strength; make usability and accessibility strategic business objectives; adopt a total system approach; ensure health, safety, and well-being are business priorities; value employees and create a meaningful work environment; be open and trustworthy; act in socially responsible ways. Written for boards, and not for certification | **The backbone.** Four of the seven are the four films. Named once, in *People before roles* |
| **Ubuntu**, as Desmond Tutu, Mogobe Ramose and Thaddeus Metz write it | A person is a person through other people | The stance. Named in *People before roles*, which points to *The team comes first* and doesn't retell it |
| The Andean ***minka*** and ***ayni***; *sumak kawsay*, living well together | Work done together for the community, and help between families that is given back; a good life as a shared one | The bridge story. Named once |
| **Self-determination theory** (Richard Ryan and Edward Deci) | Three needs people have, at work as anywhere: a say in what they do, to be good at it, and to belong. Across 881 samples from work, school, sport, parenting and health care, people whose leaders, teachers, coaches or parents support these needs are clearly better off, and somewhat better at what they do, in much the same way across cultures. On how well people work it has fewer: 22 samples, for support for autonomy | *Wellbeing is in the work*; it runs through all four |
| **Job demands and resources** (Evangelia Demerouti, Arnold Bakker and others); the **World Health Organization** on burn-out and on mental health at work; **Safe Work Australia** on psychosocial hazards; ISO 45003:2021 | What the work asks wears people out; what helps them, when it's missing, makes them pull away. Burn-out comes from work stress that wasn't managed, and isn't classed as a medical condition. The hazards an Australian workplace must manage, among them job demands, low job control, poor support, lack of role clarity and poor organisational change management | *Wellbeing is in the work* |
| **The five broad traits of personality** (the "Big Five") | The model most research uses: a few broad traits, each a matter of degree | **The reference for *No two alike*,** as the author decided on 12 November 2026, with its limits said. For understanding people, never for assessing them |
| **Sixteen-type tests** (the Myers-Briggs Type Indicator; 16Personalities) | A friendly, shared way to talk about differences, with scales underneath: Myers-Briggs's four are close to four of the five traits, and 16Personalities measures a reworked five and keeps the letters. The weak step is cutting each scale into two kinds of people | Not the reference. *No two alike* gives them one moment: a way to start talking, read as degrees, never a way to decide. No test is named in a film |
| **The Analects of Confucius**, with Sima Qian's *Records of the Historian* and Zhu Xi's commentary | One question from two students, and two opposite answers: he answered the person. In Zhu Xi's words, he "taught each person according to what they were made of" | The story of *No two alike* |
| **Studies of teams:** Google's study of 180 of its teams; collective intelligence (Anita Woolley and others); trust; kinds of conflict; team charters | How a team works together counts for more than who is in it: equal turns, promises kept, clarity, and disagreement about the work and not the person | *How, more than who* |
| **Psychological safety** (Amy Edmondson) | A team where it's safe to speak up | Taught by *The team comes first*; named once in *How, more than who*, not retold |
| From the author's own papers: **the leader without a title** (Robin Sharma), with the research on shared leadership; **two kinds of factor** (Frederick Herzberg), with the research on how work is designed; **non-violent communication** (Marshall Rosenberg); **six ways of leading** (Daniel Goleman) | What each adds, and how sure each is, is under *What the author's own papers add* | One idea each in *People before roles*, *Wellbeing is in the work* and *How, more than who*; the ways of leading in one line of *No two alike*, and in a film of their own, which the author asked for on 12 November 2026 |
| Considered, and not followed | **Maslow's hierarchy:** needs matter everywhere, but each counts whether or not the others are met, so it isn't a ladder (Tay and Diener, 123 countries). **Team roles and styles from a questionnaire** (Belbin, DiSC, strengths tests): they hand each person a role or a style, which is the sorting *No two alike* advises against. **Tuckman's stages of a group:** a description of how groups form, not a practice. **Servant leadership:** as in the module's plan, an idea that runs through the films without a film of its own. Of these, only Tay and Diener was read again on 12 November 2026 | Named here so the author can say if one should lead |

**Where the films adopt, extend and differ.**

- **Adopt:** ISO 27500's principles; the three needs; the two sides of job demands and resources; the five broad traits, as degrees; "how, more than who"; the test publishers' own rules.
- **Extend:** the standard names no philosophy, and the films give it one: Ubuntu and the Andean *minka*. The *How I work* page and the team's one page of agreements come from practice, not from a trial, and the films say so.
- **Differ:** from sixteen-type tests (no types on screen, nobody sorted); from wellbeing as perks, or as asking people to be tougher (the work is changed first: the World Health Organization recommends that employers prevent harm with "organizational interventions that directly target working conditions and environments"); from teams built by matching types of person (the studies say nothing for it, and a good deal for how a team works).

## What the evidence says about personality

The author looked at a sixteen-type test's page for teams, and is right that personalities exist and shape how people work together. Checked on 12 November 2026:

1. **People differ in lasting ways, by degree.** Research describes personality with a few broad traits, most often five (openness, conscientiousness, extraversion, agreeableness and emotional stability). Each is a scale, not a box. A person's usual level is steady enough to notice, yet "the typical individual regularly and routinely manifested nearly all levels of all traits in his or her everyday behavior" (Fleeson, 2001). The levels also shift over a working life: people tend to grow more conscientious and more emotionally stable, most of all between 20 and 40 (Roberts, Walton and Viechtbauer, 2006, 92 samples).
2. **The five traits aren't the last word.** Among the Tsimane, farmers and foragers in Bolivia, the five didn't appear; two broad factors did (Gurven and others, 2013, 632 people). So the films say "a few broad traits is how most research describes it", never "there are five kinds of difference".
3. **A type is a scale cut in two.** McCrae and Costa (1989) found "no support for the view that the MBTI measures truly dichotomous preferences or qualitatively distinct types"; it measures four dimensions, close to four of the five traits. A committee of the US National Research Council (1991) read the evidence: "the popularity of the instrument is not coincident with supportive research results". In eleven samples from the test's own manual, between 24 and 61 per cent of people got the same four letters when they took it again (median 40); in another study, 47 per cent kept their type after five weeks. The scales themselves were steady (.75 to .85). It's the cut that moves people, because many sit near the middle. The review is old and the test has been revised since, but the point follows from cutting any scale in two.
4. **16Personalities says much the same of itself.** Its own account: "any type-based theory will struggle to describe or characterize people whose scores lie near the dividing line"; it keeps the letters "for its simplicity and convenience", measures a reworked Big Five, "a model that dominates modern psychological and social research", and asks for each of its five aspects to "be seen as a two-sided continuum". Its evidence for its own accuracy is published by itself. It adds: "We don't offer anything we don't truly believe in – personality testing in job interviews, for instance".
5. **The publishers' own rules are strict.** The Myers & Briggs Foundation: "Taking the instrument is always voluntary. The information is not to be used to label, evaluate, or limit an individual in any way." And: "It is unethical practice to use results on the MBTI instrument to screen job applicants' employment or positions." Results go only to the person who took the test, unless that person agrees to share them.
6. **Why people like these tests anyway.** The committee's answer: the feedback is positive and vague enough to fit most people, and "the instrument is a vehicle for addressing problems that can be resolved through guided discussion". That last part is their real use.
7. **Personality and teams.** Traits relate to work, modestly: conscientiousness most of all (Wilmot and Ones, 2019, 92 meta-analyses), and less so in complex jobs. Who is in a team matters too. In studies of real teams, those whose members were on average more conscientious and more open, who liked working in a team, and whose least agreeable member was still agreeable did better; in laboratory teams the links were negligible (Bell, 2007). None of that is about types, or about matching one kind of person with another. And at Google, across 180 teams, "what really mattered was less about who is on the team, and more about how the team worked together"; "extroversion of team members" was among the things not significantly connected with how well a team did.

**The author's question, and his decision.** On 12 November 2026 the author asked why a sixteen-type test would have weak support when it builds on Myers-Briggs, and said that this is "the human space which is less strict". Both halves hold. The scales under these tests are sound: Myers-Briggs's four are steady, and close to four of the five traits; and 16Personalities says it uses "the acronym format introduced by Myers-Briggs for its simplicity and convenience" while its types rest on "five independent spectrums", a reworked Big Five. What is weak is narrower: the cut that turns a place on a scale into one of two kinds of person, and any use of a result to decide about someone. And a team needs no exact measurement to talk about how its people differ, which is where this space is less strict. He then took the Big Five as the reference: a way to understand that a team is different people with different traits, all of them valuable for working together; what matters is that everyone knows how to collaborate; and it is never a tool for assessment, which rests on outcomes and team culture.

**So the films show it this way.**

- The five broad traits are the reference. The film shows them as scales, says that this is how most research describes people and not the last word, and shows nobody's place on them.
- Every mix of traits is of use to a team: the one who charges ahead and the one who holds back, the one who thinks aloud and the one who thinks afterwards. The film ranks no trait above another. What matters more is that everyone knows how to work together, which is the next film.
- A leader asks each person how they work best, and never guesses from a label, a test or a first impression.
- Never for assessment. Nothing about a person's traits is used to hire, to hand out work, to judge or to assess. How someone is doing is read from outcomes and from the team's culture, as the author put it.
- No film names a test or draws type letters. The sources name them, in the script.
- When someone proposes a test for the team, the film's answer is yes, as a conversation, with four rules: it's each person's choice; the results are theirs, and they share what they choose; they are read as degrees, not boxes; and they are never used to hire, to hand out work or to judge anyone.
- What the team keeps is a page each person writes in their own words, *How I work*, and ways of working built for the whole range: the question sent the day before, time to write before anyone talks, a turn each.
- A caution for the script's sources, not for the screen: the same publisher now offers teams a chat assistant that answers questions such as "What's the best way to motivate Marcus?". That is advice about a person from a label. The film's answer is to ask Marcus.

### The bridge that is made new every year

Q'eswachaka is a rope bridge of grass over a gorge of the Apurímac river, in Quehue, in the high Andes of Cusco, Peru. Checked on 12 November 2026: first on the heritage pages, and then, by the films' threads, in the study of the bridge that Peru's Ministry of Culture published in 2015, which corrects the first reading in three places (the bridge's history, who crosses it, and who twists the cord).

- It is 28 metres long, with a floor 1.2 metres wide, about fifteen metres above the river at its middle; the river bed is at about 3,700 metres above the sea (the Ministry's study, pages 175 and 183).
- Four Quechua-speaking communities make it new every year: Huinchiri, Chaupibanda, Choccayhua and Ccollana Quehue. About a thousand people gather in the second week of June, for three days of building and a fourth of celebration (the study, page 175; the Ministry's page of 2013 says more than a thousand; the US National Endowment for the Humanities, 2017).
- **How old, and how often.** By the communities' own account, people have crossed here on rope since the time of the Incas, five centuries ago and more. Nothing was published about this bridge before 1970, so its age is their account, and the Inca way of building it (page 152). The date is new: the bridge was still made on 6 January in the mid 1990s, and moved to June, the dry season, a few years before 2002 (page 156).
- **It lapsed once, for a short time.** In 1968 a steel bridge was built almost a hundred metres away. A geographer who came in 1970 wrote that the rope bridge hadn't been raised for a few years, found it decaying, and expected the skill to die with that generation (page 153). His is the one report of how long: the study calls the lapse very brief (page 155), and a film-maker from Cusco had filmed a raising at the end of the 1960s (page 164). In October 1972 the bridge's keeper, Luis Choqueneira, stopped two visitors: "Don't cross! The bridge is dying!" He told them: "My people feel sad about abandoning the keshwa chaca for a new steel bridge upstream. So we're going to rebuild it when the New Year comes, just as we have done every year since Tupa Inca ordered our ancestors to do so. Come back in January if you want to see." *National Geographic* reported the renewal in 1973, with people of three communities taking part; that it was that January is how the study's account reads, and the article itself wasn't read. The builders, asked in 2012, name nobody from outside who prompted the return: they tell of storms and hunger in the time the bridge was left, and say that since then everyone comes (pages 154 to 156). Three communities were recognised then; Choccayhua has been one since 1975 (page 156).
- **Who crosses it.** It was once the only way between the communities. Today most people cross on the steel bridge, and the Ministry calls the rope bridge's use as a crossing *relativa*; people still cross it on foot (page 175; *National Geographic*, 2018).
- UNESCO, which put the renewal on its list of humanity's intangible heritage in 2013: the communities "see it as a means of strengthening their social links and not simply as a transport route"; "although the renewal lasts only three days, it structures the life of the participating communities all year round".
- Each family promises a cord of forty arm spans, about seventy metres, twisted by hand from *q'oya* grass, with the children taking part. On the first day each family's cord is checked in; the men lay the cords out in groups of thirty or forty and twist them into ropes, while the women and children go on making cord. Three ropes are braided into each of the four cables of the floor, and two twisted into each of the two handrails: six cables. Three communities make the cables, and the fourth the mat for the floor (pages 177 to 180). On the second day the cables are tied to old stone bases, and the bridge of the year before is cut loose. On the third, two bridge builders weave the floor to the handrails, "each starting from opposite ends" (UNESCO), until they meet in the middle.
- The bridge has no drawing and no written plan (the Endowment). The work is passed on by doing it, and the Ministry calls the way it is organised *minka*, mutual help.
- The bridge builder Victoriano Arizapana: "If we stop preserving it, it would be like if we died. We wouldn't be anything."
- In 2020 the pandemic stopped the renewal. The bridge's fall was reported on Tuesday 23 March 2021, when it was 21 months old; no report says that anyone was on it. The communities wove it again in mid June 2021, with their own means. In the early hours of 17 May 2025 someone cut it down; they had it rebuilt on 7 June.

**What no film says,** since the sources don't: that the bridge has been made new every year since the Incas, or every June, of the past; that it has never been more than a year old, or that 2020 was the only year missed; that nobody made it in the years around 1970, which rests on one visitor's report; that it carries people every day; that four communities took it up again in 1973; that nothing is written down (the communities keep written statutes and a register of their members); or a total of ropes, which the study's own count doesn't add up to.

**What the story didn't achieve, and where it stops being a guide,** said in the closing film. The renewal keeps a bridge and four communities together today, as by their own account it has since the time of the Incas. It wasn't unbroken: they let the bridge go for a time around 1970. It hasn't made the bridge safe from one missed year, or from a knife in the night. The knowledge of weaving it stays in one family line, as the builders believe it should: "that knowledge and wisdom should be passed on to one bloodline only". Fewer young people speak Quechua, the language the work is done in. Cars cross on the steel bridge, and so do most people, so visitors now help keep the rope bridge going. And at the gorge, custom settles who does which part. That is the communities' own way, and the films don't judge it; it just isn't a model for a data team, which has to ask each person, and can't leave what it knows with one line of people. *Many hats, one picture* and *The team comes first* take over from there.

### The teacher who gave two answers

*No two alike* opens in China, in the state of Lu, about 2,500 years ago. Kong Qiu, whom the West calls Confucius (551 to 479 BC, as the historian Sima Qian dates him), turned no student away: "From anyone who brought as little as a bundle of dried meat, I have never withheld teaching" (Analects 7.7). So he had students of every kind. The Analects, the book of his sayings that his followers put together, keeps one exchange (book 11, chapter 21, or 22 as some editions count):

- Zilu asks: "When I hear something, should I act on it at once?" The teacher: "Your father and elder brothers are alive. How can you act at once on what you hear?"
- Ran You asks the same. The teacher: "Act on it at once."
- A third student, Gongxi Hua, has heard both: "I am confused. May I ask?"
- The teacher: "Ran You holds back, so I urged him on. Zilu has the energy of two, so I held him back."

Sima Qian, some four centuries later, says more of the two. Zilu, nine years younger than his teacher, "loved courage and strength". Ran You, twenty-nine years younger, was steward to one of Lu's great families. In the twelfth century the scholar Zhu Xi summed up the teacher's way: he "taught each person according to what they were made of".

In the four beats: a teacher who turns no one away, and so has students of every kind; one answer for all of them fits none, since "act at once" sends the one who charges ahead into trouble and "ask first" leaves the one who hangs back standing still, and a student who hears both answers thinks his teacher has contradicted himself; so the teacher answers the person as well as the question, holding one back and urging the other on; and each gets what he needs. Sima Qian counts about three thousand students, and seventy-two who mastered the six arts he taught. Then the step the film adds: he had known them for years. A manager in her first month hasn't, and her first guess is wrong. A person can say how they work, so she asks.

Where it stops being a guide, for the script to weigh. The teacher decided what each student needed; a manager asks. The same book elsewhere lists ten students under four strengths and sums up four of them in a word each; the film takes the one exchange, and doesn't sort anyone. And Sima Qian tells how Zilu died, in 480 BC: a revolt broke out in Wei, where he served, he heard of it and rode straight there, a fellow student leaving by the gate told him to turn back, and he went in. His teacher, hearing of the revolt, said that Zilu would die there. Knowing a person isn't changing them, which is the line the frame draws.

### What the bridge stands for, and why one film leaves it

The people are never the grass. They are the families and the builders. The bridge is what they make and renew together: their ties, and their way of working. That keeps *Wellbeing is in the work* honest: what wears out and is replaced is the arrangement, never a person.

It is also why *No two alike* leaves the bridge. One blade of grass is like another, and a person isn't like anyone. The bridge has no honest part for how people differ, and bending the story to make one would break the rule that a story is told from its own sources. So the film says so in a line, and goes to a teacher whose two students asked him the same question.

Both stories are told in the module's own drawing style and with its own instruments: nothing Andean or Chinese is borrowed for the look or the sound, and no Chinese characters are drawn. The Andes appear once elsewhere on the site, in *What it must be able to do*, with the Inca's relay runners; this is a different story, of a custom that is alive today, and its oldest date in the films is 1968. ## In practice

What practitioners do, from the sources above, for the author to correct:

- Meet each person before you plan anyone's work from the list of requests. Ask about them at work: what they're proud of, what they'd like more of, what gets in their way, what you should know to work well with them. Don't ask about health, family or anything they don't offer.
- Say in your first week what won't change yet. A new manager is a change, and people fear the changes they can't see.
- Keep half an hour a week with each person, about them, as *The team comes first* and *Growing people* show.
- Look at the work before the person: how much at once, who decides the order, how often they're interrupted, who is alone on something, nights and weekends, what's unclear, who thanks whom. Change one thing in the work now, and put a date on the rest.
- Ask the whole team too: the same few questions about the work, in writing, so the quiet answer as well. Show everyone the answers and what you'll change, and ask again some months later (from the author's own practice).
- Agree with the people who ask a pace the team can keep, before the team pays for it: an order, a rhythm for planning, time to improve, and no weekends as the plan. Go prepared: what you want, what they want, what you both want, and the least you can accept (from a negotiation of the author's own).
- Notice early: worn out week after week, distant or sharp about the job, "I'm not good at this any more". Ask, listen, and lighten or change the work. When it's more than work, point to professional help and keep it private.
- Never sort people by a type, a test or a first impression. Ask "how do you work best?", and have each person write a page in their own words: when I do my best work, how to ask me for something, how to disagree with me, what wears me out, what I'm getting better at. Go first, and let each share what they choose.
- Meet in a way that suits the whole range: the question sent the day before, two minutes of writing before anyone talks, a turn each, and a way to add something afterwards.
- Write one page as a team: how we meet, how we ask for help, how we disagree, how we decide, how we say "I can't make that date". Read it each quarter, and when someone joins.
- Disagree about the work, in the open. When it turns to the person, stop and say so. Say what you saw without judging it, what you feel and need, and what you ask: feelings are there whether or not anyone names them. Keep small promises, and say early when you can't.
- Notice who people turn to, whatever the chart says, and make room for it: anyone can lead from where they are.

**Questions for the author's own practice** (AGENTS.md asks for it before a script; an answer in a line each is plenty):

1. In your first weeks with a new team, what do you ask each person, and what do you never ask?
2. For wellbeing, what do you watch for, and what has really helped? What was only a gesture?
3. Have you run a personality test with a team, 16Personalities or another? What did it change, and what wouldn't you do again?
4. Which agreements have your teams actually kept?
5. What would you tell a new manager about people that the books get wrong?

The author's papers of 2015 and 2016 answer part of these as he saw it then. What helped: a rhythm for planning, no weekends as the plan, time to improve, and asking the whole team. What a leader forgets: each person, behind the product and the team. He need only say what he sees differently now. The third question is lighter since his decision on personality: what's still worth knowing is whether a team of his has taken such a test, and what came of it.

## Rules from the plan

The module's rules hold: a draft label until the author has watched a film; no release status; nothing personal about the author on the site; light on tools; agents propose and people approve; blameless, and fair to everyone; the kit as it is; the fictional university, a year ahead. This series adds:

- **Never for assessment.** The five broad traits, and any test of them, are for understanding people. Nothing about a person's traits is used to hire, to hand out work, to judge or to assess: that rests on outcomes and team culture (the author, 12 November 2026).
- **No trait is ranked above another.** Every mix is of use to a team that knows how to work together.
- **No test is named** in a film, on screen, on a page or in Pause and think, and no type letters are drawn. The script's sources name them.
- **Nobody is labelled.** A person's way of working is shown in their own words, never as a kind of person. No film shows anyone's score.
- **Nothing is diagnosed.** The films name signs a leader can notice and what to change in the work. They say once where a manager's part ends.
- **Private matters stay private,** as in *When someone falls behind*.
- **The people are never the grass.** At the bridge, the people are the ones who build.
- **Two traditions, told with respect:** from their own sources and their people's own words, the bridge as the living practice it is, with its dates as the sources give them, and with no judgement of custom.
- **Nothing personal from the author's papers** is shown or named. They shape the emphasis only.

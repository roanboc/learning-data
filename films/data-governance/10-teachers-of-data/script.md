## The promise

Every line passes two tests: anyone who has been stopped by a rule they didn't understand can follow it, and an experienced data governance lead, or someone who has led a change at work, finds nothing wrong in it. The film closes the first part of the series and is about its people side. In Bogotá in 1995, the mayor sent mimes into the streets: when a car stopped on a crossing, the people around it whistled, a mime invited the driver to back up, and only if that failed did an officer write a fine, which the people watching applauded. The fine still came, but last. Most people keep a rule because they understand it and the people around them keep it; the fine is for the few. Governance has fines too, its checks, and most of its work comes before them: a page beside every check, written for the person it stops; office hours in place of a queue of approvals; stewards who share what broke and what helped; a new rule agreed with them, explained, and run as a warning until the day it's required, a date everyone knows; and measures read from the work, not a maturity score. The governance team's lasting job is to teach how the pieces fit. **Governance is people's work. Tools remember what people agreed.**

## The story in one paragraph

Bogotá, 1995. Drivers stop on the zebra crossings, and people on foot weave between the cars. The new mayor, Antanas Mockus, sends mimes into the streets. When a car stops on a crossing, the people around it whistle; a mime invites the driver, without a word, to back up; if the driver still won't, a traffic officer writes a fine, and the people watching applaud. By 1996, three drivers in four keep the crossings clear. Today, at the university, it's submission day: the submission to the government and Planning's census report agree, 4,867 awards, counted by the glossary's meaning, and how that maps to the government's definition is Mei's recorded decision. The checks helped, but they worked because people knew why they were there. Anil, an analyst in the Faculty of Health, adds a column, School, to the call list he made for the faculty's advisers, and the governance check sends his change back: the column doesn't say whether it identifies a person. Its note links a guide written for him, *Classify a column*; in two minutes he chooses "none", and his change is signed. Governance no longer keeps a queue of approvals: it keeps office hours, on Thursday afternoons. Once a month the stewards meet, to share what broke and what helped; Yusuf started with the few who wanted to come. At October's meeting, Yusuf brings a new rule, every column other teams read says what it holds; the stewards agree it and set a date, and until then the check only warns: Jun's change to the wallet is signed, with a warning that its column Awards held doesn't say what it holds yet, required from 1 December, and a line to its guide. Four weeks in, the warnings are gone. Binder 29 says governance maturity is assessed every year; the score of 2.1 isn't assessed again: governance is measured from each night's checks, from which of them failed and whose they are, and from who comes back to office hours. The governance team teaches, every new analyst starts with a path of films and labs, and Anil shows a new colleague the page that helped him. Of the binder's 215 lines, 92 run in the work, 64 are decisions someone owns, and 59 are gone. In Bogotá, the habits faded within about ten years, when the campaigns weren't kept up; so governance has to be something the university can always do, which is where the next part of the series begins.

## The reference, and practice

The series' reference is DAMA-DMBOK, named once, in *Off the shelf*; this film comes back to its wheel without naming it. For change, the plan names two models once, Kotter's eight steps and Prosci's ADKAR, and the film teaches what they agree on: people need to know why, to want it, to know how, to be able to, and to see it last. Other bodies of knowledge say the same in their own words, and the film draws on them without naming them:

| Body of knowledge | What it adds | Where the film uses it |
|---|---|---|
| Kotter's eight steps (*Leading Change*, 1996) | Urgency and a reason; a coalition of the willing; quick wins; anchoring the change so it outlasts its sponsors | The reason said first; the few who wanted to come; the habits that faded in Bogotá |
| Prosci's ADKAR (Hiatt, 2006) | Five things each person needs, in order: awareness, desire, knowledge, ability, reinforcement | Named, with its five needs, in chapter 8 |
| Mockus's law, morality and culture (*Cultura ciudadana*, 2001; his slides of 2003) | People keep a rule by conscience, by what the people around them do, and by the law; harmonise the three, the law last | The opening, and its order |
| Communities of practice (Lave and Wenger, 1991; Wenger, 1998) | People who do the same work learn from each other, regularly, by sharing practice | The stewards' monthly meeting |
| Diffusion of innovations (Rogers, 1962) | A practice spreads from the willing few to the rest, by what people see working | "Others joined once they saw it working" |
| Non-invasive data governance (Seiner, 2014) | Recognise the stewards already doing the work, rather than appoint new ones | The stewards are the people who already look after the data |
| Software practice: deprecation warnings, policies run as advisory before they're enforced | A change announced, warned and dated before it breaks anyone | Warn first, then required from a known date |

**Practice.** What practitioners recognise: rules people don't understand get switched off or worked around; error messages that say how to fix them get fixed; a new rule that fails everyone the day it lands teaches resentment; a community that meets to share, not approve, keeps coming; a maturity score says little about whether anyone uses governance, and coverage, time to fix and who comes back say more; and habits fade when the people who started them move on. *For the author:* what has worked for you in getting people to keep a data rule, and what hasn't? The film can use it in place of what's invented here.

## The look

*Watercolour*, the series' look since 17 October 2026, shared with the series on master and reference data, so the two look like one university.

- **Pencil and wash.** Every place is painted on paper, and people are drawn as the master data films draw them, with a soft wash of their side behind each one.
- **Only the university's places,** most of them the series' shared places, painted once: the floor of offices behind glass, Mei's desk in the registrar's office, Yusuf's office and its board, the stewards' meeting room, the faculty analyst's desk in the old building (and the same office seen from its other side, where a new analyst sits), and Noor's map. Each room keeps its wall colour. The film paints one place of its own: a crossing in the centre of Bogotá in 1995, and the same crossing years later.
- **Bogotá, painted the same way,** with the place and year in the corner: a crossing on a wide avenue of brick and concrete buildings, the green ridge of the eastern hills behind, with the white church on Monserrate, under a high grey sky. Drivers in small cars of the 1990s; people on foot in jackets and sweaters, a few with umbrellas; mimes in black with painted faces; a traffic officer in a plain uniform and cap, with a notebook. A warm umber wash behind each, as for people of the past. Nothing on screen is in Spanish: the signs are pictures, and the corner names the place. No style, pattern or music borrowed from Colombian culture: the story is told in the series' own voice.
- **Colour carries meaning,** as in the series: gold is governance's decisions, and the business side; cyan the technical side; green what runs in the work; grey what can go; red what nobody owns yet. Here, too, amber is a warning: a rule that warns, and hasn't stopped anyone yet.
- **The university's papers, not tools' screens.** The series' first part shows no files, code or tool screens (the author, 21 October 2026; the plan in three parts, approved on 23 October 2026). Where the work happens on a screen, the film shows the university's own papers: a table's headings in plain words, the sign-off sheet that films 2, 4 and 5 use too, a guide of a few plain lines, and plain scorecards. A change, not a pull request; a check, not a test; a table, not a model.
- **Fewer diagrams, more situations.** The ideas are people doing things: a mime's gesture, a page opened, a door card pinned up, a meeting, a date written down, a frame taken off a wall. The one diagram-like picture is the wheel, drawn by Yusuf's hand on a whiteboard.

## Sound

A new palette ([`source/tools/score.py`](source/tools/score.py)): a double-reed trio, oboe, cor anglais and bassoon, played softly and low, so nothing rings above the voice. The idea is teaching: one voice plays a short phrase of five notes, and another copies it. In 1996 the oboe plays it as people understand the rule, and the cor anglais copies it as the people around them keep it; when Anil's guide opens, the cor anglais plays it, and the bassoon copies it as his change is signed; the oboe plays it as a governance team teaches; and as the stewards pass it on, all three play it in turn. The mimes in Bogotá get a light trio, each gesture on a soft staccato note, as a silent film would score them, and the check that warns first gets the mime's notes. The officer's fine is one low bassoon note, and so is each check that stops the work; the applause is a muffled clatter of hands. The voices come in one at a time as things arrive in order: the three panels, the stewards as they're named, the hands that agree the rule, one soft note for each week crossed off, quieter as the warnings fall, and the five things people need, the last held longest. The opening is in G major; the university in E-flat major; Bogotá years later in E-flat minor, where the phrase stops a note short and nobody copies it; at the end it's whole again. The series' mark, four notes, 1-5-4-8, on the cor anglais, answered by the bassoon, plays under the title and the end card. As the series' rule says, music plays under the opening story, then only at a chapter's turn, under a moment on screen, or in a wordless pause: no bed under the voice, no urgent pulse, and no held notes beating against each other, as every chord is tuned pure. Nothing in it imitates the music of another culture: no instrument, rhythm or tune of Colombia's. The effects: a car's low idle and its tyres rolling back, soft whistles, applause, a notebook's page, paper, pins, a marker on the whiteboard, muffled keys, a frame lifted off its nail, a pencil on a calendar, each at the moment its thing appears, kept well under the music and all below about 2.5 kHz. The mix is -16 LUFS.

## Rigour to check before recording

Checked on 20 and 21 October 2026, and the plain words of the series' first part on 27 October 2026; the sources are listed below.

- **Bogotá, 1995, as Mockus wrote it.** Antanas Mockus was elected mayor for 1995 to 1997 (he left office on 10 April 1997 to run for president, and Paul Bromberg took his place for the rest of the term), and again for 2001 to 2003. The date and his successor are from Wikipedia's article on him; that he resigned to run for president is from Restrepo (note 3) and the same article. His own account (*Cultura ciudadana, programa contra la violencia en Santa Fe de Bogotá, Colombia, 1995-1997*, Inter-American Development Bank, technical study SOC-120, July 2001) describes the sequence the film shows, at the crossing of Avenida 19 and Carrera 7: people whistled at drivers who stopped on the zebra crossing; if the driver didn't back up, a mime appeared and tried to persuade him, with a "friendly and playful invitation"; if he still didn't, a traffic officer intervened, and "people ended up applauding, in sight of everyone, the officer's action of imposing the fine. So police repression was the last measure of a pedagogically ordered sequence." The film's "in an order meant to teach" is that last sentence. The mimes didn't replace fines, as the plan first said; they came before them.
- **When, and how many.** El Tiempo's *Diccionario de la lengua mockusiana* (26 January 2003): the mimes "went out on Calle 19 in March 1995, to teach pedestrians and drivers to cross at the zebras and keep the intersections clear". Mockus's account: from July 1995, for three months, 400 young mimes "with painted faces and dressed in black" taught without words and without shouting, then moved to the city's 19 urban localities. Other sources give 20 at first, and 420; the film gives no number. A contemporary report (El Espectador, 7 April 1995, quoted by Restrepo) found the first trial too local, a few blocks of Avenida 19, and warned that people might soon forget it.
- **"Three drivers in four."** Mockus's account: in 1996, 76.46% of drivers and 72.25% of pedestrians respected the zebra crossing; the campaign ran at 482 crossings with 425 people (mimes, police officers and others), and 364 crossings were marked. It gives no figure from before, so the film says only where it got to. Road deaths: his account gives a fall in the yearly rate of deaths in traffic accidents from 25 to 20 per 100,000 over 1995 to 1997, alongside a 1 a.m. limit on alcohol sales, designated driver campaigns and more seat belts; El Tiempo (2012) gives 24 to 7 over his two terms, which the other sources don't confirm. Because the deaths can't be credited to the mimes, the film leaves them out.
- **Why people keep a rule.** Mockus's slides for the IX Inter-American Conference of Mayors (Miami, 2003): three systems regulate behaviour, the law (admiration for it, or legal sanction), morality (one's own conscience, or guilt) and culture (social recognition, or social rejection), and the aim is to "strengthen the three regulatory systems" and "seek their harmony"; the mimes were "a new form of intervention by the authority", and the citizen cards (a white side with a thumb up, a red side with a thumb down, handed out in their hundreds of thousands) "peaceful and accepted interpersonal regulation". A 2009 profile quotes him: "Each of us must control our own inner lout. When we can't, our neighbour must. If that's not enough, then the police come in." The film's "most people keep a rule because they understand it, and because the people around them keep it; the fine is for the few" says this in plain words. Mockus's phrase "but in that order", quoted in an early note for the film, wasn't found in a source, and isn't used.
- **"The habits had faded."** El Tiempo, *Bogotanos enterraron en una década la cultura ciudadana de Mockus* (Carol Malaver, 15 September 2012): "What happened, for the ground gained by Mockus to be lost in little more than a decade? The recurring answer of the experts is the lack of continuity"; many of his civic programmes "began to be dismantled", and surveys found less willingness to correct others' behaviour on the roads. The 2012 picture is drawn for that article's year. Mockus, in the same article: "there's nothing more absurd than a project ending because the new mayor didn't understand it."

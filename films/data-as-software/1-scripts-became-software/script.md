## The promise

Every line passes two tests: someone who has never written a line of code can follow it, and an engineer who ships data for a living finds nothing wrong in it. Data work is software: kept in Git, tested before every change, reviewed by someone other than its author, and deployed the same way every time. None of that is new. Software has been made this way for decades, and data work took it up, as bridge builders took up the wind tunnel from the people who tested wings. The film shows it once, on one export, with the tools named (Git and GitHub, dbt, Declarative Automation Bundles on Databricks), and hands over to the official docs: there are already standards, so don't reinvent the wheel. **Data work is software.**

## The story in one paragraph

Washington State, 1 July 1940. A new suspension bridge opens across the Tacoma Narrows, its deck light and slender, built to the best theory of its day. From the first day, the deck rises and falls in the wind, and people call it Galloping Gertie. Nobody had tested how wind would move a deck like it. On 7 November, a wind of about 40 miles an hour sets it twisting until its middle tears away and falls into the water. No one is killed, but the only road across the Narrows is gone for ten years. The engineers who looked into it said such bridges must first be tested as models in a wind tunnel; at the University of Washington, Frederick Farquharson tested models for years, and the tests found a deck the wind can't twist: deeper, stiffer and open to the wind. The new bridge opened in 1950, tested before it was built, and has carried traffic for 76 years. Wind tunnels weren't new: wings had been tested in them since the Wright brothers. Data work did the same and took up how software is made. At the university, payroll's export to Finance ran for years as a script on one person's laptop: before six on each pay day someone ran it by hand and emailed its file to Rohan Desai in Finance, payroll's rule (approved by noon on the Monday before, paid that Thursday; later, two weeks later) inside it. It worked, and nobody is to blame; its author moved team, and it kept running, until in February the claims moved into the claims app, the spreadsheets it read stopped coming, and nobody could change it safely: one copy, no history of why, no test. Sam Okafor and Jun Park moved it into the data project: into Git, its sums a model in dbt, the rule written once on HR's calendar of pay days, confirmed by Ingrid Holm in HR, and tested first at its edge (11:59 and 12:01 on the Monday). Each change goes on a branch and comes back as a pull request, with checks; someone other than its author reviews it, an agent's change too. A Declarative Automation Bundle says how it runs: the same files go to test, then to production when Noor Rahman approves, as the platform's own account. This morning it ran for the eighteenth time, and 856 casual staff are paid today. The bridge of 1950 still stands; pay day is just another Thursday.

## Sound

A new palette, made in the film's own `source/tools/score.py` and reserved for this film in the module's palettes: **two ophicleides**, the keyed brass bass of 1817 that replaced the serpent's finger holes with keys laid out on a plan, so every note came out the same, whoever played: a hand's craft made exact by a plan. No other film on the site has one.

- **The lead,** played softly and low, with no vibrato, hollow and plain: every harmonic of a wide conical bore, the body's warmth near 400 Hz, the second harmonic a little weak, soft above 800 Hz and nothing above 2.2 kHz; the higher harmonics speak a moment after the first, with the faint buzz of the lips.
- **The partner,** a second ophicleide an octave higher, reading the same part: two players, one plan. It joins the lead where someone other than the author reads the work; a person's approval is its rising fourth, D to G (Ingrid's confirmation, the agent's change approved, Noor's approval).
- **The mark:** a phrase, G, B, D, C, B, G, played by the lead alone, a little uneven (each note a little early or late, louder or softer), then by both players together, note for note: uneven for the 1940 deck "built to the best theory of its day" and for the script run by hand; exact for the bridge of 1950, for "the same files, the same steps, every time", and under the end card.
- **Keys:** G major for the work and the plan, E minor for the problem (the untested deck, the spreadsheets that stopped), C major at "The next bridge had to be proven", D major as the tests find the deck. Just intonation from G, so nothing beats.

## Rigour

Checked on 5 November 2026; the sources are listed below.

- **The bridge of 1940.** Its grand opening was on 1 July 1940; "slender, elegant and graceful, the Tacoma Narrows Bridge stretched like a steel ribbon across Puget Sound" (WSDOT, *Tacoma Narrows Bridge history*); a suspension bridge, its road hung from two main cables, its deck stiffened by an 8-foot solid plate girder where earlier spans used deep open trusses (WSDOT, *Failure*). Built to the best theory of its day: its collapse "revealed the limitations of the 'deflection theory'", the accepted way to design long suspension spans then (WSDOT, *Failure*). The film says "light and slender, built to the best theory of its day", and blames no one.
- **"Galloping Gertie".** From its first months the deck rose and fell in the wind, so much that some motorists felt "seasick", and the bridge was known as "Galloping Gertie" (WSDOT, *Timeline*).
- **"Nobody had tested how wind would move a deck like it."** Wind tunnel tests on a model of the 1940 deck began only in September 1940, after the bridge opened, when the State Toll Bridge Authority hired Professor F. B. Farquharson "to determine the cause of and remedies for Gertie's 'bounce'" (WSDOT, *Timeline*); "Galloping Gertie became the first suspension bridge ever studied using both visual observation and wind tunnel testing" (WSDOT, *Aftermath*). So the red tag says "Not tested in the wind before it was built", as the 1950 bridge's green one says "Tested before it was built".
- **7 November 1940, about 40 miles an hour.** On the morning of 7 November the wind measured 38 miles an hour at 7:30 and 42 at 9:30; at 10:03 the roadway began "a lateral twisting motion", and the centre span fell into the water (WSDOT, *Timeline*). The film says "about 40" (64 km/h on screen). It never says "resonance": the twisting was aeroelastic flutter, as later studies found, and the film only shows it.
- **No one killed; ten years without a road.** No person died; a dog, Tubby, left in a car on the bridge, was "the only victim of that great disaster" (WSDOT, *Tacoma Narrows Bridge history*). The new bridge opened on 14 October 1950 (WSDOT, *Aftermath*), so the crossing was gone for ten years.
- **The engineers' report.** The "Carmody Board", the board of engineers the Federal Works Agency appointed, issued its final report in March 1941 (WSDOT, *Timeline*): Othmar Ammann, Theodore von Kármán and Glenn Woodruff (Wikipedia, *Tacoma Narrows Bridge (1940)*). One of its three key points: "aerodynamic forces were little understood, and engineers needed to test suspension bridge designs using models in a wind tunnel" (WSDOT, *Failure*). "Test bridges like it first, as models in a wind tunnel" is the film's summary of it.
- **Frederick Farquharson tests models for years.** Professor of engineering at the University of Washington; "for the four years of World War II, and occasionally afterwards to 1947, Farquharson studied the 1940 span and the new proposed Narrows Bridge in a specially built, 100-foot long Structural Research Laboratory that housed a wind tunnel and scale models of the bridges" (WSDOT, *Aftermath*); "1941 to 1947" on screen. He is drawn as a man of the 1940s, in a suit with glasses, not as a likeness.
- **The deck the wind can't twist.** The 1950 bridge's deck is stiffened by a 33-foot deep Warren truss, with "double (top and bottom) lateral bracing" that "gave the bridge exceptional torsional rigidity", and Farquharson "decided to test the model equipped with open wind grates to permit freer airflow" (WSDOT, *Aftermath*): "deeper, stiffer, and open to let the wind through". Since then, "wind tunnel testing for aerodynamic effects on bridges is commonplace" (WSDOT, *Failure*).
- **76 years of traffic.** 1950 to 2026; the bridge of 1950 still carries State Route 16's westbound traffic, beside a second bridge opened in 2007 (WSDOT, *Tacoma Narrows Bridge history*). The film shows only the bridge of 1950.
- **Wings since the Wright brothers.** At the end of 1901, frustrated by their gliders' flights, the Wright brothers built a wind tunnel to test wing shapes; "at the end of their 1901 wind tunnel tests, the Wright brothers had the most detailed data in the world for the design of aircraft wings" (NASA Glenn Research Center, *Beginner's Guide to Aeronautics*). "Since 1901" on screen.
- **The data story.** The university, its people and every number are the module's invented ones (`/mnt/project-files/modern-data-basics/numbers.md`, and the example project): 2,960 paid on 5 November 2026, 856 of them casual staff paid from approved claims; the claims app live on 23 February 2026; the export moved in the first week of March and run for every pay day since Thursday 12 March, so 5 November is its eighteenth run; pay days every second Thursday; the rule (approved by 12:00 on the Monday before, paid that Thursday; otherwise a fortnight later) and its calendar in `seeds/reference/staff/pay_days.csv`, owned by HR. No one's pay is shown.
- **What the film shows in the tools comes from the example project** (`films/analytics-engineering/project/`): `mart_finance__casual_pay_paid.sql` and `pay_day_for()` (`macros/payroll/pay_day.sql`), the unit test at the rule's edge in `models/marts/finance/_finance__models.yml`, `CODEOWNERS` giving every change's review to the data team (Finance and HR ask for changes in tickets, linked to the branch and pull request, and check the result before the ticket closes), the bundle `databricks.yml` (`data_project`, targets `dev`, `test` and `prod`), the job `resources/payroll_export.job.yml` (06:00 every Thursday, Australia/Sydney, a dbt task, as the platform's service principal), and `examples/scripts-became-software/` (the script before, the release workflow, and why each step is there). The release workflow is an example and never runs.
- **Never a release status.** The film, its captions, its page and Pause and think say no feature's release status (the author, 5 November 2026).

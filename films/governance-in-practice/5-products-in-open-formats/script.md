*The script of *Products in open formats*, film 5 of 6 of *Governance you can use*, the third series of data governance, in nine chapters, in English, painted in watercolour. Drafted on 28 October 2026 and made on 29 October 2026, when the author asked for the films of the three series to be made and merged one after another. It follows the plan the author approved on 23 October 2026 (film 18 of that plan: the tools films open on a scene of the university's own, on a line from the film they expand), and the rule of 28 October 2026 for every opening (scenario, problem, solution and benefits, each said outright; *Opening story* quotes them). Every file on screen is the example project's own, as it is on main, or one of *Governed in dbt*'s worked examples kept beside it (*What the example project has*); the products check's and the impact check's lines are their own, captured in a scratch copy. On 10 November 2026 it was told again, as the author asked of every governance film: nobody from governance reviews pull requests, and only the core, canonical tables have an enterprise contract, in the open standard, written first by data governance's information architect and built to by the data team (`governance-review-order`; *Decided*, 10 November 2026). As the author asked for these films, it isn't rendered here: the release workflow renders it.*

## The promise

Every line passes two tests: someone who has never read a data contract can follow it, and a data architect or a data product owner finds nothing wrong in it. *A product with an owner* made each table other teams read part of a product, with a promise and an owner who keeps it; *Change without surprises* kept a change from breaking its readers; *Where each piece lives* gave every artifact one home. This film shows the promise as files: the product's file, written by its owner; the enterprise contract of each core table it's built on, written first by data governance's information architect, with the model the data team builds to it, and a check on every pull request that the two still agree; Clara's own table, whose promise is in its code, a dbt contract the data team can change without waiting on governance; the check that every table other teams read is in exactly one product; the impact check that stops a breaking change, and the version that goes beside the old one, with a date to go; who reads each product, and what it's built from; the two open standards both files are written in, kept by Bitol at the Linux Foundation; and a catalogue on the platform, Ontos, from Databricks Labs, that imports a copy, while the files stay at home in the project. **A contract in an open format outlives the tool.**

## The story in one paragraph

Today, in Noor's room, Noor and Clara go over Clara's product. Noor designs the university's data and its six data products, each data with a promise and an owner who keeps it; Clara's is the count of learners near an award, ready by seven on census day. But the promises were typed by hand into a catalogue, where people look for data, while the tables changed every week: the catalogue could promise a column the table no longer had, and on census day a faculty's report could break, just when it's needed to plan next semester's places; and each catalogue kept its promises in its own format, so a new one meant typing them all again. Now each promise is a file kept with the code, in two open standards that any tool can read, one for the product and one for the contract of each core table it's built on; data governance's information architect writes that contract first, the data team builds to it, and a check stops the two drifting apart. When credentials needed a new column, its contract got the line first, Jun built it, and no report broke; all ten tables other teams read have a promise that's checked on every change; and whichever catalogue shows them, the promises stay the university's. The film then opens each piece: Clara's product file, with what it's for, how to use it, its owner, its table, its service levels and where to ask; the contract of a core table, written first by the information architect, each of its lines threaded to its line in the model the data team builds to it, the check that fails when the model drifts from it, and Clara's own table, with no enterprise contract, which the data team can change without waiting; the ten shared tables in six products, and the check that stops a new one in none; a rename in Clara's table, stopped by the impact check, the old version kept with a date to go, and credentials' two versions, the first retiring on 31 March 2027; who reads each product, and what it's built from, so Mei knows who to tell; the two standards, Bitol's, and the versions the project keeps; and Ontos, which imports each contract, lays the products out by domain, lets each owner publish their product, with no steward's review, checks every product each night, and links a column to its business term, but is a Labs project, with no formal support, so it holds a copy. On census day next March, Anil, in the Faculty of Health, plans next semester's places from Clara's product by seven, whichever tool shows it.

### 3 · A contract written first · 2:12–3:17

**Narration.** The contract is the promise about the table itself: each column, its type, whether it's always there, whether it can identify a person, and the tests it must pass. Only the core tables have one: the shared tables that every product is built on. Data governance's information architect writes it first, in the Open Data Contract Standard. The data team builds the model to it. Side by side, each line of the contract has its line in the model. On every pull request, a check fails if the model has drifted from its contract, or if the contract breaks the standard's rules. Then data governance looks at how the change got there: the contract changes first, and the code follows. Clara's own table has no enterprise contract: its promise is in its code, the data team's. So it can change without waiting on governance.

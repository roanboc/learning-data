## The promise

Every line passes two tests: someone new to leading people can follow it, and an experienced data manager finds nothing wrong in it. The film's idea is ownership: answering for an outcome, not only doing a task. An owner knows who uses the work and for which decision, notices before anyone asks, says so first when something is wrong, and fixes it or raises it. It takes one named owner, with the authority to match, and it is never blame: where nobody is blamed, saying it early is safe.

**Takeaway:** "Own what it's for, not only what you did."

## The story in one paragraph

Kenya, in the 1970s: in the hills of the central highlands, rural women tell Wangari Maathai, a biologist, that their streams are drying up, their food is less sure, and each year they walk further for firewood. The forests had been cleared for farms, for tea and coffee to sell, and for timber; each choice made sense to the people who made it, but without trees the soil washed off the hills and the springs ran dry, and the harm fell first on the women, who fetched the water, gathered the firewood and grew the food. In 1977 Maathai starts the Green Belt Movement with the National Council of Women of Kenya. Groups of women grow seedlings in their own nurseries from the seeds of local trees and plant them out, and they are paid a small sum for each tree still alive months later, not for each seedling put in the ground: they answer for living trees, not for the planting. More than fifty million trees have been planted in Kenya since; they give fuel, food and shelter, and thousands of women earned an income from them; in 2004 Maathai became the first African woman to win the Nobel Peace Prize. At the university, a year from now, Ivy Reyes becomes the owner of her first data product on Thursday 1 July, and Helena brings her a seedling in a pot: "It's yours now." *Teaching space use*: counts per room per hour, never a person, refreshed every Monday by seven. She knows its readers by name (Graham Ellis at Facilities, Mei Tanaka at the registrar's office) and what it's for: next year's timetable will move about one peak class in six and release rooms nobody uses, chosen from her counts. Many do part of the work; one answers for the outcome, and Helena gives her the authority to match. On Monday 23 August the refresh runs at seven and every check passes, but Ivy reads the numbers: in forty rooms the counts have dropped to about a quarter. She doesn't wait to be sure, or to be asked: she writes to Graham and Mei herself not to use them yet, and that the right counts come tomorrow. With Sam she finds the cause: Facilities had upgraded the sensors, which now send four counts an hour, and her model kept only the first. That part is hers to fix; Jun reviews her change, and by Tuesday morning the readers have the right counts. The sensors aren't hers, so she raises it with Graham, and Facilities now tells the data team before a sensor changes; her own new check would hold the next refresh. Nobody did anything wrong: what failed was the way the parts fit together. Helena's first question is what happened and what Ivy needs, never who.

## The reference, and practice

The brief names three references, each named once; the film teaches what they share: one person answers for what the work is for, with the authority to act on it, and when something goes wrong the question is what happened and what must change, not who to blame. *A product with an owner* (the data governance films, film 8) shows how a product gets an owner; it is named once and not retold.

| Body of knowledge | What it adds | Where the film uses it |
|---|---|---|
| Zhamak Dehghani, data mesh: "Data Mesh Principles and Logical Architecture" (martinfowler.com, 2020) and *Data Mesh* (O'Reilly, 2022) | Data as a product, owned by the domain that knows it; the domain data product owner answers for what the product is worth to the people who use it, not only for the pipeline | "Zhamak Dehghani's data mesh calls her the data product owner: she answers for what the product does for its readers." |
| RACI, the responsibility assignment matrix | Responsible, accountable, consulted, informed: many can do part of the work, and in the common practice only one person is accountable for each piece of it | "Many people do part of the work." "One person answers for the outcome. In a RACI chart, that's the A, for accountable, and there's only one." |
| Sidney Dekker, *Just Culture: Balancing Safety and Accountability* (2007) | After something goes wrong, ask what happened and what must change, rather than who to blame; accountability as giving an account, looking forward, so people can say what went wrong without fear | "Sidney Dekker calls it a just culture: when something goes wrong, ask what happened and what must change, not who to blame." "Being accountable means giving an account: what happened, and how the next time goes better." |
| Practice | An owner reads the numbers, not only the green tick; tells the readers first, before the fix; fixes what's theirs and raises what isn't; and adds the check that would have caught it | The whole data story, and the four steps of *On Monday* |

**Where the film adopts, extends or differs.** It adopts data mesh's product owner (who answers for the product's value to its readers) without the rest of data mesh's architecture, which a newcomer doesn't need for this decision. It adopts RACI's one A, and uses the chart only for that: one person answers, and many do part of the work. It adopts Dekker's question (what happened, not who) and his reading of accountability as an account given. It extends them from practice: ownership needs authority to match (Ivy can hold a refresh, change the model with a review and tell the readers without asking first), and an owner says so first, before the fix. It differs from a common reading of "ownership" as doing every part yourself: Ivy doesn't own the sensors or the platform, and a fix that isn't hers is raised, not done.

**Practice.** A practitioner will recognise the Monday when every check passes and the numbers are wrong; an improvement upstream that nobody thought would change the data; a step written for the data as it was; the word that goes to the readers before the cause is known; the change that isn't yours to fix; and the review where the first question is what happened, not who.

- **What rural women told Maathai.** The Green Belt Movement's own history: the movement began from "the needs of rural Kenyan women who reported that their streams were drying up, their food supply was less secure, and they had to walk further and further to get firewood". Maathai's Nobel Lecture (Oslo, 10 December 2004) names the needs "identified by rural women, namely lack of firewood, clean drinking water, balanced diets, shelter and income". The film says the women told her in "the hills of the central highlands": she grew up in Nyeri, in the central highlands, where her lecture remembers the forests cleared and the stream that dried up; the movement's women came from rural Kenya more widely, which the film's general "rural women" keeps (an assumption, in the film's assumptions file).
- **Maathai, a biologist.** A bachelor's degree in biology (1964), a master's in biological sciences (1966) and a doctorate in veterinary anatomy (1971), the first woman in East and Central Africa with a doctorate (Wikipedia, *Wangarĩ Maathai*). The film says "a biologist" and no title.
- **The cause.** The lecture: "forests being cleared and replaced by commercial plantations", and "the introduction of commercial farming, which replaced the growing of household food crops".

## The promise

Every line passes two tests: someone who has never measured data quality can follow it, and a data engineer or a data quality lead finds nothing wrong in it. It closes the series with the dimension *Rules that run*'s scorecard left in pencil: timeliness. Late data looks complete. Every row in it is right, and every check that reads the rows passes, so nothing says it's late. Whether it's too late depends on the use, so agree with the people who use the data how fresh each use needs it, and let the strictest use set the level. Say how old the data is wherever it's shown, for each source. And check its age where it starts, at each source, on when each file arrived: warn first, so the people who need to know hear at once, then stop the step that can't be undone, the send, never the data. **Agree how fresh it must be, and always say how old it is.**

## The story in one paragraph

November 1970: a cyclone comes ashore on the coast of East Pakistan, now Bangladesh, in the evening, with the high tide. Forecasters warn of danger, and as it nears, the radio broadcasts a great danger signal; but on the low islands it means little, and survivors said later they took signal number one to be the worst. Most people know a storm is coming, yet almost nobody takes shelter, and that night the storm surge sweeps over the islands: at least 300,000 people die, perhaps half a million. A warning people can't read, or don't act on, is no warning. In 1972 the Bangladesh Red Cross Society starts the Cyclone Preparedness Programme, run with the government from 1973: volunteers from the coast's own villages carry each warning the last mile, house to house with megaphones, and lead people to shelters, and their flags rise with the danger, one, then two, then three, a signal the whole village can read. In November 2007, Cyclone Sidr strikes the same coast with winds as strong as 1970's, though at low tide. Warnings have gone out for days, more than 40,000 volunteers carry them, a million and a half people are in shelters, and about 3,400 die: still too many, and about one for every hundred in 1970. Data has a last mile too. At the university, on Wednesday night, 17 June 2026, the student system's nightly file doesn't come: a full disk on its side holds it. At three, the night's update builds the graduation list from what was there, the file of the night before: 689 graduates, and every check green, even the check against the exam boards' own lists, which come in the same transfer and were a day old too. Missing: 23 graduates the boards approved on Wednesday. On Thursday at 8 am, with the list due at the printer at five, Sam Okafor, who runs the platform, sees in Databricks' monitoring that the student system's tables are late, and dbt's freshness check says thirty hours. The level there was, a warning after a day, was set by the builders, agreed with nobody and never run at night. Malia Fonoti, who sends the list, holds the send; the file arrives at 11:40, the list is built again at 12:10 with 712 graduates, her team checks the 23 new names, and it goes at 3:20. The December before, six graduates were left off the same way, and nobody saw. On Monday, Malia and Sam meet Mei Tanaka, who owns the list: the certificates need that night's files when the list goes to the printer, seating the Great Hall can use a list a few days old, and Planning's count for the government is fine weeks later. The strictest sets the level, a day old at most at the send, agreed by Malia, owned by Mei, and written beside the list's columns in dbt as the first timeliness rule. From then on every row of the list carries its sources' age, shown first of all in the printer's file. Sam checks the age at each source, on when each night's file arrived: older than 24 hours, the check fails. Each night the update runs it first; if it fails, Sam and Malia hear at once, and the list is still built, saying its age. The send to the printer runs the same check, and waits if it fails. The data isn't stopped; the send is. The volunteers can't stop a storm: they make sure the warning arrives in time, in words people understand. A list can't make a late file come: it can say how old it is, and the send can wait until it's fresh.

## The reference, and practice

The module's reference is DAMA-DMBOK's data quality chapter as the map, with Danette McGilvray's *Ten Steps* and Thomas Redman's practice as the spine, and the quality movement underneath (the module's *Decided* table records why). For this film, five bodies of knowledge, none taught:

| Body of knowledge | What it adds | Where the film uses it |
|---|---|---|
| DAMA's timeliness (DAMA UK's *The six primary dimensions for data quality assessment*, 2013; DAMA-DMBOK, second edition, 2017) | Timeliness as a dimension of its own: data that represents reality from the point in time it's needed | *How fresh, for what*: the six dimensions as *Rules that run* left them, timeliness in pencil until its first rule |
| ISO/IEC 25012:2008's currentness | Data "of the right age" in a specific context of use: how old is fine depends on the use | Not named; it's the film's "how fresh each use needs it" |
| Service levels, as site reliability engineering and data contracts write them | A level agreed with the people who depend on a service, measured, checked, with what happens when it's missed decided in advance | *How fresh, for what* and *Warn first, then stop the send*: one level, agreed, owned and checked |
| The Open Data Contract Standard (Bitol, a Linux Foundation project; the project pins v3.1.0) | A quality rule with the dimension `timeliness`, and a product's latency as `slaProperties` | *How fresh, for what*: the rule in the list's contract |
| dbt's source freshness, Databricks' data quality monitoring and Lakeflow Jobs (their docs, checked on the day) | How old each source is, from when its data arrived; which tables are late; a job's tasks, the condition each runs on, and whom a failed task tells | *Thirty hours* and *Warn first, then stop the send* |

**Where the film adopts, extends or differs.** It adopts DAMA's timeliness as a dimension of its own, and ISO's idea that the right age depends on the use: one list, three clocks. It extends them with the practice service levels teach: the level is agreed with the people who use the data, owned by the person who answers for it, checked by a machine every night, and what happens when it's missed is decided in advance, warn first and then stop the step that can't be undone. It differs from monitoring-first teaching: a platform can see that a table is late, as Databricks' monitoring does, but only its users can say when late is too late, so the agreed level comes first and the tools check it. It measures age at the source, from when each file arrived, not from the newest row, whose date is a business date that can be days old on a quiet night. And it uses one level where dbt offers two: a dbt warning exits as a success, so it tells nobody; the warning is a failed task that tells two people, while the build goes on.

**Practice (the module's plan and brief, 5 November 2026).** The brief asks for three habits a practitioner will recognise: agree how fresh each use needs the data, say "as of" wherever it's shown, and check freshness at the source, warning first, then stopping the send, not the data. A practitioner will recognise the night, too: a list built on time from yesterday's file, every check green; a freshness level set by the builders as a default, agreed with nobody who uses the data, and run by hand when someone thinks of it; a warning that goes to a log nobody reads. The film's numbers are believable ones: a nightly file a morning late, a level of 24 hours at the send that a nightly file meets, and a list that waits three hours for its file and still goes before five.

**What the film leaves to others.** How fresh data must be decides how it moves: that's *Hot, warm, cold*'s (*Modern data basics*), named once. A stale number labelled, and why stale and labelled beats fresh and wrong, is *Too good to be true*'s, named once. The scorecard and its dimensions are *Rules that run*'s; a data contract between systems, and its last good data, are *Silent change*'s; measuring and improving the list week after week is *Quality as a habit*'s, the next series.

- **The 1970 cyclone.** Frank, N. L., and S. A.

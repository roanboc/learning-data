## The promise

Every line passes two tests: anyone who has asked a chatbot the same question twice can follow it, and a data engineer, or a psychologist who studies memory, finds nothing wrong in it. An agent invents, and changes its answer. So do we: our memory doesn't play back a recording, it rebuilds itself each time we remember, fills its gaps with what seems likely, and bends to the question we're asked. Three thousand years ago, with nothing written down, the reciters of the Rigveda built for exactly that: every hymn was learned in several patterns whose words had to agree, so a slip showed before it was passed on, and the words have come down almost unchanged. At the university, the agent gives Clara three answers in two days, each as sure as the last, and cites a review that doesn't exist; and Clara is sure of last year's number too, and wrong. The team blames nobody. It checks the work, the same way for people and agents: every answer shows where it came from, every number is reached a second way, what's been checked is written down outside any one chat or any one head, and a person approves. The second way finds three students counted twice, and the agent changes its answer for a reason it can show. **Trust no mind blindly. Check the work.**

## The story in one paragraph

Northern India, about three thousand years ago. Families of poets and priests keep the Rigveda, more than a thousand hymns, their most sacred words, and nothing is written down: every hymn lives in memory, passed from teacher to student. But memory changes; a slip that's taught becomes the hymn, and with no page to check against, in a few generations the families could lose their hymns for good. So, over the centuries, their teachers built several patterns for every hymn: as it's recited, word by word, in pairs, and braided, back and forth. The words had to agree in every pattern, so a word that slipped would show. It worked: three thousand years later, reciters still learn the hymns this way, and the words have come down almost unchanged; scholars compare them to a recording. It's the same with data: every mind changes, a person's and an agent's, and checked more than one way, a change shows before anyone acts on it. Today, at the university, Clara, in Planning, is writing the paper on where two new advisers for first-year students should go, and needs to know which faculty lost the most first-years in the first semester. On Monday, the agent tells Jun: Engineering and IT, 131. On Tuesday, in a new chat, it tells Clara: Health, 126, "as the university's 2025 retention review shows"; Clara asks "are you sure?", and it apologises and says Business, 140. There's no 2025 retention review. And Clara was sure Health lost the most last year too; her own paper from last year says Engineering and IT, and Health was the year before. Memory doesn't play back; it rebuilds (Bartlett, 1932: a man who retold a folk tale six and a half years on added a totem and a pilgrimage, and was surest of exactly those), and the question shapes it (Loftus and Palmer, 1974: "smashed" brought faster guesses, and broken glass that was never there). A model doesn't play back either: it writes likely words, may take another route in a new chat, fills a gap with something likely, and gives way when doubted. So nobody is blamed; the team checks the work. Every answer shows where it came from; every number is reached a second way, and the data platform's enrolments and the student system's own report first disagree, 134 and 131, because three students were counted twice; the agent says so rather than picking one, and once it's fixed, changes its answer, with the reason. Monday's answer was right after all, but without its evidence nobody could tell. A plain check now looks for anyone counted twice every night; what's been checked is written down for the whole team, outside any one chat; and Clara reads the evidence and signs, because the agent never approves. Next semester, the two new advisers start in Engineering and IT. The reciters never asked whose memory was best: every hymn was checked, every time. Trust no mind blindly; check the work.

## The reference, and practice

The plan names two studies as the film's reference, and the film teaches what they found rather than a theory's vocabulary. The others below are worth knowing; the film uses what they share and names only the two, plus one study of models, by its year.

| Body of knowledge | What it adds | Where the film uses it |
|---|---|---|
| Reconstructive memory (Bartlett, *Remembering*, 1932) | Remembering is "an imaginative reconstruction", built from a few details and what we already know; retellings grow shorter, more familiar, and gain inventions their tellers are sure of | Chapter 3, and the line "we rebuild it from a few details, and fill the rest with what seems likely" |
| The question shapes the memory (Loftus and Palmer, 1974) | Words in a question change what people estimate and, a week later, what they remember seeing | Chapter 3; "both bend to the question" |
| Source monitoring (Johnson, Hashtroudi and Lindsay, 1993) | We judge where and when a memory came from as we recall it, and can get it wrong | Clara's two years joined into one (named only in this sheet) |
| Why models hallucinate (Kalai, Nachum, Vempala and Zhang, 2025) | Models guess when uncertain, "producing plausible yet incorrect statements", because training and tests reward a guess over "I don't know" | "Where neither knows, something likely fills the gap, and comes out as sure as a fact" |
| The FlipFlop experiment (Laban and others, 2023); sycophancy (Sharma and others, 2023) | Asked "are you sure?", ten models changed their answer 46% of the time, and were right less often after; models tend to match what the user seems to believe | "In a 2023 study, models asked "are you sure?" changed their answer almost half the time" |
| Checking a model's answer by asking again (self-consistency, Wang and others, 2022; SelfCheckGPT, Manakul and others, 2023) | Answers a model knows tend to agree across tries; invented facts tend to diverge | Behind "may take another route to another answer"; the film's checks go further, to the evidence |
| Blameless reviews (Allspaw, 2012; the site reliability practice since) and just culture (Reason, 1997) | Ask what happened and why, not who to blame, so people report what went wrong | "Nobody is blamed. The team checks the work instead" |

**Practice.** What practitioners recognise: a number reconciled from two sources before it goes to a decision; a query and its rows attached to an answer, so anyone can follow it back; a uniqueness check on the keys a count depends on, run every night; a decision log or team notes kept where everyone can read them, not in one person's chat; four eyes on anything that changes a decision; and, from working with agents, an answer that comes back different in a new session, a source cited that doesn't exist, and a correct answer abandoned when the user pushes back.

**The author's view** (the plan, 3 November 2026): inventing and changing their mind is not the real complaint about agents, since our memory is volatile too; the answer is evidence and checks, for people and agents alike. The module's rules hold throughout: agents are never approvers, their review is only for reference; memory is kept outside a single chat and shared by the team; tools read with the fewest rights (here, read only); and where a check can be a plain, deterministic one, it is.

### 4 · Rebuilt, not replayed · 4:02–4:59

**Narration.** Our memory doesn't play back a recording. Each time, we rebuild it from a few details, and fill the rest with what seems likely. A model doesn't play one back either. It writes likely words, one at a time, from what it learned and what it's given. Ask again in a new chat, and it may take another route to another answer. Where neither knows, something likely fills the gap, and comes out as sure as a fact. Both bend to the question, too. In a 2023 study, models asked "are you sure?" changed their answer almost half the time. None of this is lying. It's how memory and models work. The trouble isn't a mind that changes. It's a change we can't see the reason for.

## Rigour to check before recording

Checked on 3 November 2026; the sources are listed below.

- **The Rigveda, and who kept it.** The Vedic texts "were orally composed and transmitted, without the use of script, in an unbroken line of transmission from teacher to student that has been formalized early on" (Witzel, 2006). They were composed "in northern India, at first in the Greater Punjab and later on also in more eastern areas", "between c.1500 BCE and c.500-400 BCE", "by members of various clans of poets", whose hymns "belonged to them and were transmitted by them"; most were "intended to be recited at the yearly Soma ritual" (Witzel, 2006). "The RV contains 1028 hymns" (Witzel, 2006); other sources give about 10,600 verses. The film's "about three thousand years ago" is the time the families kept the collection, within Witzel's range; its "families of poets and priests" is his "clans of poets", and UNESCO's "Brahmin priests", without naming the class; "their most sacred words" because the Vedas are the oldest and most sacred texts of the tradition, recited at its rites. No absolute dating exists for any Vedic text (Witzel, 2006), so the film says "about".
- **Why the problem was real.** With no writing, a word changed in one recitation and taught onwards would become the text: the tradition says its methods exist "to preserve the unwritten Vedas in their original form" and "to make sure that not a syllable was changed in chanting" (*Hindu Dharma*, the talks of the Shankaracharya of Kanchi, 1907 to 1994, chapter "Methods of Chanting"); UNESCO: "To ensure that the sound of each word remains unaltered, practitioners are taught from childhood complex recitation techniques". The film's "a word can slip" and "a slip becomes the hymn" are the reason the tradition itself gives; "Each time we remember something, we rebuild it" is the film's own explanation, from Bartlett (below), not a claim about what the reciters believed. The fear is the film's plain words for the tradition's aim; no source tells of a hymn that was lost this way.
- **The patterns, and who built them.** Staal's notes for the recordings *The Four Vedas* (Folkways FE 4126): "On the basis of the padapāṭha a number of special recitations or Vikṛti recitations have been constructed. The original purpose of these constructions must have been that the sacred text should be preserved by different mnemonic techniques, designed to prevent the reciters' forgetting even a single word"; "The oldest of the Vikṛti recitations is the Krama recitation, which is at least 2500 years old". Writing a verse's words as letters, Staal gives saṃhitā "abcde", pada "a / b / c / d / e /", krama "ab / bc / cd / de /" and jaṭā "abbaab / bccbbc / cddccd / deedde /". The film shows four of the eleven recitations: as it's recited (saṃhitā), word by word (pada), in pairs (krama) and braided (jaṭā, which the Kanchi talks describe as "going back and forth"; Ramaswami, cited by Wikipedia, as "braided together"). The word-by-word recitation is credited by the tradition to Śākalya and the pairs to sages named by Pāṇini, and the braided ones came later, after the fifth century BCE (Wikipedia's *Vedic chant*, after Filliozat, 2006); so the film says "over the centuries, their teachers built several patterns", not that they did so three thousand years ago.
- **"The words had to agree in every pattern, so a word that slipped would show."** The Kanchi talks: "The words tally in all these methods of chanting and there is the assurance that the original form will not be altered." Filliozat (2006, pp. 138–140, as cited by Wikipedia): each text was recited in a number of ways "to ensure that the different methods of recitation acted as a cross check on the other". The film simplifies how a slip shows: in the pairs every word is said twice, once with the word before and once with the word after, and in the braid each pair three times, so a slip would have to be made the same way every time, in every pattern, to pass; and a teacher hears it. The boy corrected by a raised hand is the film's picture of that, not a described custom. The Ṛgveda Prātiśākhya, a phonetic treatise of about the fifth century BCE, opens its chapter on teaching with the teacher reciting for his pupils, "one or even two pupils" sitting "to his right", facing "the East, the North or the North East" (Staal's notes); the film seats them so.
- **"It worked", and "a recording".** Witzel (2006): oral transmission "insured an impeccable textual transmission (for exceptions see below); it is something like a tape recording of c.1500-500 BCE. Not just the actual words, but even the long-lost musical (tonal) pitch accent ... has been preserved up to the present." Staal's notes: "the texts themselves ... have for more than two milleniums remained surprisingly unchanged", while the way the accents are sung has changed from place to place; so the film says "the words have come down almost unchanged", not that the chanting sounds as it did. UNESCO inscribed the *Tradition of Vedic chanting* (India) on the Representative List of the Intangible Cultural Heritage of Humanity in 2008 (proclaimed in 2003), for "the ingenious techniques employed by the Brahmin priests in preserving the texts intact over thousands of years"; it also says that "only thirteen of the over one thousand Vedic recitation branches have survived", and that four schools are under threat. The film's "reciters still learn the hymns this way" is UNESCO's present tense; it doesn't say the tradition is safe.
- **Drawn, not copied.** The reciters' white cloth and the hair knotted on the right are from the Rigveda itself, 7.33.1, on the family of poets who made its seventh book ("white-robed", "with hair tied on the right"). The pipal tree is the Rigveda's *aśvattha*. Houses of wood and thatch, a grass mat, bare feet. No sacred thread, mark or symbol is drawn, since the film can't be sure which belong to the period. No Sanskrit on screen and no chant in the sound (PLAYBOOK, *One language on screen*; AGENTS.md, *Rights and respect*). The tiles a, b, c and d are Staal's notation, not the Rigveda's words.
- **Bartlett, 1932.** Read in *Remembering: A Study in Experimental and Social Psychology* (Cambridge University Press, 1932; the 1995 reissue). In the Method of Repeated Reproduction, "A subject was given a story ... He attempted a first reproduction usually after an interval of 15 minutes, and thereafter gave further reproductions at intervals of increasing length"; twenty subjects, "seven being women and the rest men", were given *The War of the Ghosts*, "adapted from a translation by Dr Franz Boas of a North American folk-tale" (Boas's *Kathlamet Texts*, Bureau of American Ethnology Bulletin 26, 1901, pp. 184–5), chosen because it "belonged to a level of culture and a social environment exceedingly different from those of my subjects". So the film says "people", not students, and "a folk tale from a culture far from their own", and draws none of the tale. "The more familiar 'boat' once replaces 'canoe'; hunting seals becomes merely 'fishing'." Six and a half years after his first reading, one subject, met again by chance, wrote a version with a totem, filial piety, a pilgrimage and a dark forest, none of them in the story: "There is a good deal of invention, and it was precisely concerning his inventions that the subject was most pleased and most certain. The totem, the filial piety, the pilgrimage—these were what he regarded as his most brilliant re-captures". The film names two of them. Bartlett's summary: "Remembering is not the re-excitation of innumerable fixed, lifeless and fragmentary traces. It is an imaginative reconstruction, or construction, built out of the relation of our attitude towards a whole active mass of organised past reactions or experience, and to a little outstanding detail ... It is thus hardly ever really exact". The film's "we rebuild it from a few details, and fill the rest with what seems likely" says this in plain words. "A century ago" because his experiments on remembering ran from the 1910s, before the book of 1932; the corner says "about 1920".
- **Loftus and Palmer, 1974.** E. F. Loftus and J. C. Palmer, "Reconstruction of automobile destruction: An example of the interaction between language and memory", *Journal of Verbal Learning and Verbal Behavior* 13 (1974), 585–589; students of the University of Washington, in Seattle. Experiment 1: 45 students saw seven short films of traffic accidents; the question "About how fast were the cars going when they (smashed / collided / bumped / hit / contacted) each other?" drew mean estimates of 40.8 mph for smashed and 34.0 for hit. Experiment 2: 150 students saw a one-minute film with a four-second multiple-car accident; 50 were asked with "smashed", 50 with "hit", 50 not asked about speed; a week later, asked "Did you see any broken glass?", 16 of 50 in the smashed group said yes (32%), 7 of 50 in the hit group (14%), and 12% of the others; there was no broken glass in the film. The film's bars show experiment 1's means, rounded (34 and 41 mph), and its "a third of them" is experiment 2's 32%. The figures were checked against two summaries of the paper, Simply Psychology's and Themantic Education's (both read 3 November 2026): they agree on every number the film uses, except experiment 1's mean for smashed (40.8 in one, 40.5 in the other), which is 41 either way, rounded as the film shows it; the second also gives 10.46 and 8.00 mph for experiment 2's smashed and hit groups, so in that experiment too the second group guessed faster. The paper itself is behind the publisher's paywall and wasn't read.
- **Clara's memory.** That a memory can keep its content and lose where or when it came from, and join two times into one, is what research on source monitoring describes (Johnson, Hashtroudi and Lindsay, *Psychological Bulletin* 114, 1993, 3–28). The film doesn't name it.
- **How a model writes, and why it changes its answer.** A language model writes one word (strictly, one token) at a time, each picked from the words its training makes likely given everything before it, the question and whatever it's given included; picked with a little chance, as most assistants are set up to do, the same question can come back differently, and an agent can take another route through its tools. A new chat starts without the last one unless it's given it. The film says "likely words" and "may take another route to another answer", and makes no claim about any product's settings.
- **"Where neither knows, something likely fills the gap, and comes out as sure as a fact."** For people, Bartlett's subject, surest of his inventions (above). For models: "Like students facing hard exam questions, large language models sometimes guess when uncertain, producing plausible yet incorrect statements instead of admitting uncertainty", because "the training and evaluation procedures reward guessing over acknowledging uncertainty" (Kalai, Nachum, Vempala and Zhang, *Why Language Models Hallucinate*, arXiv 2509.04664, 4 September 2025). The invented "2025 retention review" is that kind of guess, and the agent that later says "the two ways disagree" instead of picking a number is the behaviour that paper argues for.
- **"In a 2023 study, models asked "are you sure?" changed their answer almost half the time."** Laban, Murakhovs'ka, Xiong and Wu, *Are You Sure? Challenging LLMs Leads to Performance Drops in The FlipFlop Experiment* (arXiv 2311.08596, 14 November 2023): ten models, seven classification tasks; "models flip their answers on average 46% of the time and ... all models see a deterioration of accuracy between their first and final prediction, with an average drop of 17%". The film dates it, since newer models may do better; Sharma and others, *Towards Understanding Sycophancy in Language Models* (arXiv 2310.13548, 2023), found the same tendency to match what the user seems to believe in five assistants.
- **None of this is lying.** Neither a false memory nor a model's invention is told to deceive: Bartlett's subject was "pleased and satisfied" with his version; Kalai and others call model hallucinations errors that arise "through natural statistical pressures". The film's "It's how memory and models work" doesn't say the two work the same way inside: it says both rebuild rather than replay, and that's the only likeness it draws. *What they tell us about ourselves*, later in the series, asks how far the likeness goes.
- **The checks.** Each is common practice, not a product feature: an answer with its query and rows attached; a number reconciled from two independent sources (here, the data platform's enrolments and the student system's own report, both read only, as tool servers get the fewest rights in this module); a uniqueness check on the student key the count depends on, run every night (the module's rule: automate deterministically first, and keep agents for what isn't deterministic); a shared record of what's been checked, outside any one chat (the module's rule: memory is kept outside a single chat and shared by the team); and a person who approves (the module's rule: agents are never approvers, their review is for reference only). Nothing here reaches production: the number goes into Clara's paper.
- **Three students counted twice.** Invented, and common: a student who changes programme in the first semester can have two enrolment rows, one ended and one current, and a count that doesn't take the latest counts the student twice. The film doesn't say which; the rigour of the picture is that the second way caught it, not how it happened.
- **Invented, and believable:** the question, the paper, the numbers (Engineering and IT 131, the platform's 134, Health 126, Business 140, and last year's and the year before's leaders), the days, the "2025 retention review", and the two advisers are the film's. Clara Jensen, in Planning, and Jun are the governance films' and the series' people. The faculties are the example project's four (Arts and Education, Business, Engineering and IT, and Health), as the series' other films have them. No real university or company is named; the university is the site's fictional one.
- **Names said aloud.** Checked by printing the voice's phonemes (3 November 2026). The voice says Rigveda "RIG-ved-uh", so `film.json` respells it "Rig vayda" (rig-VAY-duh); Frederic with the stress on its second syllable, so it's respelled "Fredric"; Jun as "Joon", as in every film; Bartlett, Loftus, Palmer, Cambridge and Seattle as they should be. Years and counts are written out in each line's `say`, as the analytics and governance films do ("nineteen seventy-four", "twenty twenty-three", "a hundred and thirty-one"), since the voice reads 1974 as "nineteen hundred seventy-four". The module's rule for "AI" (`\bA I\b` back to `AI`) is in `film.json` too, though the narration doesn't say "AI".

## The promise

Every line passes two tests: someone who has never run a data platform can follow it, and a platform lead or a data engineer finds nothing wrong in it. *Scripts became software* showed data work kept as software: versioned, reviewed, tested and deployed the same way every time. This film shows the platform that runs that software watching itself: an agent that watches its jobs, pipelines and tables, finds a failure, traces its cause through lineage, says what it touches and proposes a fix, which a person reviews and a second person approves before anything reaches production. It is light on purpose, as the module is: it names the idea, shows it once in the tools, gives the architectural advice (what it can trace, what to keep, who reviews and who approves) and hands over to the official docs, linked on its page.

**Takeaway:** "The platform proposes; a person approves."

## The story in one paragraph

On 20 July 1969, Neil Armstrong and Buzz Aldrin fly their lunar module, Eagle, down to the Moon, its guidance computer steering. Five minutes into the descent a light comes on with a code, 1202: the computer has more work than time. The crew don't know the code and ask Houston. If the computer stopped, nothing would steer them; if Houston called an abort, they'd turn back without landing; someone had to decide in seconds. The computer's makers at MIT had built it to watch itself: with too much to do, it starts again, keeps the steering and its most important work, and drops the rest, and it raises the alarm so that people can decide. In Houston, the guidance officer, Steve Bales, 26, had just helped review its alarms: 1202 meant go on, unless it came too often or the descent went off course. Half a minute after the alarm, the word goes up: go. Four more alarms come, each a go, and Eagle lands. At the university, the claims job sends the hours casual staff have claimed to the payroll system on the Monday night before each pay day. On Monday 2 November 2026 at 15:02 a new version of the claims app lets tutors type their hours as they say them, `1:30` where it sent `1.50`, with its contract still saying decimal hours; at 22:03 the job fails, and nothing wrong reaches payroll. Genie ZeroOps, which Sam has watching the pay run's jobs and tables, finds the failure, traces its cause through lineage to the app's change, says what it touches and proposes a fix: by morning it's one insight in Sam's inbox, and nothing in production has changed. At 08:30 on Tuesday Sam checks it, asks it to push the fix to a new branch and opens a pull request; its tests run; at 09:05 Noor, who didn't write it, approves; at 09:20 the job runs again, payroll has the hours by ten, and on Thursday 856 casual staff are paid, Linh Nguyen among them. The fix mended the night, not its cause: Sam's pull request also asks Ingrid Holm, in HR, who owns the claims' data, to change the contract first next time. On Apollo 11, the cause took a night to find, a document between two systems that had said too little; for the next missions, the checklist changed and the software learned to check. Watching systems isn't new; what's new is that the platform does the first hours of the work, overnight. There are already standards, so don't reinvent the wheel: investigate, adapt, implement, iterate, starting from the official docs.

## The reference, and practice

The module's reference is DAMA-DMBOK as the map and the data engineering lifecycle of *Fundamentals of Data Engineering* as the spine (`films/data-that-moves/proposal.md`). This film sits in DAMA-DMBOK's *data storage and operations* and in the lifecycle's undercurrents, *DataOps*: observability, monitoring and incident response. It names site reliability engineering once, as the practice it builds on.

| Body of knowledge | What it adds | Where the film uses it |
|---|---|---|
| Reis and Housley, *Fundamentals of Data Engineering* (2022), DataOps as an undercurrent of the lifecycle | Automation, observability and monitoring, and incident response as part of every stage, not an afterthought: "data engineers should be the first to know" when something breaks, before the people who use the data | *The night the job failed* and *Watching overnight*: the job fails at night and the platform knows before payroll does (not named) |
| DAMA-DMBOK2 (2017), *Data storage and operations* | Operations as a data management area of its own: running, monitoring and restoring the systems data lives in, with people accountable for them | The whole film's area (not named) |
| Google, *Site Reliability Engineering* (2016), *Monitoring Distributed Systems* and *Postmortem Culture: Learning from Failure* | Alerts that tell a person something needs a person; people on call; blameless reviews after an incident that ask what in the system let it happen, not who | *Don't reinvent the wheel*: "site reliability engineers have done it for years, with alerts, people on call and blameless reviews"; the night told blamelessly |
| Data contracts, as *Silent change* and *Moving is easy, agreeing is the work* teach them (the Open Data Contract Standard in the example project's `governance/5-data-contracts/`) | The contract at an interface says what each field holds; a change to it is agreed before the system that sends the data changes | *The contract first* (not taught again) |
| Genie ZeroOps, Databricks' docs (updated 20 October 2026) | "A background agent that monitors your jobs, pipelines, and tables, detects failures and data quality issues, identifies the root cause, and proposes a fix", in four steps: detection, root cause analysis, impact analysis, resolution; "Genie ZeroOps doesn't change production data or code without human review"; for a job that runs code from Git, "it pushes a fix only after you explicitly ask it to and confirm the push", to a new branch, and "the change goes through your regular review process" | *Watching overnight* and *A person approves* |

**Where the film adopts, extends or differs.** It adopts the practice the references share: watch the systems, tell a person what needs a person, find the cause, and review without blame. It adopts the agent films' rule that an agent proposes and a person approves, and that a change to production is approved by someone other than its author. It extends the practice with what the tools now do: the first hours of an incident, the tracing through lineage and the impact on what comes after, done overnight by the platform, so the person's work in the morning is to check and decide. It differs from an incident review in where it ends: not with the fix but with the contract, because the night's cause was an agreement that changed without the contract changing first.

**Practice, from the author (the plan's insights and the author's rules for agents):** software practice is no longer new in data work; agents are never approvers; nothing reaches production without a person, and every change to production is approved by someone other than its author; tools show, they don't lead; and the module stays light on purpose, with the depth in the official docs. A practitioner will recognise the night: a source changes the form of one field, a cast fails at ten at night, and the morning goes on tracing it with a deadline at noon. What they won't recognise from older platforms is a morning that starts with the trace already done.

## What the example project has

The film adds the night to the example project (`films/analytics-engineering/project/`), on top of the claims app's source and staging that *One copy, one platform* adds, and the bundle and payroll's export that *Scripts became software* adds. - **The night's claim**, one row appended to `data/claims_app/claims.csv`: CL-104287, Linh Nguyen's consultation, typed at 15:40 on 2 November with `hours` as `1:30`.
- **The fix**, a macro, `macros/hr/decimal_hours.sql`, which reads `1.50` and `1:30` alike as 1.50 and lets any other form fail as before, and in `models/staging/claims_app/stg_claims_app__claims.sql` one line replaced in place (line 18), so no cited line moves.

- **Two decisions**, appended to `sources/claims_app/_claims_app__decisions.yml`: DEC-CLM-02, the hours are read in either form the app sends (agreed by Sam with Noor, 3 November 2026), and DEC-CLM-03, a change to what the app sends is agreed in its contract first (proposed, for Ingrid Holm to decide).
- **The night as a worked example**, `examples/the-night-the-claims-job-failed/`: its README, the build as it failed that night, the insight in its four steps, the fix as a patch, and Sam's pull request. Taking the patch out and building again shows the night fail.

### 2 · The night the job failed · 1:40–2:39

**Narration.** At the university, casual staff, paid by the hour, claim the hours they teach in an app. On the Monday before each pay day, at ten at night, the claims job builds the hours to pay, and sends them to the payroll system. On Monday the second of November, at 3:02 in the afternoon, a new version of the app goes live. Tutors can now type their hours as they say them: 1:30 for an hour and a half, where it sent 1.50. ## Sound

The module's palette for this film (`palettes.md`): two **terpsitones**, both made in `source/tools/score.py`, since the site's instruments have none. The lead is the terpsitone, the dance platform of 1932 whose space senses a body moving above it and turns it into tone: a platform that senses, as Genie ZeroOps senses the jobs and tables it watches. It plays plain stepped notes, never sliding, with no vibrato: a tone near a sine with a soft breath of air at its edge, in the middle register. Its partner, the thread's choice, is a second terpsitone, an octave and more lower and rounder, as a second dancer would play it: the people who decide. It sounds only where a person decides, reviews or approves, and under the story's turns, never for the agent; a person's decision is its rising fourth, F to B-flat. It was chosen as *Scripts became software* chose a second ophicleide, because every plucked or struck colour the site lacks either belongs to another culture or sounds like one the site has; a clavioline was set aside because it sounds like the site's ondes Martenot. B-flat major for the platform, the people and what they agree; G minor for what goes wrong; just intonation from B-flat. The mark: a still note that steps up when something moves, then settles back (F, G, F).

- **Music under the opening story,** then only at a chapter's turn, under a moment on screen or in a wordless pause, about once every 10 seconds at most. In the opening: the descent stepping down, the mark at the PROG light, G minor for what either way would cost, the restart that keeps the steering, the rule agreed, a person's fourth with the go, four steps rising with the four alarms, the landing, the mark as the computer says what was wrong, G minor at the night's failure, and the title. After it: one note for the claims job, the app's new version, the contract's agreement and G minor at 22:03; the mark as the agent finds the failure, a fix proposed and E-flat major at the inbox, a person's turn; the morning, Noor's approval, pay day and the mark at "The platform proposes"; G minor for a fix that mended the night but not its cause, the decision proposed, B-flat major as the contract reaches everyone who reads the hours, Apollo again on the lead's F alone, and the checklist; four steps rising as the four steps light, as the four alarms did; and the end card, the mark over the partner's B-flat.
- **No urgent pulse** (`film.json` lists no chapter): the night is told calmly, because the platform handles it calmly.
- **Effects,** one at a time, each tied to something on screen, soft and below about 2.5 kHz: a breath of air as the computer's smaller work falls away; a soft low thud at touchdown, with the dust; a low knock at 22:03; a small lock when nothing wrong reaches payroll, when nothing in production has changed and when an agent never approves; a breath of air up the lineage, and a knock where the hours changed form; a soft knock as a card or window is set down; a tap for each claim Sam checks, each test, each owner's name and each of the four words; a soft click at Sam's confirm and as each task turns green; a page turned at the checklist. No beeps, tones or countdown for Apollo 11, and nothing borrowed from the mission's own sounds.
- Held notes at even levels (`_band`, `_held`); the mix at -16 LUFS; `quiet.py` (with `held.py`) passes.

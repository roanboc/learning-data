## The promise

Every line passes two tests: anyone who has seen a change break something nobody knew depended on it can follow it, and an experienced enterprise architect finds nothing wrong in it. Its idea: when every element of the map says who owns it and where it came from, the map can be tested as code is, on every change, and each rule's check is itself tested on a case that must fail, because a check that has never failed may be checking nothing. A proposed change can then be walked through everything it touches, down to the tables, naming who must agree, before anyone builds. An agent can draft that walk in minutes, but it's tested first on a change whose answer is known, and tested again whenever its model or its instructions change. The walk is checked against what's really running, since the map and the code say only how things are meant to run. People decide.

**Takeaway:** "Give every element an owner and a source, and test the map on every change, with a case that must fail. Walk each change through all it touches, check it against what's really running, and let the owners decide."

## The story in one paragraph

Babylon, over 2,600 years ago: along the river, King Nabopolassar's workmen are building a wall of baked brick to keep its water out. A wall like that was never done: king after king repaired it, on the same line, for centuries. Once a brick was laid among millions, nobody could tell who had made it or what it was for, and a king feared his work would be forgotten, or his words removed by a king who came after him; every stretch of wall needed to say who had built it, and what for. So on the edges of his baked bricks, his workmen stamped or wrote the king's name and the work the brick was for. One reads: "Nabopolassar, king of Babylon, … the one who built the embankment of the Arahtu River for the god Marduk, his lord." About two and a half thousand years later, archaeologists dug out the river wall, and its bricks still said which king built each stretch; in one stretch alone, they mapped sixteen of Nabopolassar's bricks, still in place. At the energy utility today, Tomás's map changes every day: 140 changes last month, each confirmed by its owner. Last year, planned work, when the network's crews switch a street off to mend its lines, moved to a new table, and the letters that warn households kept reading the old one: for three weeks, 1,900 households got the wrong date, and on the real day their power went off without warning. Nobody had done anything wrong; nobody could see what read the old table. Now the head of retail asks, in a ticket, for a text to each household two days before planned work. Every element on the map says who owns it and where it came from, so the map is tested on every change, each rule with a case that must fail: sending the network's list of homes with solar panels to retail must fail, and it does. The map agent can draft the walk of a change in minutes, but first it's tested on the social tariff, which Tomás walked by hand: fourteen elements, five owners. It finds thirteen, missing the secure exchange that carries the agency's answers, because nothing on the map linked the agreement to it; the line is drawn and a check added, and run again, it finds all fourteen. Then it walks the new change down to the tables, stops where a new texting service has no owner until the head of retail takes it, and lists who must agree, each with its ID and evidence. Checked against the running estate, read-only, with personal and sensitive data masked, the walk finds a reader the map doesn't show: the contact centre's software keeps its own copy of phone numbers, and one number in eleven differs. The head of retail decides the customer system is the source, and the copy goes on the map, with an owner. In April, the first texts go out, to 3,400 households, each two days before the crews reach its street, every one with the date the crews have.

Where the film differs: fitness functions usually test a system's code and its running qualities; here the same idea tests the architecture model itself, each rule of the map with a case that must fail. How the agent walks a change (search, retrieval, how a model reasons) stays in the AI agents module; the film says only what it reads, what it drafts, and how it's tested. The agent changes nothing: a person decides, and the copy goes on the map with an owner. No product is named, and no law: the rule at the wall cites "the regulator's code", as the map records it.

Practice, from the author and the series' plan: give every element of the map an owner and a source; test the map on every change, as code is; give every rule's check a case that must fail; let a change that fails a check wait until it's fixed, or its owner says why; before relying on an agent's walks, test it on a change whose answer is known, and test it again whenever its model or its instructions change; when it misses something, ask whether the gap is the map's; walk each change down to the tables, naming who must agree, each with an ID and evidence; check the walk against what's really running, read-only, with personal and sensitive data masked; and let the owners decide. Every one of these is said or shown in the film.

- **The story.** *The Royal Inscriptions of Nabopolassar (625–605 BC) and Nebuchadnezzar II (604–562 BC), Kings of Babylon, Part 1* (Jamie Novotny and Frauke Weiershäuser, RINBE 1/1, 2024, [open access](https://epub.ub.uni-muenchen.de/118421/), read again on 12 November 2026).

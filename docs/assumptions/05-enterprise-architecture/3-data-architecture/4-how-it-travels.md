| 2 | Who went on strike in October 1972, and why? | Truck owners, against a plan for a state trucking company, joined by shopkeepers, bank clerks and others (Medina 2006, p. 593 and fn 72). The film names no side and judges no one: it tells what the strike stopped, and what the lines carried. | Chapter 1 |
| 3 | Could shops and factories really run out of essential goods? | Said with care, "could run out", from secondary sources only (*Americas Quarterly*, 2019, and general accounts of the strike): Medina, the series' main source, tells the centre's work, not the shortages. The film doesn't say how many ran out. | Chapter 1 |
| 4 | "The government needed to hear, as it happened, where goods were short and which trucks could still run"? | The film's reading of Medina (2006, pp. 593–594): during the strike, the telex network let the government follow what was happening across the country and send the trucks still running where they were needed. Said as what the government needed, not as a quotation. | Chapter 1 |
| 5 | Did the centre answer the telexes, and did messages go "in both directions"? | Medina (2006, pp. 593–594) has the team and officials handling about two thousand telexes a day, from the north to the south, and the government directing the trucks still running. That they answered them, and that the lines now carried messages both ways, is the film's reading of that work; the strips' words ("GOODS SHORT", "TRUCKS COMING") are illustrative, since no source shows a standard telex form. | Chapters 1 and 8 |
| 6 | About 200 trucks against 40,000? | Medina (2006, p. 594), citing Gustavo Silva: about two hundred trucks still in service kept goods moving, against the effects of some forty thousand on strike. The film says "about" both times, and "against the effects of", as he does. | Chapter 1 |
| 7 | Is "Fernando Flores, who had started Cybersyn at CORFO" fair? | Yes: Flores, then at CORFO, wrote to Stafford Beer in July 1971 and began the project (Medina 2006, the article's opening pages; *Between the business and the systems*). He set up the emergency operations centre during the strike (p. 593). | Chapter 1 |
| 8 | Eleven paths across the wall, four on the map? | Invented for the film's utility: the four on the map are the readings file, the new connection points, the planned outages and the life support list; the seven nobody drew are the database link, the nightly script, a copied table, a weekly spreadsheet, an emailed fault list, a shared folder and a report someone runs. | Chapters 1, 2, 7 and 8 |
| 9 | Why is the complaints team's link a problem, if it was made for a good reason? | It shows every connection point's readings, the 230,000 served by other retailers among them, and the wall is there so customer information stays with the business that collected it. The film doesn't blame the team: the link was made years ago so they could check a reading during a call, and they keep that, reading the same published messages as the rest of customer service. | Chapters 2 and 5 |
| 10 | Is the nightly script *Rules with a home*'s? | Yes, the same utility's: in March it wrote for months to a queue nothing read, and about 1,900 bills went uncorrected (*The map before the data*). Here it's one of the paths nobody drew, and its path becomes the published flow of replaced readings, which is why five paths become flows, the four on the map and this one, and six are closed. | Chapters 2 and 7 |
| 11 | How does an estimate get replaced? | The meter stores its readings while it can't send them, the network estimates the missing days, billing uses the estimate, and the network replaces it when the stored readings arrive. How estimates are made, flagged and replaced differs by market (*Rules with a home*'s row: Ofgem; ESB Networks; the National Electricity Market); the film keeps the pattern. | Chapter 3 |
| 12 | Why those five parts on the agreed list? | They're what a retailer needs to correct a bill and no more: the connection point by its network ID (not the customer's name, which retail already holds), the days, the estimate it replaces, the readings, and when it was replaced. Voltage, power quality and meter events stay with the network. The agreed list is the one from *Who does it, and where meaning changes*. | Chapter 3 |
| 13 | Why publish and subscribe, and not hub and spoke? | The three questions: many readers (every retailer, and in each, billing and customer service), acting at different times (at the next bill, during a call), and each allowed to see only its own customers'. Publishing once, with each retailer receiving only its own, fits all three; a hub would also work if it filtered per retailer, and the film doesn't say hubs are wrong, only which shape fits this flow. | Chapters 4 and 5 |
| 14 | Why "within the hour"? | Nobody acts on it sooner: billing at the next bill, customer service during a call that comes days later. Near real time would cost more and give nobody anything; the contract says within an hour of the replacement. | Chapters 5 and 6 |
| 15 | Who agrees the contract? | The two teams at the flow's ends, Grace's network operations, which sends it, and billing, which reads it, with nobody else's sign-off, since it keeps to the agreed list: the site's rule that contracts between teams are agreed team to team (the author, 12 November 2026), with an enterprise contract only for canonical tables. On the map, the owners at its ends are Grace and the head of retail, billing's head. | Chapters 6 and 7 |
| 16 | What does the contract say? | Four terms practitioners expect first, plus who agreed it: what crosses and what each part means; how soon (within an hour of the replacement); what happens when one is late or wrong (the network's team is told that day and corrects it at home, never in the copy); and how it changes (a part may be added, but none removed or renamed without a new version, and readers are told first), as the example project's contracts change (decided 20 October). | Chapter 6 |
| 17 | How is the flow drawn on the map? | In ArchiMate, as the series draws it: one business object, the meter reading, over the wall, realised by two data objects, its home in meter data and its copy in billing, with a flow between them named with its shape and its contract, and an owner under each end. | Chapter 7 |
| 18 | Does the film teach how the flow is built? | No: connectors, events and pipelines are the series *Data that moves*, named once at the end of chapter 7. | Chapter 7 |
| 19 | The utility's numbers? | Invented: 540,000 connection points, 230,000 with other retailers; about 1,900 bills; the Mendozas at 14 Quarry Lane, CP-0061420, and the other retailers' CP-0047730 and CP-0052219; 20 to 28 November; 29 November, 10:14 and 10:15; 2,140 estimates replaced, all but nine within the hour, those nine the same day. | Chapters 2, 3, 5 and 8 |

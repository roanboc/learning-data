## The promise

Every line passes two tests: someone who has never moved data between two systems can follow it, and a data architect or a platform lead finds nothing wrong in it. It is the module's opening film, so it says the module's promise first: moving data, running a platform, serving data to people and agents, and reading documents all used to take whole teams; Databricks and dbt now do much of it; each film shows what got easier, what still needs people to agree, and where the official docs take you next. It says outright that the module is light on purpose: courses and the official docs go deep, and stay up to date; these films give the architectural advice. Then its own idea. Data reaches the platform in two shapes, events and batches, which have had names for twenty years; what changed is how little it now takes to move them, five ways: a managed connector, a query in place, a mirror, a share and an API. What still needs people is the contract at each interface, where two systems meet: what each field means, who answers for it, what a deletion means, and what's personal. **Let the tools move it; spend your time on the contract.**

## The story in one paragraph

June 1883, on the Murray River, the border between New South Wales and Victoria. At Albury, a new bridge joins the railway from Sydney to the railway from Melbourne. But each colony had built its railway to its own gauge, the distance between the rails, and Victoria's were about a hand's length further apart, so no train could run on both. Every passenger changed trains at Albury, and every crate and bale of wool was lifted into another wagon, which cost travellers time and traders money on every journey. Each colony had its reasons: New South Wales had changed its gauge with a new engineer, and Victoria had kept the one both first chose. For 79 years, everything stopped at Albury. In 1962, Australia's government, with New South Wales and Victoria, opened a new line from Albury to Melbourne, built to one agreed gauge: freight ran straight through that January, passengers from April, and within a year the freight it carried had grown by a third. At the university, HR's contracts once reached Finance as a file each teaching period, and Rohan Desai's team spent two days working out what each column meant. Moving the data was never the hard part: agree what it means, once, and it passes straight through. The film's one contract is Linh Nguyen's, T-26188, to tutor HI101 this semester. HR recorded it on 14 July, and it reached the platform as one change; on 20 July, the first day of the teaching period, the HR system sent its whole file again. A managed connector, which Sam Okafor set up, brings the HR system's tables into bronze; a query in place reads Finance's general ledger in the finance system without copying it; a mirror shows the university's catalog in Microsoft Fabric, where Rohan works; a share gives a partner data where it lives; an API serves an app just what it asks for. What's left for people is the contract at each interface. Ingrid Holm, who owns the HR system's data, and Noor Rahman, the data architect, agree the HR system's: what `pay_type`'s three values mean (CON is continuing, not contract); who answers for it (Ingrid, by name); what a deletion means (a cancelled contract arrives as a change with its time, never as a row missing from the next file, and the platform closes it and keeps its history); and what's personal (names, birth dates and emails come across, marked personal; bank details and rates of pay never leave HR). They write it in the Open Data Contract Standard, and Jun Park's tests check every row against it. Back at Albury, the trains always ran; what took 79 years was agreeing the gauge. Now HR's contracts reach Rohan in Fabric the day they're made, meaning just what HR means. There are already standards for this, so don't reinvent the wheel: investigate, adapt, implement and iterate, starting from the official docs, and if these basics are still a struggle, look first at your governance or your tooling.

## The reference, and practice

The module's reference is DAMA-DMBOK as the map (data integration and interoperability is this film's area) and the data engineering lifecycle of *Fundamentals of Data Engineering* (Reis and Housley, 2022) as the spine; the module's *Decided* table records why. For this film, three bodies of knowledge, each named once and none taught:

| Body of knowledge | What it adds | Where the film uses it |
|---|---|---|
| *Enterprise Integration Patterns* (Gregor Hohpe and Bobby Woolf, 2003) | The names for how systems exchange data: events, messages, batches, and the channels between them | *What got easier*, in a line: the shapes data comes in have had names for twenty years |
| The Open Data Contract Standard (Bitol, a Linux Foundation project; the project pins v3.1.0) | One open way to write down what a dataset means where it's handed over: its fields and their meaning, its owner and support, its quality checks, its service levels, what's personal | *The work that's left*: the HR system's contract is written in it, and the example project holds it |
| Databricks' and Microsoft's docs (read 5 November 2026) | What a managed connector, a query in place (Lakehouse Federation), a mirrored catalog in Microsoft Fabric and a share (OpenSharing) do, and when the docs recommend each | *Five ways to move it* |

**Where the film adopts, extends or differs.** It adopts DAMA-DMBOK's view that integration is first about meaning and agreement, and only then about mechanics, and *Enterprise Integration Patterns*' names for the shapes data travels in. It differs on weight: DAMA-DMBOK gives the mechanics of extraction, transformation and loading a long chapter, and this film gives them one line ("What changed is how little it now takes to move them"), because the tools now carry them. Where a data contract is often taught as a producer's promise about a table's columns, the film keeps to the four things the module's plan names, and teaches a contract between systems in *When things go wrong*'s *Silent change*, which it names and doesn't teach again.

**Practice, from the author (the module's plan, 5 November 2026):** moving data is no longer the hard part, and his team mirrors Unity Catalog into Microsoft Fabric today, so the outbound way the film shows is the one practitioners use; the work that still needs people is agreeing what the data means where two systems meet; and there are standards for this already, so don't reinvent the wheel: investigate, adapt, implement, iterate. If these basics are still a struggle, the cause is usually governance or tooling, which other modules show. A practitioner will recognise the opening's data story: a file that arrives each period, whose columns someone has to decode again every time.

**What the film leaves to others.** How data moves through the platform, layer by layer, is *The Inner Life of Data*'s; changes to a contract, and a new value only one side understands, are *Silent change*'s; sharing outside without copies is *Products in open formats*' and *The marketplace*'s; the night a field changes and its contract doesn't is *A platform that watches itself*'s; how fresh each kind of data must be is *Hot, warm, cold*'s. Personal data in depth belongs to the module *Data protection and privacy*.

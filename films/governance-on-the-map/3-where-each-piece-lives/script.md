## The promise

Every line passes two tests: anyone who has found two copies of the same thing that disagree can follow it, and an experienced enterprise architect, data platform lead or data governance lead finds nothing wrong in it. Under the Joseon kings, historians at court wrote down what was said and done every day; when a king died, one office made the record of his reign, his annals, from their notes and printed them, every copy the same, kept in four archives, and nobody could change one, not even a king. In 1592, war came up the roads and three of the four archives burned. Only Jeonju's copy was left, and with it 175 years of the kingdom's memory: what was decided, by whom, and why. Two local scholars carried it into the mountains and guarded it for more than a year; after the war, the court printed the annals again from that one copy, in five sets kept far apart, four of them high in the mountains, and every few years a historian climbed to each archive, aired the books and wrote down what he found. The annals survive, complete, and anyone can read them today. Jeonju's copy could rebuild the rest because every copy was made from one source, one way, and nobody wrote in one. It's the same with data: a copy can be trusted when it's made from its home, one way, and checked. At the university, a planner's request to read Planning's product waits nine days with Jun, who built the table, because the platform's page still names him, typed by hand, though Clara has owned it since October. Yusuf counts seven places that hold governance's pieces; Noor puts each on her map as an application, with what it holds, under one rule: every piece has one home, where it's changed, and an owner, who decides it; every other place keeps a copy, made from its home one way, saying where it came from and when. Then the flows between them, each one way, and a check every night that finds any copy that has drifted. Each team still works in its own tool; what changes is that everyone knows which copy is theirs to change, and which only to read. The map becomes the plan for the next series, where each film builds a part of it in the tools. **One home for every piece. Every copy follows it.**

## The story in one paragraph

Korea, under the Joseon kings. Every day, historians at court write down what's said and done. When a king dies, one office makes the record of his reign, his annals, from their notes, and prints them; every copy is the same, kept in four archives, and nobody may change one, not even a king. In 1592, war comes up the roads, and three of the four archives burn. Only Jeonju's copy is left: if it burns too, 175 years of the kingdom's memory are gone, what was decided, by whom, and why. Something has to change. Two local scholars carry it into the mountains, and guard it, in turns, for more than a year. After the war, the court prints the annals again from that one copy, five sets, far apart, one in the capital and four high in the mountains, and every few years a historian climbs to each archive, airs the books, and writes down what he finds. The annals survive, complete: 472 years, twenty-five kings, 888 books, and today anyone can read them online, free. Jeonju's copy could rebuild the rest because every copy was made from one source, one way, and nobody wrote in one. It's the same with data: a copy can be trusted when it's made from its home, one way, and checked. In February, at the university, a planner in the Faculty of Business asks to read Planning's list of learners near an award, to plan next year's courses. On the data platform, the table's page names its owner as Jun, so her request goes to him, and waits: Jun only built it, and since October its owner is Clara, in Planning. Nine days later, Jun passes it on; had he said yes, someone who isn't the owner would have decided who may see it. The page was typed by hand when the table was built, and nobody kept it in step. Sam, who runs the platform, wants its pages to name the right owner, and asks Yusuf where governance's pieces live. Yusuf counts seven places: the architecture tool, with Noor's map; the glossary; the document library, with the policies and the council's minutes; the data team's repository, with owners, classes, tables, checks, decisions and each product's promise; the data platform, with who may read what and the record of who read it; the catalogue, where people find products; and the tracker, for issues and requests. Some pieces sit in more than one place, and one copy had drifted. Noor puts each on her map as an application, with what it holds, under one rule: every piece has one home, where it's changed, and an owner, who decides it; every other place keeps a copy, and says where it came from, and when. A product's owner lives in the data team's repository, and the catalogue and the platform's page copy it. As with the annals, nobody writes in a copy: a fix goes to its home, and every copy follows. Then the flows, each one way: definitions from the map to the glossary, and from the glossary to every tool that reads them; the map's concepts to the data team's tables; each column's class from the repository to the platform, where it sets who may read it; each product's promise from the repository to the catalogue. Nothing flows back. Every night, a check compares each copy with its home; the first night, it finds the old owner's name on the table's page, and wrong names on two more, and the sync puts them right by morning, and tells each owner what changed. Drift is something a check finds, not something a person remembers, as the historians climbed to each archive and wrote down what they found. Each team still works in its own tool, and everyone knows which copy is theirs to change, and which only to read; the planner's next request goes straight to Clara, who decides it in a day. Noor's map becomes the plan for the next series, *Governance you can use*. Give every piece of governance one home, and every copy can be trusted: everyone reads the same thing, and every request reaches the person who decides.

**Practice.** What practitioners recognise: the same owner, definition or class typed into three tools, and only one kept up; a platform's "owner" field that names whoever built the table, not whoever answers for it; requests that wait with the wrong person; a sync nobody owns that stops without anyone noticing; a reconciliation report nobody reads; and teams that won't give up their own tool, and needn't, once each knows which copy is theirs to change.

**The author's view** (20 October 2026): the films on the tools "would get the architectural context knowing there are multiple governance artifacts maintained in other repos and apps". This film draws that context, and hands it to *Governance you can use* as its brief. ## The look

*Watercolour*, the series' look since 17 October 2026, shared with the series on master and reference data, so the two look like one university.

- **Pencil and wash.** Every place is painted on paper, and people are drawn as the master data films draw them, with a soft wash of their side behind each one.
- **Only the university's places,** all of them the series' shared places: the floor of offices behind glass, the platform team's room and Sam's desk, the data team's room, Planning's room, Yusuf's office, Noor's room and her map, and a faculty's office (here, the Faculty of Business's planner, at a desk in the newer building). The film paints its own places for the opening: Joseon Korea, the court, an archive in a town, a mountain path, and an archive hall high in the mountains.
- **Joseon Korea, painted the same way,** with the place and year in the corner, checked against Korean sources: officials and historians in court robes and hats, scholars in plain robes; the annals as stitched books in wrapping cloth, kept in chests; an archive as a raised wooden hall with a tiled roof inside a walled compound, in the mountains among pines. A warm umber wash behind each person of the past. Nothing on screen is in Korean or Chinese: the annals' writing is drawn as columns of marks too small to read, and the corner names the place. No style, pattern or music borrowed from Korean culture: the story is told in the series' own voice.
- **Colour carries meaning,** as in the series: gold is governance's decisions, and the business side; cyan the technical side; green what runs in the work; amber what a check has found. On Noor's map, a home is drawn solid and a copy dashed, and every flow has one arrowhead.
- **No files, code or tool screens.** Where a tool appears, the film shows a plain page in the university's words, such as the table's page with its owner, and names applications for what they do: the architecture tool, the glossary, the document library, the data team's repository, the data platform, the catalogue, the tracker.
- **One diagram-like picture: Noor's map,** its application band close, where the seven places, their pieces and their flows go up. Everything else is people doing things: a request waiting, a card pinned, a night check walking the copies, a name put right.

## Rigour

Checked on 28 October 2026, before recording and again when the film was made; the sources are listed below. The Korean history is checked against Korean sources, as AGENTS.md asks for a story from another culture.

- **The annals.** The *Joseon wangjo sillok* (the Annals, or Veritable Records, of the Joseon Dynasty) record the reigns of twenty-five kings over 472 years, from Taejo (1392) to Cheoljong (1863): 1,893 chapters in 888 books. They're Korea's National Treasure and, since 1997, on UNESCO's Memory of the World register. The film says "472 years, twenty-five kings, 888 books"; "books" are the bound volumes (*chaek*).
- **How they were made.** Historians (*sagwan*) attended the court every day and wrote down what was said and done; when a king died, an office set up for the purpose (the *Sillokcheong*) compiled his annals from their drafts and other records. The early annals were copied by hand; from Sejong's time they were printed with movable type, and copies were kept in four archives, at the capital (the Chunchugwan), Chungju, Seongju and Jeonju. The film's "one office makes his annals from their notes, and prints them" and "kept in four archives" say this in plain words. A king could not read the annals of his own reign or his father's (Sejong was dissuaded from reading Taejong's): "nobody may change one, not even a king" is the film's plain words for that rule. Where later annals were revised (the *sujeong sillok*), the revision was kept beside the original, not written into it.
- **1592, and Jeonju's copy.** In the Japanese invasion of 1592 (the Imjin War), the archives at the capital, Chungju and Seongju burned; Jeonju's survived. Two local scholars of Jeongeup, An Ui and Son Hong-rok, with others, moved its annals (Taejo to Myeongjong, 1392 to 1567: 175 years) to Naejangsan, to a cave hermitage, and kept watch over them in turns, as their diary records, for about 370 days. The film shows the move and the watch, names them on screen only ("An Ui · Son Hong-rok", "local scholars"), and draws the cave and the chests plainly. Korea now marks 22 June as a day for people who protect cultural heritage, after their rescue.
- **Five sets, far apart.** From 1603 to 1606, the court printed the annals again from the Jeonju copy: three new printed sets, and with the Jeonju original and the proof copy, five, kept at the Chunchugwan in the capital and in four mountain archives (Myohyangsan, Taebaeksan, Odaesan and Manisan on Ganghwa; Myohyangsan's set later moved to Jeoksangsan, and Manisan's to Jeongjoksan). The film names the four mountains on tags in English letters, as "Mount Odae", "Mount Taebaek", "Mount Myohyang" and "Mount Mani".
- **The airing.** Every few years (often every two or three), a historian was sent to each archive to air the books (*poswae*), and wrote down what he found and what he aired. The film's "Every few years, a historian climbs to each archive, airs the books, and writes down what he finds" says this, and it's the film's picture of a regular check.
- **Today.** The National Institute of Korean History publishes the annals online, free, with a modern Korean translation: "Today, anyone can read them online, free." Some sets were lost or scattered in the 20th century (Odaesan's was taken to Japan in 1913 and mostly burned in 1923; its surviving books came back from 2006); complete sets survive, so the film's "The annals survive, complete" holds.
- **The opening's four beats.** The author's rule for every opening story (28 October 2026): the scenario, the court's historians and the annals printed from one office, every copy the same, in four archives; the problem, three archives burned in 1592, one copy left, and with it 175 years of what was decided, by whom and why; the solution, two scholars guarding it, and the annals printed again from it in five sets far apart, four in the mountains, with a historian checking each every few years; the benefits, the annals complete, read by anyone today, rebuilt because every copy came from one source, one way; and the same for data, "A copy can be trusted when it's made from its home, one way, and checked", which the film's end gives in its own terms.

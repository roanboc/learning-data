## The promise

Every line passes two tests: anyone who has asked a chatbot a question can follow it, and a neuroscientist or a machine-learning engineer finds nothing wrong in it. Nature has tried out designs for billions of years, and people have borrowed from it: the Wright brothers took a buzzard's twist for their wings, and Frank Rosenblatt modelled the perceptron on a nerve cell. That cell was a picture from 1888, when Santiago Ramón y Cajal made a stain work and drew separate cells, passing messages one way, where others saw one continuous net. Inside a nerve cell, a pulse is all or nothing, and learning is connections that change: in a jellyfish, a sea slug, a bee, an octopus, a finch and us. A brain doesn't take in everything; it fills its gaps with what's likely. Models borrowed an idea, not a brain: simple units that add up weighted inputs, in layers, their weights set by training. They write one likely word at a time, drawn with a little chance, so the same question can bring a different answer; a setting called temperature trades sticking to the patterns against new ways and more mistakes; and where a fact is missing, they fill the gap with a likely one, as fluent as the rest. **A likely answer isn't a checked one.**

## The story in one paragraph

In 1958, the psychologist Frank Rosenblatt showed a program that learned from examples, the perceptron, and modelled it on a nerve cell. Why look to nature? Because nature has been trying out designs for billions of years and keeping the ones that work: Wilbur Wright saw buzzards twist their wingtips to keep their balance, and the brothers built that twist into their wings; engineers still study the maple seed, which spins down like a tiny helicopter. For a machine that learns, nature had one working example, a brain; but in 1888 nobody could see how one was wired. Barcelona, 1888: Santiago Ramón y Cajal, a doctor who teaches anatomy, looks at thin slices of brain, and under the lens they're a thicket of fibres, too dense to follow. Many, Camillo Golgi among them, think the fibres are fused into one continuous net; if they are, nobody can say where a message starts, or how a brain learns, and when a stroke takes away someone's speech, doctors can't tell what it has broken, or whether it will heal. Golgi had found a stain that turns a few cells black, at random; Cajal makes it work reliably, on young birds' brains, where the thicket is thinner, and each black cell stands out whole. He draws separate cells, close but never joined, and argues that a message travels one way. In Berlin in 1889 the leading anatomists look down his microscopes and agree; in 1906 he and Golgi share the Nobel Prize, though Golgi still defends his net. The neuron became the starting point of brain science, and many medicines for the brain work at the gaps he drew. His picture gave machines a design: many simple cells, each listening to many others, passing one message on. Inside a nerve cell: signals come in on its branches; when enough arrive together it fires, a pulse that's always the same size; messengers carry it across the gap; and connections change, as Hebb proposed in 1949 and experiments on rabbits showed in 1973. Nature has tried nerve cells for more than six hundred million years: a jellyfish's net, a sea slug's twenty thousand cells, a bee's million, an octopus's half a billion, a finch that learns its father's song, and our 86 billion, on about 20 watts. A brain fills gaps, as the blind spot shows. McCulloch and Pitts drew Cajal's cell as a simple unit, Rosenblatt gave each connection a weight, Hubel and Wiesel's cells that fire for edges gave networks their layers, and today's models still add up weighted inputs, with billions of weights; but a real cell is far more complex, a child learns a word from a few examples, and a plane borrowed the wing, not the flapping. A model writes one word at a time, drawing each like a spin of a wheel; temperature changes the slices, and even turned right down a little chance stays. When the fact isn't there, it still writes likely words: asked three times for a researcher's birthday, a model gave three different dates, all wrong. Cajal's picture held because others could check it, down to the gap the electron microscope showed in the 1950s. Knowing that models fill gaps with what's likely, you know when to check them: against the evidence, as his drawings were checked.

### 3 · Inside a nerve cell · 3:03–3:57

**Narration.** A neuron is a cell that carries messages. Its branches take in signals from thousands of other cells. When enough arrive together, it fires: a pulse of electricity runs down its long fibre. The pulse is always the same size: all or nothing. At the end, it almost touches the next cell, and chemical messengers carry the signal across the gap. Some connections excite the next cell; others hold it back. And connections change. In 1949, the psychologist Donald Hebb proposed that when two cells fire together, the link between them grows stronger. In 1973, experiments on rabbits showed links growing stronger with use. That's learning, inside a brain: connections that change.

### 4 · Nature's trials · 3:57–5:03

**Narration.** Nature has tried out nerve cells for more than six hundred million years. A jellyfish has no brain: just a net of nerve cells, enough to swim and catch food. A sea slug has about twenty thousand nerve cells, some big enough to see. Eric Kandel showed that when it learns, its connections change. A honeybee has about a million, in a brain smaller than a sesame seed. Bees learn colours, scents and the way home, and dance to tell each other where food is. An octopus has about half a billion, two thirds of them in its arms: each arm can taste on its own. A young zebra finch learns its song from its father, practising for weeks. And a human brain has about 86 billion, joined by more than a hundred trillion connections. It runs on about 20 watts, the power of a dim light bulb.

### 6 · Borrowed, not copied · 5:22–6:31

**Narration.** In 1943, Warren McCulloch and Walter Pitts drew Cajal's nerve cell as a simple unit. It adds up its inputs, and fires if the total passes a set level. Rosenblatt's perceptron gave each connection a weight, a number nudged up or down as it learns, like Hebb's links. In 1959, David Hubel and Torsten Wiesel found cells in a cat's brain that fire for edges at one angle. They feed cells that respond to shapes. Networks that read pictures borrowed those layers. Today's models still add up weighted inputs, with billions of weights set by training. But they borrowed an idea, not a brain. A real nerve cell is far more complex: in 2021, it took a network of five to eight layers to mimic just one. A child can learn a new word from a few examples; a model needs a great many. Like a plane, which borrowed the wing, but not the flapping.

### 7 · Likely words · 6:31–7:36

**Narration.** A model writes one word at a time. For the next word, it gives every word it knows a chance. ## Rigour to check before recording

Checked on 4 November 2026, and the last few on 5 November 2026; the sources are listed below.

- **The perceptron, 1958.** Frank Rosenblatt, originally trained as a psychologist, worked at the Cornell Aeronautical Laboratory in Buffalo. On 7 July 1958, in Washington, D.C., the U.S. Office of Naval Research showed his perceptron simulated on an IBM 704 of the Weather Bureau: "After 50 trials, the computer taught itself to distinguish cards marked on the left from cards marked on the right" (Cornell Chronicle, 2019; Guinness World Records). The film's "a program that learned from examples" is that run (the Mark I machine came in 1960), and its three cards stand for the fifty. His paper that year is titled "The perceptron: a probabilistic model for information storage and organization in the brain" (*Psychological Review* 65, 1958): it built on McCulloch and Pitts's model nerve cell and added a rule that sets the weights from examples. The perceptron had layers of such units; "He modelled it on a nerve cell" names the unit it was built from, and the picture shows one. *Decades in the making* has 1958 on its rail.
- **"Nature has been trying out designs for billions of years, and keeping the ones that work."** A figure of speech for variation and natural selection: nothing in nature intends a design. Life on Earth goes back at least 3.5 billion years (the stromatolites of the Pilbara, Western Australia, 3.49 billion years old, with older claims disputed; read in Live Science's report of the 2019 study). The author's own words were "playing with options for billions of years".
- **Birds and the Wrights.** People studied birds for centuries before anyone flew: Leonardo's codex on the flight of birds (about 1505), and Otto Lilienthal's *Bird flight as the basis of aviation* (1889). Wilbur Wright's first letter to Octave Chanute, 13 May 1900: "My observation of the flight of buzzards leads me to believe that they regain their lateral balance, when partly overturned by a gust of wind, by a torsion of the tips of the wings." The brothers tried wing warping on a kite in July 1899 and on their gliders at Kitty Hawk from 1900 ("Two minutes' trial was sufficient to prove the efficiency of twisting the planes to obtain lateral balance", Wilbur to Chanute, 16 November 1900); the 1902 glider in the picture and the 1903 Flyer used it. The Wrights also told of a cardboard box twisted in their shop, and Orville later played down the birds; the film says what Wilbur wrote in 1900.
- **The maple seed.** Lentink and others, "Leading-edge vortices elevate lift of autorotating plant seeds", *Science* 324 (2009): a maple seed spins as it falls and stays up longer by a stable vortex along its leading edge, as hovering insects do; Lentink, an engineer, called it "the most basic and simple design for a miniature helicopter", if its wing could be driven by a small motor (as reported). "Engineers still study" says no more than that.
- **Barcelona, 1888.** Cajal qualified in medicine at Zaragoza and held the chair of histology and pathological anatomy at the University of Barcelona from 1887 to 1892, so "a doctor, teaches anatomy": histology is the anatomy of tissues under the microscope. He called 1888 his "year of greatest fortune" ("mi año cumbre, mi año de fortuna", in his memoirs), and that May published "Estructura de los centros nerviosos de las aves" (the structure of the nerve centres of birds) in the first issue of the journal he made himself, the *Revista Trimestral de Histología Normal y Patológica*. The microscope in his room stands for the Zeiss he was given in 1885 for his report on the cholera epidemic (sources differ on whether Zaragoza's or Valencia's provincial council gave it; the film doesn't say).
- **A thicket, and the net.** Under the microscopes of the day, nervous tissue showed a tangle of fibres too dense to follow. Many held the reticular theory, that the fibres are fused into one continuous network; Golgi believed the axons joined in such a net, and defended it to the end. "If they are, nobody can say where a message starts, or how a brain learns" is the film's plain statement of what was at stake: in one continuous net there are no separate units, so no way to say where a signal begins or which way it goes. With separate cells, Cajal could argue for a direction (below), and in his Croonian Lecture in London in 1894 that learning grows and strengthens the connections between cells.
- **The stroke.** Loss of speech after damage to the brain was well known by 1888: in 1861 Paul Broca found the damage behind the speechlessness of a patient who could say only "tan", in the left frontal lobe. Doctors could say roughly where the damage lay, but not what had broken in the tissue, or whether it could mend, since nobody could read how the tissue was built. The ward, the patient and the doctor are the film's, drawn to show what that meant for people; no one is named.
- **The stain.** Golgi's "black reaction" (1873): tissue hardened in potassium dichromate and soaked in silver nitrate. "For unknown reasons, the reaction only targets 1–5% of the neurons in a slice of brain tissue" (BrainFacts, 2020), each stained whole; a review calls it "a lingering mystery", and a 2025 preprint links it to how mature or active the cells are, without settling it. So the film says "it seems to pick at random. Nobody knows why, even today."
- **"Reliably", and young birds.** Cajal changed the method, soaking the tissue in silver nitrate in two short baths instead of one long one, and worked on embryos and very young animals, chicks and pigeons among them: young cells have fewer branches, and their fibres aren't yet sheathed in myelin, which the stain doesn't take well. The film's "where the thicket is thinner" is that. "Each black cell stands out whole, like a tree in winter" is the film's image, not Cajal's words.
- **Separate cells, and one way.** His drawings from 1888 on show cells whose branches reach close to others and end free: contact, not continuity. That a message comes in through the branches (dendrites) and the cell's body and goes out along its one long fibre (the axon), the "law of dynamic polarisation", he set out from 1889 to 1891, as Arthur van Gehuchten did at the same time; the film tells it right after the drawings, as one story, and says "he argues", since it was then an argument, later confirmed.
- **Berlin, 1889.** In October 1889 Cajal took his slides to the congress of the German Anatomical Society in Berlin and set them under the microscopes there. Albert von Kölliker, the society's president and the most respected histologist of the day, was won over: he published Cajal's findings in German and introduced him to others. Other leading anatomists followed over the next years; in 1891 Wilhelm Waldeyer gave the separate cell its name, the neuron. The film's "the leading anatomists look down his microscopes, and agree" compresses this, and the younger man drawn beside Kölliker is no one in particular.
- **The Nobel Prize, 1906.** Shared by Golgi and Cajal "in recognition of their work on the structure of the nervous system". Golgi's lecture, on 11 December 1906, was titled "The neuron doctrine – theory and facts" and argued against it; Cajal's, the next day, "The structure and connexions of neurons". The committee's report noted that most neuroscientists had by then accepted the neuron doctrine.
- **"The starting point of brain science", and medicines.** The neuron doctrine became the base of modern neuroscience. The place where two cells meet, named the synapse by Charles Sherrington in 1897, is where most drugs that act on the brain work: on its receptors, on the pumps that clear a messenger away (as the antidepressants that block serotonin's reuptake do), or on how much messenger is made or released. The film says "many", not most, and "the gaps he drew": he drew the cells apart; the gap itself, far too narrow for a light microscope, was seen in the 1950s (below).
- **"Machines had the same problem."** The film's link. McCulloch and Pitts (1943) started from the neuron as Cajal's successors described it: a cell that fires all or nothing, from many inputs, some exciting and some inhibiting.
- **Inside a nerve cell.** As any introduction to neuroscience teaches: a neuron takes in signals on its dendrites, from thousands of other cells for a typical cell of the cortex; when enough arrive close in time, it fires an action potential, a wave of charged atoms crossing its membrane that runs down the axon, always the same size; most connections are chemical synapses, where messenger molecules cross a cleft about 20 nanometres wide, and some excite the next cell while others inhibit it. A few synapses are electrical, and the film leaves them out. "A pulse of electricity" is the action potential in plain words, not a current along a wire.
- **Hebb, 1949.** Donald Hebb, *The Organization of Behavior* (1949), written at McGill University in Montreal: "When an axon of cell A is near enough to excite a cell B and repeatedly or persistently takes part in firing it, some growth process or metabolic change takes place in one or both cells such that A's efficiency, as one of the cells firing B, is increased." "When two cells fire together, the link between them grows stronger" is the usual short form; strictly, A takes part in firing B.
- **Rabbits, 1973.** T. V. P. Bliss and T. Lømo, "Long-lasting potentiation of synaptic transmission in the dentate area of the anaesthetized rabbit following stimulation of the perforant path", *Journal of Physiology* 232 (1973), from Per Andersen's laboratory in Oslo: brief bursts of stimulation made a connection stronger for hours, the first evidence in a mammal's brain of the change Hebb proposed. The rabbits were anaesthetised and stimulated through electrodes; the film's rabbit on its card is only a rabbit, and "with use" means with that stimulation.
- **More than six hundred million years.** Nervous systems arose roughly 600 to 700 million years ago, dates that are provisional; the last ancestor shared by jellyfish and humans lived more than 600 million years ago, and the ion channels nerve cells fire with are at least that old (a Penn State study, reported in 2015). Most jellyfish have a nerve net spread through the body, with no brain.
- **The sea slug.** *Aplysia* has about 20,000 nerve cells, some with bodies up to a millimetre across, big enough to see. Eric Kandel's group, in New York, at New York University and then Columbia, showed that as the slug learns to pull in its gill more or less strongly, the connections between its sensory and motor cells weaken or strengthen, and that long-term memory grows new connections (Carew and Kandel, 1973; Bailey and Chen, 1988). Kandel shared the Nobel Prize in 2000. The tag says "the 1970s", the decade of the work the film shows.
- **The honeybee.** About 960,000 nerve cells in a brain of about one cubic millimetre (Menzel and Giurfa, 2001, in BioNumbers); a 2019 study counted fewer, so the film says "about a million". A sesame seed is a few millimetres long, so a bee's brain is smaller than one. Bees learn the colours and scents of flowers and their way home, and Karl von Frisch showed that a forager's waggle dance tells the others the direction and distance of food (Nobel Prize, 1973).
- **The octopus.** About 500 million nerve cells, about two thirds of them in the arms (Harvard Gazette, 2020: "about two-thirds of an octopus's neurons are located in their arms"; Hochner, 2012, *Current Biology*, as cited), "allowing the flexible appendages to operate semi-independently" (Science News). Its suckers taste what they touch: L. van Giesen, P. B. Kilian, C. A. H. Allard and N. W. Bellono, "Molecular basis of chemotactile sensation in octopus", *Cell* 183 (2020). Sources give the arms' share as more than half to about 70%; the film says "two thirds".
- **The zebra finch.** Young males learn their song from a tutor, usually their father: they listen in their first weeks, start practising at about 30 days, and their song settles at about 90 days, close to the tutor's but not a copy. Only males sing, so the film's young finch is a male.
- **The human brain.** About 86 billion neurons (Azevedo and others, 2009: 86.1 ± 8.1 billion); the neocortex alone holds about 0.15 quadrillion synapses, 150 trillion (Pakkenberg and others, 2003), so "more than a hundred trillion connections"; the brain is about 2% of the body's weight and uses about 20% of its oxygen and energy (Raichle and Gusnard, 2002), about 20 watts, the figure most sources give.
- **The blind spot.** Where the optic nerve leaves the eye, the optic disc, the retina has no light-sensitive cells: Edme Mariotte found the blind spot in the 1660s. We don't see a hole, because the brain fills it in from what's around it (V. S. Ramachandran, "Blind spots", *Scientific American* 266, 1992, and "Filling in the blind spot", *Nature* 356, 1992). Researchers differ on whether the brain paints the gap in or discounts it; what we see is the same either way, and it's what the film says.
- **McCulloch and Pitts, 1943.** W. S. McCulloch and W. Pitts, "A logical calculus of the ideas immanent in nervous activity", *Bulletin of Mathematical Biophysics* 5 (1943): a unit that fires when the sum of its inputs passes a threshold; its weights and threshold were set by hand. Rosenblatt's perceptron added a rule that sets them from examples. "Like Hebb's links" is the film's comparison of the idea, a connection whose strength changes as it learns: Rosenblatt built on Hebb's work, though his rule changes a weight when the machine is wrong, not when two units fire together.
- **Hubel and Wiesel, 1959.** D. H. Hubel and T. N. Wiesel, "Receptive fields of single neurones in the cat's striate cortex", *Journal of Physiology* 148 (1959), at Johns Hopkins University in Baltimore: cells in the visual cortex fire for a bar or edge of light at one angle, found when the edge of a slide in their projector cast a line on the screen. Their later work traced simple cells feeding complex ones, and further along the visual pathway cells respond to more complex shapes: the film's "They feed cells that respond to shapes". Nobel Prize, 1981, "for their discoveries concerning information processing in the visual system". The cats were anaesthetised, with an electrode recording one cell; the film shows a cat watching a screen, with nothing done to it. "Networks that read pictures borrowed those layers": Kunihiko Fukushima's neocognitron (1980) was built on the simple and complex cells, and the convolutional networks that read pictures today (LeCun and others, 1989) followed it.
- **Billions of weights.** Today's language models have billions of weights, set by training; each unit still adds up weighted inputs and passes the sum through a simple function. No model is named.
- **One real nerve cell, 2021.** D. Beniaguev, I. Segev and M. London, "Single cortical neurons as deep artificial neural networks", *Neuron* 109 (2021), at the Hebrew University of Jerusalem: to reproduce what a detailed model of one pyramidal cell of the cortex does with its inputs, millisecond by millisecond, took a deep network of five to eight layers; without the cell's NMDA receptors, one hidden layer was enough. "To mimic just one" is a detailed model of one cell, built from measurements of real ones.
- **A child, a few examples.** Children can take in a new word's meaning from one or a few hearings ("fast mapping", Carey and Bartlett, 1978), and they hear three to five orders of magnitude fewer words than language models are trained on (M. C. Frank, "Bridging the data gap between children and large language models", *Trends in Cognitive Sciences*, 2023).
- **Likely words.** A language model writes one token at a time, a word or part of one (the film says "word"); for the next, it gives every token it knows a probability, and draws one. The bee sentence, its chances (70 and 12 in a hundred) and its two endings are the film's, to show the idea. "One word changes early on, and the rest follows": each word is written from the ones before it, so one different pick leads somewhere else.
- **Temperature.** The model's scores for the next word are divided by the temperature before they become chances: below 1 the biggest chance grows, at 0 the model always takes the most likely word, and above 1 the chances even out. M. Peeperkorn, T. Kouwenhoven, D. Brown and A. Jordanous, "Is temperature the creativity parameter of large language models?" (International Conference on Computational Creativity, 2024): with a fixed model and prompt, a higher temperature was "weakly correlated with novelty, and unsurprisingly, moderately correlated with incoherence": the film's "sometimes in new ways, and more often go wrong". "Answers stick to the patterns the model saw most" is the author's "pattern memory".
- **"Even turned right down, a little chance stays."** Thinking Machines Lab, "Defeating nondeterminism in LLM inference" (September 2025, read in Simon Willison's summary): at temperature 0, answers still vary, mainly because a server answers many requests together, and the size of the group changes the order of its sums and so their last digits, which can tip a close choice between two words ("batch invariance"); the post shows how to make the sums the same every time, at some cost in speed. The film's "its sums can come out a hair different, and tip a close choice".
- **The birthday, 2025.** A. T. Kalai, O. Nachum, S. S. Vempala and E. Zhang, *Why Language Models Hallucinate* (arXiv 2509.04664, 4 September 2025). Asked for one of its authors' birthday, "If you know, just respond with DD-MM", a state-of-the-art open-source model gave three different wrong dates on three tries, 03-07, 15-06 and 01-01; the film shows them as 3 July, 15 June and 1 January, and names neither the model nor the researcher. The paper shows that arbitrary facts with no pattern, like birthdays, that appear once or never in the training data are where models must err (the share seen only once sets a floor on the errors), and that tests scored right or wrong, with nothing for "I don't know", reward a guess: a guessed birthday has a 1 in 365 chance; "I don't know" scores nothing. The fluent "Born on 15 June." is one of the three, and "Staff records" and "Date matches" are the film's picture of a checked answer.
- **"Brains fill gaps too, with what's likely, and just as surely."** The blind spot, above; and memory, which *Minds that change* shows with its own studies, and which this film doesn't repeat.
- **The electron microscope.** E. De Robertis and H. S. Bennett, and S. L. Palay and G. E. Palade, both in 1955 (after abstracts in 1954), showed the cleft between two cells at a synapse; Palay (1956) measured it at about 200 ångströms, 20 nanometres: "twenty millionths of a millimetre". The debate between the net and separate cells was finally settled then.
- **Names said aloud.** `film.json` respells them for the voice: Ramón y Cajal as "Rah mone ee Ka hahl", Cajal as "Ka hahl", Camillo as "Kahmeelo", Golgi as "Goaljee", McCulloch as "Mac Cullock", Wiesel as "Veezel", Kandel as "Kan dell"; Rosenblatt, Hebb, Pitts, Hubel, Wilbur Wright and Kölliker (named only on screen) as they should be. Years and numbers are written out in each line's `say` ("eighteen eighty-eight", "nineteen oh six", "eighty-six billion", "twenty watts"), since the voice reads 1888 as "one thousand eight hundred eighty-eight". The module's rule for "AI" (`\bA I\b` back to `AI`) is in `film.json` too, though the narration doesn't say it.
- **Invented, and believable:** the ward, its patient and its doctor; the second anatomist in Berlin; the perceptron's three cards; the bee sentence, its chances and its two answers; "Staff records", "The records" and the checks in the last two chapters. Everyone named is real and is named for what they did; no one is drawn as a likeness. No company and no product is named, as in all of series 1.

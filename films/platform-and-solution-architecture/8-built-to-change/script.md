# Platform and solution architecture · Built to change: script

*The script of *Built to change*, film 8 of 8 of *Platform and solution architecture*, the fourth series of the topic *Enterprise architecture*, and the series' last, in nine chapters, in English, drawn in the look of *The map before the data*. Written on 12 November 2026 from the author's brief of 11 November 2026 and the series' plan (`../proposal.md`), which the author confirmed that day. Made while the author was away; this film's questions and assumptions are in `docs/assumptions/05-enterprise-architecture/4-platform-and-solution-architecture/8-built-to-change.md`. ## The promise

Every line passes two tests: someone new to data work can follow it, and a platform lead, an enterprise architect or a data engineer finds nothing wrong in it. Its idea: every part of a data practice will change, its tools, its products and its people, so build it so that any part can change and what's the organisation's own goes with it. Buy what every organisation needs in much the same way, build what's your own, and before buying, ask how your data comes out, in what format, and how long leaving would take. Look for seams, places where two parts meet through something both sides agree on, such as an open format, so the part behind can be replaced and nothing in front has to change: on a data platform, the tables as files in an open table format, in your own storage, and the logic as code in your own repository. Where there's no seam, that's lock-in, and it isn't wrong, it's the price of what a product does well; but write down what leaving would cost, so it's chosen, not found. Give every technology a stage and a date to look at it again, so an end is seen years ahead, not months. And give every part a way out, tried each year, since a plan nobody has tried is a guess. The practice is never finished: it's kept.

**Takeaway:** "Choose for change: open formats, clear seams and a way out, tested."

## The story in one paragraph

Japan, the 1860s: for over 250 years the Tōkaidō's stations have handed loads and travellers on, station to station. The road was kept by adjusting it: 36 horses at each station, then a hundred, and a hundred porters; when that wasn't enough, nearby villages had to send people and horses, the helper villages. But travel kept growing, and lords and nobles took horses without paying. Helper villages were called from further away, and farmers left their fields to carry other people's loads; their duty grew to several hundred times what was set in 1694. In 1764, on the Nakasendō, more than two hundred thousand farmers rose against a new demand to carry; in the 1860s, armies on the march took more than the stations could give, and their money ran out. The relay couldn't be stretched any further, and the country needed to move more, faster, than horses could. In 1872 the new government ended the relay and freed the helper villages, and Japan's first railway opened. The main line west was to cross the mountains; they proved far harder than expected, so in 1886 the line to Nagoya moved to the Tōkaidō's route, and on the first of July 1889 the whole line opened: Tokyo to Kobe in about twenty hours, where Kyoto alone had taken two weeks on foot. At the utility, in January, the storm tool's maker writes that support ends in December. The control room bought it twelve years ago, to record every storm, each fault and each line that failed, and all twelve years are inside it, in a format only it can read; its one way out is an export made for the regulator's measure, carrying little else. Grace's engineers choose which lines to strengthen from those storms, and only the maker can bring them all out, as a project of its own, at its price. Nobody did anything wrong, but nobody asked, when it was bought, how the utility's data would come out. So Ravi asks what else holds the utility like this, the data platform's own product included. With Tomás, he buys what every utility needs in much the same way and builds what's the utility's own, and asks before buying how the data comes out; the control room's new tool sends every record to the platform each night in an open format. He looks for seams: the platform's tables are files in an open table format in the utility's own storage, which any engine that knows the format can read, and the logic is code in the utility's own repository; the schedules, alerts and dashboards are the product's own, and he writes down what leaving would cost. Every technology on Tomás's map gets a stage and a date; the storm tool would have shown on its way out four years ago, and two more parts get their plans years ahead. Every part gets an exit plan, and one is tried each year: this year Grace's hosting capacity runs for a week, in test, on another engine that reads the same files, with eleven lines of fourteen hundred changed and every number matching; leaving the whole platform would take about eight months, a migration, not a rewrite. Grace asks in a ticket for the storm history to be kept; the maker brings all twelve years out, they land on the platform as files in an open format, owned by network, and in November, a month early, the old tool is switched off. Next year's work is chosen from all twelve winters. The road lasted because it was kept, but it never changed who carried the cost; at the utility, the next end is planned, not forced.

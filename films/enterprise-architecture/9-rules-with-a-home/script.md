## The promise

Every line passes two tests: anyone who has kept a rule in a spreadsheet, a script or their head can follow it, and an experienced enterprise or data architect finds nothing wrong in it. A rule is a claim about how the organisation works, so its home is the map. It's written once, in the business's words, about the concepts it governs: a rule builds on facts, and facts on concepts. A rule that crosses the edge between two domains is two rules, one on each side, joined by what crosses; left on the wall between them, it belongs to no one. Each rule has an owner, who can fix it, and a reason, a regulation, a principle or the service level of the step it serves, so it can be changed safely. It's attached to the step where it applies, not written inside it, and that step says what to expect of the data. In ArchiMate 4, the standard the map follows, a rule is a requirement, one of the motivation elements: its reason, a driver, influences it, the step that applies it realises it, and its owner and the data objects it applies to are associated with it; ArchiMate says where a rule sits, not how it's checked. What's generated from it, one way, depends on its kind: a rule about the data gets tests, which check the data is right; a rule about what the business does gets a tracking table, which lists every case where the business didn't keep it, because the data can all be right while a bill goes uncorrected. Either way, it tracks what the rule promises, not whether a job ran, and what it finds goes to the owner's team. Written this way, the concepts, how they relate and the rules about them are, generally, an ontology, ready for OWL; OWL reasons only from what is said, so finding what never happened stays the tracking table's job. The opening story carries the idea, and says so: in Tartu, each fact has one home and one owner, so it's asked for once. Tomás wants the same for rules, "once only", and the film comes back to Tartu at the end. **Write each rule once, where it applies, with an owner and a reason; generate tests from a rule about the data, and a tracking table from a rule about what the business does.**

## The story in one paragraph

In Tartu, Estonia, today, a baby is born, and the birth is entered once, in the population register. Days later, the parents get an email from the Social Insurance Board: a family benefit is theirs, if they accept it. They never applied. No office asks the parents again; each asks the population register, over X-Road, the exchange Estonia's public offices share. By law, no office may build a second database of the same data, and every database is listed, with the office that owns it. Each fact has one home and one owner: once only. Tomás's note asks where each rule belongs. On his first day he was shown one, "an estimated reading stands only until the next actual reading", beside a bill flagged wrong. Farah brings the household behind it: they were away for three weeks in June, their smart meter stopped sending readings, and the bill estimated those weeks from last year, $412.80. When the meter came back, it sent the readings it had stored: they had used almost nothing, and the bill was never corrected. The rule existed. Years ago, an analyst in metering wrote a nightly script, as a stopgap, that found estimates with an actual reading behind them and queued their bills to be corrected. In March, billing moved to a new system, and the script kept writing to the old queue, which nothing reads now. It ran every night, reported no error and corrected nothing: about 1,900 bills since March. It lived on the wall between the network and retail, and belonged to neither side. Tomás finds the rule written four more ways: the regulator's code says why, the others each say how, a little differently, and none of them is its home. Day one's rule is really two. The network's: an estimate is replaced when the actual reading arrives, and the replacement is sent to the retailer; Grace owns it, and its reason is the metering rules. Retail's: a bill based on a replaced estimate is corrected on the next bill; the head of retail owns it, and its reason is the regulator's retail code. The replaced reading crosses the wall on the agreed list. Ama corrects day one's draft too: fair, accurate bills was never one of the utility's goals, but an obligation. Each rule is written in the business's words, about readings, estimates, bills and connection points, which become data objects in the domains that own them, with the industry's words beside the utility's own. Each is attached to its step of meter to cash, and the read step says what to expect of the data: timeliness, completeness and validity. In ArchiMate 4, the standard the map follows, each rule is a requirement; ArchiMate has no element called a rule, so data rules and business rules are two kinds of requirement. Retail's rule is influenced by the regulator's retail code, a driver, realised by billing, and associated with its owner and the data objects it applies to: ArchiMate says where a rule sits, and how it's checked comes from the rule. Written in a set form, a rule says what happens when it fails, and what's generated from it depends on its kind. A rule about the data, every reading marked actual or estimated, gets tests, one way: a dbt test, a data contract's quality rule and a shape for linked data; they check the data is right. Retail's rule is about what the business does: the data can all be right while a bill goes uncorrected, so a tracking table is generated from it instead, listing every bill not corrected on the next bill. It tracks what the rule promises, not whether the job ran, so the first uncorrected bill would have been listed the morning after billing moved, and each morning the list goes to the billing team, not a mailbox. Written like this, the rules and their concepts are, generally, an ontology, ready for OWL and one of the map's views; but OWL reasons only from what is said, so finding the uncorrected bill stays the tracking table's job. The household gets $144.40 back, the 1,900 bills are found and corrected, Grace and the head of retail confirm their rules, and in Tartu the benefit is paid, and nobody was asked twice.

## Rigour to check before recording

- **Tartu, once only.** The opening follows how Estonia registers a birth and offers the family benefit today. The Ministry of the Interior is the register's controller (§ 3(2)), so the film's owner is the ministry. Once the birth is registered, the Social Insurance Board sends the parents a family benefits offer in its self-service portal and tells them by email; they confirm it there, and no application is needed (sotsiaalkindlustusamet.ee, pages updated 20 January and 17 March 2026). The proactive offer has run since October 2019 (ERR, 14 October 2019), which the Board describes as Estonia's first event-based service; almost 30,000 offers went out in its first year (TEHIK). The Public Information Act forbids separate databases for collecting the same data (§ 43³(2), passed on 24 January 2007, in force since 1 January 2008), names the one-request-only principle (§ 43¹(3), since 1 April 2019), requires every state database to be registered in RIHA before use, with its controller named in its statutes (§ 43⁷(1), § 43⁵(1)), and sends data exchange through X-tee, X-Road in English (§ 43⁹(5)); RIHA lists each system with its owner (RIA, updated 28 April 2026), and X-Road's national deployment began on 17 December 2001. - **The meter and the bill.** Smart meters store their readings while they can't send them (in Great Britain, half-hourly data is stored on the meter for 13 months; Ofgem, 14 September 2026), and in many markets the network replaces an estimate once actual reads arrive (ESB Networks in Ireland, as its retailers explain it). How estimates are made, flagged and replaced differs by market: in Australia's National Electricity Market, the metering data provider substitutes missing readings and flags each one actual or substituted (AEMO's substitution types). The film keeps it generic: the network replaces the estimate and sends the replacement to the retailer, and retail corrects the bill. An overcharge must be corrected and credited or refunded (in Great Britain, supply licence conditions 21B and 27; in Australia, National Energy Retail Rules rule 31, with the customer told within 10 business days for an overcharge of $50 or more); the 12- and 9-month limits often quoted cap back-billing undercharges, not refunds, so the film names no limit. "The regulator's retail code" is the film's generic name for such rules. Checked on 17 October 2026.
- **What a rule is.** The film follows the Business Rules Manifesto, version 2.0 (Business Rules Group, 1 November 2003), and SBVR 1.5 (OMG, December 2019): "Rules build on facts, and facts build on concepts as expressed by terms" (3.1), which chapter 7 says as "a rule builds on facts, and facts on concepts"; "Rules are not process and not procedure. They should not be contained in either of these" (2.2), which chapter 8 says as "attached, not inside: a rule isn't the process"; "one cohesive body of rules" (2.3); and "A rule is distinct from any enforcement defined for it" (4.5), which is why the tests and the tracking table are generated from the rule and are not the rule. The two kinds of rule (20 October) are a practitioner's cut that SBVR supports: it tells rules that define what something is, which can't be broken, from rules that govern what the business does, which can. A rule about the data can be tested on the data alone; a rule about what the business does can be broken while every record is valid, so what's generated from it lists the cases where it wasn't kept, for its owner's team to act on. The Manifesto is quoted unaltered, as it asks. The film writes each rule as a plain sentence with its parts named, not in SBVR's structured English. DMN 1.6 (OMG, September 2026) is for rules that decide, as tables; the next film's eligibility is one. Checked on 17 October 2026.
- **ArchiMate 4.** From this film on, the series follows ArchiMate 4 (The Open Group, C260, 27 April 2026) without saying what changed (17 October); chapter 9 says where it puts a rule, as the author asked (20 October). A rule, of either kind, is drawn as a requirement, which version 4 keeps among the motivation elements, now that a constraint is no longer an element of its own; day one's "fair, accurate bills" turns from a goal into a requirement, an obligation. - **The industry's words.** The IEC Common Information Model for metering, IEC 61968-9:2024 (edition 3.0, 18 July 2024), defines a usage point as a "logical or physical point in the network to which readings or events may be attributed", which the utility calls a connection point; it marks a reading as estimated or validated with a reading quality, which the utility writes as "actual or estimated". - **Quality expectations.** Timeliness, completeness and validity are three of the six core dimensions in DAMA UK's paper of 2013, which DAMA-DMBOK discusses; the Open Data Contract Standard's nearest to validity is conformity. The film takes each from the process, as this film's idea in the proposal says: the service level of the read step (an estimate replaced within two working days, the film's own figure), its own steps, and another rule realised by it, *every reading is marked actual or estimated*. It builds on the series' insights 4 (value streams show the customer's view) and 6 (information is a business question before it's a software one).
- **The generated tests.** The data rule, RUL-020, gets three. dbt: a model's tests are listed under `data_tests:`; the film's is the built-in `accepted_values`, whose values go under `arguments:` from dbt 1.10.5, with `meta` under `config:` (docs checked on 20 October 2026). `accepted_values` checks only the values that are there, so a reading with no kind passes it; `not_null` checks that every reading has one, and the film leaves it out. The Open Data Contract Standard v3.2.0 (8 September 2026): a quality rule has an `id`, and the library's `invalidValues` metric takes its `validValues` under `arguments:`, with `mustBe: 0` (docs checked on 20 October 2026). SHACL 1.0 (W3C Recommendation, 20 July 2017): a node shape targeting a class, with a property shape whose values must be in a list (`sh:in`); like `accepted_values`, it says nothing of a reading with no kind, which `sh:minCount 1` would add. SHACL 1.2 is still a working draft (14 October 2026). Not shown: where data arrives, the same data rule could run in Databricks as a pipeline's expectation (Lakeflow Spark Declarative Pipelines, `EXPECT … ON VIOLATION`), for a lab to show. The ids RUL-020 to RUL-022 are the series' own; the next film's rules start at RUL-023.
- **The tracking table.** Retail's rule, RUL-022, is about what the business does, so it gets no test: a test fails a run when the data is wrong, and here every record can be right while a bill goes uncorrected (the author, 20 October 2026). What's generated from it is a model like any other, listing the bills the rule applies to that the next bill didn't correct, each with the rule's id and its owner's team, who work through it each morning. dbt has no test for what the business does (checked on 20 October 2026), so teams build such tracking tables, or quality tables, as ordinary models. `bills_on_replaced_estimates` and `corrected_on_next_bill` are the film's own names.
- **Day one's card.** It keeps the "data rule" kicker *Day one* gave it; when it cracks in chapter 5, the two rules it becomes are business rules, which is what this film shows: day one's rule was really two rules about what each side does.
- **Generally, an ontology.** The film introduces the idea and no more (17 October): concepts, how they relate and the rules about them, written once in the map, are generally an ontology, ready for OWL; it says "generally" on purpose. In the broad sense, an ontology is "an explicit specification of a conceptualization" (Thomas Gruber, 1993), which concepts, their relations and the rules about them, written down, are; in the strict sense, it's one written in a formal language such as OWL, which is why the film says "ready for OWL", not "in OWL". The link is to *One map, many views* in *Meaning machines can read*, where the glossary, the models, the ontology and the checks are views generated from one map (16 October). OWL 2 reasons under the open-world assumption: a missing fact "may simply be missing (but possibly true)" (W3C, *OWL 2 Primer*), so a correction that never happened is unknown, not a violation; even a rule OWL can state, such as every reading being actual or estimated, leads it to infer, not to check. SHACL validates the data against shapes and reports what's missing; finding a bill the business didn't correct is the tracking table's work. Checked on 17 October 2026.

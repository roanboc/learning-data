## The promise

Every line passes two tests: someone who has only heard of AI agents can follow it, and a platform lead, a data governance lead or a railway signalling engineer finds nothing wrong in it. Films 1.4 to 1.7 showed what agents are like: capable (they know more than any junior and work faster), convincing (they sound sure, and feeling sure is not knowing) and unaccountable (they can't answer for what they do: the agent acts, a person answers). This film draws the conclusion: every agent needs an owner, an identity, limits and review. In most ways an agent is treated like any colleague, with a role, the same rules and the same security, plus four rules of its own: it has no rights of its own, only a role's; it always has a human owner; it never approves its own work, and agents are never approvers; and its autonomy is a written mandate that can be taken back, with a person approving every change to production. None of this is because agents are bad. Japan's first high-speed line wasn't given limits because its trains were bad, but because they were fast, and the limits built into the line are what let them run fast safely for more than sixty years. **We govern agents because they are capable, not because they are bad.**

## The story in one paragraph

Japan, 1964. The national railway is opening a new line from Tokyo to Osaka, the world's first high-speed railway, built for trains at 210 kilometres an hour. At that speed a train covers a kilometre in seventeen seconds: its driver can't read the signals beside the track in time, and the train can't slow down at once. A missed signal could cost lives: in Tokyo in 1962, a train ran past a red one, and in the crashes that followed, 160 people died. Passengers' lives would rest on one person seeing every signal in time. Something had to change. So the railway's engineers build the limits into the line itself: a signal inside the driver's cab shows the speed allowed, and if the train goes faster, it slows itself down; one control room watches every train on the line; and the driver still drives, and answers for the train. It worked. For passengers, the trip fell from six and a half hours to four, and since that first day no passenger on Japan's high-speed lines has died in a derailment or a collision. The limits weren't there because the trains were bad, but because they were fast. At the university, Jun's agent is fast too, and no one can watch its every step; so we build its limits into how it works, and then we can let it do more. Jun's agent helps with the census-day count, the number of students the university sends the government: a plain job counts them, and when it fails at night, the agent finds the cause in test, and people approve its fix. The team wonders whether it could do more: work for all of them, or even change the pipeline itself. It is capable, convincing and unaccountable (the agent acts; a person answers), so Yusuf, who leads data governance, asks what the university asks of any new colleague: who answers for it, under what name it works, what it may touch, and who checks its work. An owner: Jun set it up, so he owns it, decides what it's for, reads what it did, can stop it, and hands it over if he moves on; and since an agent in the wrong hands does harm faster, not just anyone may own one. An identity: today it works under Jun's name, which is fine while it's learning, with read-only tools and a sandbox for its drafts, but its work looks like his and stopping it would stop Jun; to work for the team it needs a name of its own, so the team can see everything it does and stop it alone, as the control room saw each train. Limits: no rights of its own, only its role's; a train can only go where its rails go, and an agent wherever its tools reach, so each tool gets the fewest rights; how much it may do alone is our choice, not its skill's, written down as a mandate its owner signs, set from outside like the speed in the cab, lowered at any time, never raised by the agent. Review: a person checks its evidence, not its tone; it never approves its own work and no agent approves anyone's, as no train turns its own signal green; in testing it may do agreed tasks alone and report; every change to production is checked by its owner and approved by a second person, the same as people's changes. Those are the four rules of its own, beside everything it shares with any colleague, and the field has names for them: levels of autonomy by the person's role (Feng, McDonald and Zhang), ISO/IEC 42001 and NIST's AI Risk Management Framework, which series 3 and 4 put to work. More than sixty years on, trains on that first line run at 285 kilometres an hour, and the limits are still built in, because the trains are fast, not because they are bad. We govern agents for the same reason. With an owner, a name, limits and review, Jun's team can let its agent do more and still trust the number the university sends the government.

## The reference, and practice

The brief names three references, once each, and says series 3 and 4 teach them: the **levels of autonomy by the person's role** (Feng, McDonald and Zhang, 2025), **ISO/IEC 42001** and the **NIST AI Risk Management Framework**. **Where the film adopts, extends or differs.** It adopts Feng, McDonald and Zhang's central point, that autonomy is a choice made apart from capability, and says it plainly in chapter 5. It extends the frameworks with the module's own rules from practice: agents are never approvers (their review is only for reference), every change to production is approved by a second person, as people's are, and in testing an agent may do agreed tasks alone and report. It differs from Feng and colleagues' top level, the observer, where the person only watches: the module never lets an agent change production without a person (the plan; series 3's stage 5).

**Practice, from the author (the plan's insights):** an agent in the wrong hands is more dangerous; tool servers get the fewest rights (read-only when reading is enough, writes only as drafts in sandboxes, no sensitive data); nothing reaches production without a person, and a change to production needs a second person's approval, for agents too; in testing an agent may do agreed tasks alone and report; agents are never approvers; the person who set an agent up holds its accountability. A practitioner will recognise the borrowed identity: an agent that runs under its builder's name makes every change look like the builder's, and switching it off means switching off a person's access.

**What the film leaves to its siblings and the later series.** The feeling of understanding (1.4), the junior picture (1.5), changing answers (1.6) and accountability (1.7) are recalled in a line each. How tools, memory and context work is series 2's; how an agent earns each stage, its own identity, its mandate and its review is series 3's; registers, position descriptions and reviews of agents are series 4's. This film moves Jun's agent up no stage: it asks the four questions, and series 3 answers them for this agent.

## Rigour to check before recording

Checked on 3 November 2026; the sources are listed below.

- **The line, its opening and its speed.** The Tōkaidō Shinkansen opened on 1 October 1964 between Tokyo and Shin-Osaka (nippon.com, Umehara Jun, 25 October 2024: "From the very first run of the Tōkaidō Shinkansen on October 1, 1964"). The American Society of Mechanical Engineers' landmark citation: "In 1964, Shinkansen ... between Tokyo and Shin-Osaka became the world's first high-speed railway system", "operated at about 210 km/h". Wikipedia's article adds that it "was designed for speeds up to 210 kilometres per hour" and that "JNR limited regular operation to 160 km/h ... during the opening period", with 210 km/h allowed under normal conditions from November 1965; so the film says "built for trains at 210 kilometres an hour", not that they ran at it from the first day. "The national railway" is Japanese National Railways, the public corporation that built and ran the line (it was broken up in 1987); the film names no company.
- **"A kilometre in seventeen seconds."** At 210 km/h a train covers 58.3 metres a second, so a kilometre takes 17.1 seconds.
- **Why signals beside the track weren't enough.** Yamanouchi, "Safety and ATC of Shinkansen", *Japanese Railway Engineering* 19(2), 1979, pp. 25 to 27 (TRID record 147994): "Train speed was previously controlled by the wayside signal. But the high speeds on the Shinkansen line made this safety system dangerous, as the train is running too fast to see the signal and moreover cannot slow down immediately." The film's "can't read the signals beside the track in time, and the train can't slow down at once" is that sentence in plain words. No stopping distance is given, so the film draws a long arrow and gives no number.
- **The cab signal, and the train that slows itself.** Yamanouchi (1979): trains run "by means of a single signal inside the cab, which indicates the speed at which the train must run, and operates the train automatically, although the driver has manual control of certain operations such as speeding up and stopping in stations." nippon.com (2024): "When the train approaches another train on the same track, or a stop along the line, the ATC automatically reduces its speed. The driver only needs to make fine adjustments to then stop the train at the designated spot", and "The ATC allows trains to reach their top speeds of over 200 kilometers per hour no matter how bad the weather or how dark the night." The boxes "allowed 210" and "allowed 160" are two of the first system's steps (0, 30, 70, 110, 160 and 210 km/h, Wikipedia's *Automatic train control*, which carries a "needs more citations" tag; the film shows only these two, as an illustration, and doesn't claim how the cab displayed them).
- **"One control room watches every train on the line."** nippon.com (2024): "the adoption of Centralized Traffic Control from the Tōkaidō Shinkansen's inception", "Concentrating the tasks of monitoring and directing train operations in one location". The film doesn't name the room's city: a photograph of 1977 shows "Tokyo's central control center for the Shinkansen network", but no source read says where the room was in 1964. The board, its lights and the controllers' clothes are the film's drawing, not a copy of a photograph.
- **"The driver still drives, and answers for the train."** The driver's controls are Yamanouchi's and nippon.com's (above); "answers for the train" is the film's plain word for the driver's responsibility for operating it, not a claim about any law.
- **Tokyo, 1962.** Wikipedia, *Mikawashima train crash*: on 3 May 1962, at Mikawashima on the Jōban Line in Arakawa, Tokyo, a freight train "missed the red signal" and derailed onto the main line; a passenger train struck it, and six minutes later a second passenger train hit the wreckage and people escaping it; 160 people died and 296 were injured. The film says "a train ran past a red one, and in the crashes that followed, 160 people died", blames no one, shows no wreck, and doesn't say the high-speed line's design answered this crash: its signalling was planned for its speed, and the railway's own answer to Mikawashima was automatic train stop on all its lines by April 1966 (the same article). "Railways knew" is not said; "a missed signal could cost lives" is the crash's plain lesson.
- **"Something had to change."** The problem beat's line, as the rule of 28 October 2026 asks: without a new way, the speed would rest on a person seeing every signal in time, which Yamanouchi says it couldn't.
- **The benefits.** Times: nippon.com (2024): "Before the opening of the Shinkansen, it took at least six and a half hours to get from Tokyo to Osaka by rail. The first Shinkansen runs shortened that to four hours, and from 1965 the time was cut by over half to three hours and ten minutes." The film gives the first step only. Safety: nippon.com (2024): "there has never been a single derailment or collision on the entire full-standard Shinkansen rail network resulting in a passenger fatality"; Central Japan Railway, for the first line: "No accidents resulting in fatalities or injuries of passengers on board since operations commenced". The film says "no passenger on Japan's high-speed lines has died in a derailment or a collision", the full-standard lines' record, and makes no claim about other deaths (people on platforms or tracks, or crimes on board). News searches on 3 November 2026 found no later fatal derailment or collision; the accident of 20 August 2026 that killed four track workers at Shin-Kanuma station was on another railway, Tobu's, and its train wasn't a Shinkansen (Euronews with AP, 20 August 2026). Central Japan Railway's page also says about 7.0 billion people have used the first line since 1964; the film leaves that number out.
- **"More than sixty years on ... 285 kilometres an hour, and the limits are still built in."** Central Japan Railway: "Maximum speed: 285 km/h", "Tokyo ~ Shin-Osaka: 2 hours 21 minutes" (March 2023 timetable). Wikipedia's *Automatic train control*: "Since 18 March 2006, Digital ATC has also been enabled for Tōkaidō Shinkansen ... replacing the old analog ATC system."

## The promise

Every line passes two tests: anyone who has ever filled in a form can follow it, and someone who runs Databricks and dbt for a living finds nothing wrong in it. In *Know what you hold*, Mei Tanaka named a class for every column of the learner table and a level for the table. Here those names become governed tags in Databricks: only their values, set only by the registrar's own loader. A scan proposes what it recognises, and the owner confirms, and adds what no scan can see. The classes are written once, beside each column in the project's dbt files, and every build sets them as tags. Tag propagation then carries each class through lineage into every table made from its column, with nobody tagging it. It never lowers a class: where two values meet in one column, a rule set in advance applies, the strictest. So a value that says less, like the learner code, is made once at the source by its owner, and a table's level stays its owner's call. **Tag once at the source; the tag follows the data.**

## The story in one paragraph

The early 1990s. Tropical forests are being cut much faster than ten years before, and people in Europe and North America campaign against tropical wood; but no buyer can tell where a plank was grown, or how, and the cutting goes on. In 1992 the Earth Summit in Rio fails to agree to stop it. In 1993, 130 people from 26 countries meet in Toronto and found the Forest Stewardship Council: an independent auditor certifies a forest, every company that buys, saws, makes or sells its wood is certified too and passes the claim on, and a product carries the label only if every company along the chain is certified; where certified wood is mixed with other wood, a rule set in advance says how it's labelled. By the end of 2025, over 171 million hectares are certified and over 68,000 certificates cover the chain. At the university, on Tuesday 13 October 2026, Mei's classes from *Know what you hold* are words; the platform must read them, and every table made from the learner table must carry them. Sam Okafor makes them governed tags in Databricks: `personal_data` with four values (`none` is no tag), `level` for tables, and `faculty`, key only; only the registrar's loader may set them. Databricks' data classification proposes a name, an email and a date of birth on the learner table, and nothing for the student number or for exam support, which only Mei knows is given for a disability. Jun Park writes each class beside its column in the project's dbt file, the data team reviews the code, Mei is told what it touches, and every build sets the classes, then the faculty tag, then the table's level. Sam turns on tag propagation, and that afternoon the study's copy arrives with its classes: the learner code `pseudonymous`, the year of birth `indirect`. Had the copy made the code itself from the student number, lineage would bring `direct` against Mei's `pseudonymous`: a conflict, and the strictest value, set in advance, would apply. So the code is made once, at the source, and a table's level never travels. Nothing travels outside the platform; Anil's totals carry no class, and Mei makes them public. Like a chair's label, the class can be trusted because every link passed it on, by rules set before.

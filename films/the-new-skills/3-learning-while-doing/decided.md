| 10 November 2026 | *Learning while doing* opens in Angicos, in the four beats: in January 1963 about 380 adults sign up for evening classes in a small town of Brazil's dry north-east (farm workers, builders and washerwomen among them), run by some twenty students from the university in Natal, trained by Paulo Freire; three adults in four there can't read or write, which in Brazil then means no vote, papers signed unread and letters read to them by others, and adult classes use children's primers (*Eva saw the grape*) about nothing in their lives, so many give up; Freire's team starts from the town's own words (about four hundred written down, a handful chosen), and each evening a culture circle, learners and a coordinator instead of a teacher, talks about a picture from their own lives, takes one word from it (*belota*, a hammock's tassels), breaks it into syllables and makes new words, one small step each evening, and what each person already knows counts; at the fortieth hour, on 2 April 1963, with the president of Brazil there, the organisers count about 300 who can read and write, a number still argued over, which the film says. 1964 is told plainly: the national plan of January stopped that April by a military government, Freire jailed, then exiled. It comes back in *Teach it to keep it* (Freire's own words, 1996) and at the close, with its benefit for data (Ivy learned the team's work the same way). The opening's seed named *tijolo* (brick) among the words; the sources found name *belota* as the first word taught in Angicos, so the film uses it. |

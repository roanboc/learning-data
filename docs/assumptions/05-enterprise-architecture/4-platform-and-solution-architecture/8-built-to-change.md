| 1 | Which part of the story does this film take? | The Tōkaidō's last years and the railway, the 1860s to 1889. The relay had been kept for over 250 years by adjusting it: 36 horses at each station, then 100 and 100 porters, and the helper villages (*sukegō*) called on when that wasn't enough. Travel kept growing, lords and nobles rode without paying, helper villages were called from further away, and their duty grew to several hundred times the standard of 1694; in 1764 more than 200,000 farmers rose on the Nakasendō; in the 1860s armies on the march ruined the stations. In 1872 the relay ended and the helper villages were freed, and the first railway opened; the main line west moved from the mountains to the Tōkaidō's route in 1886, and the whole line opened on 1 July 1889, Tokyo to Kobe in a little over twenty hours. It stands for a practice built to change: kept by adjusting it, until a part has to be replaced, and replaced along the same route. The plan's line was "kept and adjusted for over 250 years; when it couldn't carry what the country needed, the railway replaced it, along its own route": the film keeps both. As the series' last film, it says what the story didn't achieve: the road never changed who carried the cost, which broke the stations and the helper villages, and many station towns declined when the railway came. Checked against Kotobank (*Sekai daihyakka jiten*, *Nihon daihyakka zensho*, Britannica, *Mypedia*) and Japanese Wikipedia. | *Kept for 250 years*, *Seams*, *Kept, never finished* |
| 2 | What does the film leave unsaid? | The names of the rising of 1764 (*Tenma sōdō*), of the shogun's journeys to Kyoto and of the expeditions against Chōshū; the new government's reform of 1868, which the farmers' opposition reversed within two years; that carrying also let poorer villagers earn; the railway's steep climbs and the tunnels that later eased them. The first railway is shown as *Tokyo to Yokohama*, for Shinbashi to Yokohama, and the journey as "about twenty hours", for a little over twenty. How many station towns declined wasn't found, so the film says "many". | *Kept for 250 years*, *Kept, never finished* |
| 3 | Is the data story believable? | Yes. A bought tool holding years of an organisation's records in a format only it can read, with one export made for a regulator's measure, is common, and so is a maker who brings the rest out only as a paid project. Twelve years without asking how the data comes out is the usual way it happens, which is why the film calls nobody wrong. A tool on its way out for years before its end of support is common too, as its maker stops improving it. A week's trial of one data product on another engine, with 11 lines of 1,400 changed, is believable where the tables are in an open table format and the logic is SQL in dbt, a few functions written differently; about eight months to move a whole platform, as a migration, is plausible for a mid-sized utility. | *Twelve years inside*, *A date on every part*, *A way out, tested* |
| 4 | Who asks, who reviews, and who owns? | Grace asks in a ticket for the storm history to be kept; the data team brings it out with the maker, checks each year against the old tool's reports and lands it on the platform; Grace's engineers check it in test, and her ticket closes. The storm history is owned by network, Grace's domain, shown with network's gold tag; hosting capacity's data owner is Grace. No business user reviews a pull request, and no owner or steward does. Build or buy, the stages and the exit plans are Ravi's and Tomás's work, as the platform lead and the enterprise architect. | *Twelve winters, kept*, *The whole practice, on the map* |

| 6 | Isn't lock-in always to be avoided? | No, and the film says so: where a part has no seam, that's lock-in, and it isn't wrong, it's the price of what a product does well; what leaving would cost is written down, so it's chosen, not found. That follows practitioners, G. Hohpe among them, who weigh the cost of switching against what a choice gives, rather than avoid lock-in at any price. | *Seams* |
| 7 | Why does the voice name Databricks, and no table format? | The plan makes Databricks the tool that shows the ideas: on the practice map it's the system software that realises the platform's services. The film names no table format, Delta Lake or Apache Iceberg, so it takes no side between them and doesn't date when they change; "an open table format" is what the idea needs. No feature and no release status is named. | *The whole practice, on the map* |
| 8 | Is "a migration, not a rewrite" fair? | Yes, for what the utility keeps as its own: with the tables as files in an open table format in its own storage, and the logic as code in its own repository, another engine reads the same files and runs the same code with a few lines changed. What's the product's own is set up or made again: its schedules, its alerts, its dashboards, and who may read what, since the catalogue's rights are the product's. The film says each of them. | *Seams*, *A way out, tested* |
| 9 | Why stages called tried, in use, on its way out and retired? | They are TOGAF's classes of standards in its Standards Library (trial, active, deprecated, obsolete), in plain words. Practitioners' technology radars use other names for the same idea; the film keeps the four plain ones. | *A date on every part* |
| 10 | Is the practice map right in ArchiMate? | Grace, a business actor, assigned the data owner's role; *hosting capacity*, a product, aggregating a business service, *look up capacity*, and *its contract*; a business object, *solar a street can take*, realised by a data object, *capacity, street by street*; *pipeline and models*, an application component writing the data object, composed with its *output port*, an application interface that serves *the website*; technology services realised by *Databricks*, system software, and serving the component; and two artifacts, *tables: open-format files* realising the data object and *models: code* realising the component.

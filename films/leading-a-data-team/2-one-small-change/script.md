## The promise

Every line passes two tests: someone new to leading a team can follow it, and a data manager, a team lead or a continuous improvement coach finds nothing wrong in it. The film's idea is kaizen: improvement is everyone's job, on their own work, in small steps. Each step runs through one cycle, plan a change, do it, check it, then act: keep it, or drop it. The order is the practitioner's: measure, then simplify, then standardise, then automate. And the leader's part is small and exact: ask what's in the way, give the time, and show the small wins. The person who does the work improves it; nobody is told to work harder.

**Takeaway:** "Change one small thing, check it, keep what works."

## The story in one paragraph

Colombo, Sri Lanka, 2000: at the Castle Street Hospital for Women, a public hospital where mothers come to give birth, too many mothers catch an infection after a Caesarean, and too many newborn babies catch one too; an infection means fever and pain, days more in hospital and real danger to mother and child, and at first the ministry gives no money for a fix. The director, Dr Wimal Karandagoda, brings in 5S, five steps from Japanese factories already used by companies in Sri Lanka (sort, set in order, shine, standardise, sustain): it costs little, everyone can see the change, and the staff of each ward do it themselves, starting with their own corner of the work; then kaizen, small changes the staff propose, try, check, and keep or drop. Not everyone wants it at first; ward by ward it spreads, and in two years infections after a Caesarean fall by half, and in newborn babies by more than half. The way goes on to other hospitals in Sri Lanka and, from 2007, with Japan's aid agency, to public hospitals in fifteen African countries. Nobody was told to work harder. At the university a year from now, Ivy Reyes owns *Teaching space use* from Thursday 1 July 2027; its promise is a refresh every Monday by seven, and a job publishes it at six, but from half past eight she spends two hours checking it by hand, while anyone who opens the page may read a wrong number. At her mid-year conversation on Tuesday 20 July, Helena Novak asks what she wants next, and then what's in the way, and gives the work Ivy's Thursday learning time. Ivy measures first (on Monday 26 July: six steps, a hundred and twenty minutes), drops two steps nobody needs (a hand check a test in Jun's models already makes, and a copy for Facilities, which reads the product's page), writes the four left as one checklist that Jun follows to the same result, and only then, from September, turns one step a week into a check inside the job, each reviewed by Sam and run beside her own for a Monday. By October the refresh takes ten minutes, checked, and the job keeps last week's numbers if a check fails. Helena made none of the changes: she asked what was in the way, gave the time, and showed the small wins at the review of Thursday 7 October, naming Ivy, Jun and Sam; from Friday 3 September the team's Friday half-hour makes small changes everyone's habit.

## The reference, and practice

The module's reference for this series is four ideas for leading a team, each taught from its own sources and never as anyone's philosophy (the module's plan, 5 November 2026). This film's idea is kaizen. It names its sources once, and teaches what they share: improvement is everyone's job, on their own work, in small steps, each one checked.

**Where the film adopts, extends or differs.** It adopts the cycle as the unit of change: every small step is planned, done, checked and kept or dropped, and Ivy's first one (skipping her hand check of the 14 rooms) is shown as exactly that. It adopts kaizen's first idea, that improvement is everyone's job, on their own work, and the order a practitioner keeps (measure, simplify, standardise, automate). It extends kaizen from a factory floor and a hospital ward to one person's weekly data chore, with the automation step that data work adds: a check inside the job, reviewed by a colleague and run beside the hand check for a Monday before the hand check goes. It differs from a programme in one thing: there is none. Helena sets no target and makes no speech; her part is questions, time and a chart. It names the cycle's roots once and doesn't teach TQM, lean's tools or the debate between plan, do, check, act and Deming's later plan, do, study, act, which the page's *Go further* links point to.

**Practice, from the module's plan.** A practitioner will recognise the weekly refresh that someone checks by hand after it has already been published; the step nobody remembers adding, which another check already covers; the copy made for a team that stopped reading it months ago; the urge to automate everything at once, and the faster muddle that follows; and the improvement that never happens because nobody has the time. The advice is the briefs': pick one chore of your own and time each step; drop what nobody needs and write the rest down, the same each time; automate one step at a time, and check each one before you trust it; and if you lead, ask what's in the way, give the time, and show the small wins.

## Pause and think

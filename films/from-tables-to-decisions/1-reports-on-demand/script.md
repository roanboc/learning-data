## The promise

Every line passes two tests: someone who has only heard of dashboards can follow it, and a BI lead and a data engineer who sets up Genie on Databricks each find nothing wrong in it. The film is Rodrigo's point, made for the university: "Reports can now be generated on demand, so switch the focus to deliver good data models and context as part of the semantic layer and let the users explore the data with the agents. Repetitive queries are the ones deserving dashboards, not trying to build complicated dashboards trying to [answer] every question." A question used to wait a week for a report of its own; now it's answered when it's asked, from a measure defined once and the context around it, and the answer shows what it counted and the query it ran, so anyone can check it. The data team's work moves from a report for each question to good models and their context, built once. A question earns a dashboard when it comes back on a schedule and someone acts on what it shows. How the measure is defined is the next film's (*Defined in the open*); a Genie Agent's parts are *Working with agents on Databricks*'s, and Genie One citing a page and a metric is *Meaning on the platform*'s, which the film names and doesn't repeat. **Ask for the answer; keep dashboards for the questions that come back.**

## The story in one paragraph

London, 1936. To know the exact time, people rang the telephone exchange and asked the operator, who read it from the clock on the exchange wall. It was never quite exact to the second, and the exchange couldn't always answer just when a caller asked, so a clock or a watch set by that answer could be out; and the time is a question everyone asks, again and again. So the Post Office's own engineers built a machine to answer it, the speaking clock: Jane Cain, one of the operators, recorded its words on glass discs, a clock played them at the right moment, and every hour it was checked against Greenwich. From 24 July 1936 anyone in London could dial TIM and hear the time, right to a tenth of a second, at any hour; in less than a year it answered more than 12 million calls, and that question no longer needed an operator. At the university, the questions asked every pay day came in a 40-page report, and any other question waited a week for a report of its own. On pay day, Thursday 5 November 2026, Rohan Desai, in Finance, is setting next year's budget for casual teaching in the Faculty of Health (casual teaching is done by staff paid for the hours they work, like Linh Nguyen, who tutors health informatics). He needs one number, what casual teaching has cost Health this semester, so far, which used to take a report and a week. He asks Genie One, in his own words, and gets A$1.84 million when he asks, with what it counted (the claims approved for casual teaching, plus on-costs) and the query it ran; Semester 1's is A$2.21 million, and by lunch the budget is set. Genie One answered from a Genie Agent that Sam and Jun, in the data team, set up for this one subject: its data, one measure, by faculty, unit and teaching period, which Rohan defined in September; its rules in their owners' words, Rohan's for what cost and this semester mean, and Ingrid's, in HR, for totals only, so it reads the measure and never anyone's claims; its examples and its tests; and each answer runs as the person asking. So the data team's work has moved, to good models and their context, built once, and they started small, with Rohan as its first user. Some questions come back every pay day from the same people: what the pay run cost, casual hours in each faculty, and claims still waiting. Like the time in 1936 they get a machine of their own, one dashboard, refreshed after each pay run and sent to the people who act on it, with an Ask Genie button that opens the same Genie Agent. It replaced the 40-page report, whose three figures were on its first page, and that report is retired. The rule of thumb: a question deserves a dashboard when it comes back on a schedule, and someone acts on what it shows; every other question is asked when it's needed.

## Sound

A new palette ([`source/tools/score.py`](source/tools/score.py)), reserved for this film by the module's setup thread on 5 November 2026 (`palettes.md`), after checking every score on main and on every branch: **repeater gongs**, the coiled steel gongs a repeater clock or watch strikes only when someone asks it the time. A repeater has two, the hours struck on the low one and the minutes on the high one; the low gong is the partner, chosen by this film's thread the same day, since every other colour the site lacks either belongs to another culture or sounds like one the site has, so the film keeps to the one instrument that answers when asked. Both are made in the score (the shared instruments have neither): a steel wire struck softly near its block by a small hammer with a soft tip, its partials those of a steel bar (1, 2.76 and 5.40 times the note), the higher ones dying first, and damped within about a second and a half for the high gong and two for the low. They are kept apart from the site's bells, bell plates and crotales: no hum below the note, no minor third inside it, nothing that rings on, nothing higher than F 5, and no two partials a little apart, so nothing beats. **The mark:** silence until asked, then the answer struck once, both gongs together, F 3 on the low one and C 5 on the high one. It sounds as the speaking clock answers ("They heard the time"), at the title card, as Genie One answers Rohan, and at the takeaway. F major for an answer on demand; D minor for the problem (the exchange that couldn't answer on time, the report and the week); all tuned pure from F, and G never struck with B flat or D, whose intervals would beat. No chime tune: one note at a time, or two struck together, never the quarters' ding-dong or the pips of a time signal.

- **Under the opening,** D minor on the low gong, one note at a time, as callers ring the exchange and the operator reads the time from the wall; never quite exact, late when the board is busy, the watch out; asked again and again; and nothing under "Something had to change". At the university, D minor again under the report and the week; F major as any question is answered when it's asked; the mark at the title card.

## Rigour to check before recording

Checked on 5 November 2026.

- **London, 1936, and the operator.** Before the speaking clock, callers asked the operator, who gave "the time by the exchange clock on the wall", and it was "not precise to the second, nor could the exchange always answer just when the customer wanted" (Andrew Emmerson, *History of the Speaking Clock*, britishtelephones.com); the film says just that ("never quite exact to the second", "couldn't always answer just when a caller asked"). The watch set by that answer is the film's picture of what it meant. The film says only that "that question no longer needed an operator": no source found says the clock was built to relieve operators, so it doesn't say so.
- **"The Post Office's own engineers built a machine."** Hansard, 3 May 1937: the machine was designed by the Post Office Engineering Research Department. Its card says "built by its own engineers".
- **Jane Cain.** Ethel Jane Cain, a telephonist, the first voice of the speaking clock, chosen in a competition among the Post Office's telephonists; sources differ on the year (1935 or 1936), so the film doesn't say it. Her tag calls her "Operator", the word the film uses for telephonists. Her home town isn't sourced well enough, so it isn't said.
- **"Recorded its words on glass discs … A clock played them at the right moment."** britishtelephones.com: "The sounds which come from this clock are recorded on glass discs", four of them, read by light onto photo-electric cells, and driven by "a master clock having a free pendulum, beating seconds"; Andrew Emmerson: "recorded and replayed rather like the optical sound track of a film".
- **"Every hour, it was checked against Greenwich" and "a tenth of a second".** britishtelephones.com, describing the first machine: "In order that the error between the third pip and true time shall not be more than 0.1 second, the master clock is connected by relays to Greenwich Observatory and synchronisation takes place every hour"; and telephonesuk.org.uk: "Originally the accuracy of the BT Speaking Clock was one-tenth of a second". "Where Britain's time was kept" is the Royal Observatory, Greenwich.
- **"From 24 July 1936, anyone in London could dial TIM."** Hansard, 26 April 1937: "introduced in London on 24th July last". T, I and M are 8, 4 and 6 on a British dial of the time, as the card shows. "At any hour of the day or night": its two clocks were "both running continuously", the second taking over if the first broke down or erred by more than a tenth of a second "at any hour" (britishtelephones.com).
- **"In less than a year, it answered more than 12 million calls."** Hansard, written answer of 31 July 1939: about 12¼ million calls in the year to 30 June 1937, a year that began with the service on 24 July 1936, so less than a year of it; the card says "24 July 1936 to 30 June 1937". (Hansard of 26 April 1937 gives 9,226,320 calls to 17 April 1937, which agrees; Andrew Emmerson gives "nearly 13 million calls" in its first year.)
- **The university's numbers** are the module's, agreed in `numbers.md` with films 7 and 8 before the script used them: casual teaching cost is the claims approved for casual teaching (casual staff, teaching duties), plus on-costs, counted by the teaching period the teaching was done in; Health, Semester 2 2026, so far on Thursday 5 November 2026, A$1.84 million; Semester 1 2026, A$2.21 million; on the dashboard, A$9.6 million paid to 2,960 people on 5 November, casual hours by faculty (Arts 1,950, Business 3,120, Engineering 2,380, Health 2,640, 10,090 in all) and 143 claims waiting for the pay run of 19 November. All are totals: no one's pay or rate is shown, on screen or in the voice. The faculties' names and `teaching_period` values are the example project's ('Faculty of Health', `2026-S2`). Pay days fall every second Thursday (22 October, 5 November, 19 November 2026). The 40-page report and its three figures are the module's.
- **Genie One and a Genie Agent.** Databricks' *Use Genie One* (updated 3 November 2026; "Genie One was previously known as Databricks One"): business users ask questions of their data in their own words. *Genie Agents* (updated 2 November 2026): "A Genie Agent is a domain-specific natural-language chat interface in Databricks where users ask questions of their data and get back SQL queries, results tables, and visualizations. Data analysts curate each agent with datasets registered to Unity Catalog, example SQL queries, SQL expressions for business semantics, and text instructions tailored to the organization's terminology." ("Genie Agents were formerly known as Genie Spaces.") The film's four tabs are those parts, named plainly: Data, Rules (its instructions), Examples (example SQL queries) and Tests (benchmarks). *Show code* is where a Genie answer shows its SQL.
- **"Each answer runs as the person asking."** *Create and manage a Genie Agent* (updated 9 October 2026): "Data access is always evaluated using each end user's own Unity Catalog permissions."

| # | Question for Rodrigo | Assumption taken | Where it shows |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Which framework is the reference for the ways to hide a value? | The series' own table (its proposal) is the syllabus. It agrees with ISO/IEC 20889, the Article 29 Working Party's Opinion 05/2014 (singling out, linking, inferring), the EDPB's 2025 guidelines on pseudonymisation, ENISA's 2019 techniques, NIST SP 800-188 and the statistics offices' rule of hiding a second count. The film names none of them and teaches what they share: three questions for each way. The script's *Reference* says where each comes from. | Script, *Reference*; research.md |
| 2 | Keep the setup's opening, the secret ballot of Victoria in 1856, although *Who did what* also opens in Australia? | Kept. Its mechanism is the film's: the roll says who voted and nothing about how, the box says how and nothing about who, and only totals are announced. | Chapter 1, and the returns in chapters 4 to 6 |
| 3 | Can the film say Victoria's ballot was the first? | No. Tasmania passed a law weeks earlier and France had one in 1795, so the film claims no first. It says the colony passed a secret ballot that March, Britain took it up in 1872, and 38 American states had by 1892. | Chapter 1 |
| 4 | Who gets the credit, Henry Chapman or William Nicholson? | Chapman, who worked out how it would run; Nicholson moved the principle. One name keeps the line short. Neither is drawn: only invented figures in 1850s dress appear. | Chapter 1 |
| 5 | Should the film say that only men could vote? | The narration says "a man", which is accurate; it doesn't open a second topic. The script's rigour notes that Victorian women won the vote in 1908. | Chapter 1; script, *Rigour* |
| 6 | Mask it in part: the series' table says "the last four digits", but a student number has five. | The help desk sees the last two digits and the email's first letter: `S-***12`, `s***@mail.example`. Four of five digits would hide almost nothing. | Chapter 3; numbers.md |
| 7 | When is it "leave it out" and when "mask it whole"? | Leave a column out when the reader needs nothing of it, not even to know it's there: exam support, which is special and lives in its own restricted table, isn't given to the study. Mask it whole when one table serves people who may see a column and people who may not: the registrar's office needs names, the study reads stars. | Chapter 3 |
| 8 | Which learner is the example? | Samuel Osei, S-20412, from the example project, studying Public health (90 of 120 points). His date of birth, 14 May 1987, is invented (the project has none). Settled with *Know what you hold*. His exam support is never shown. | Chapters 1 to 6; numbers.md |
| 9 | What does the study's keyed hash use as its key? | One key for the study, kept sealed by the registrar's office, apart from the study. On screen the key is a sealed card; the code `a7772705…` was made with a key drawn at random for the film and thrown away. | Chapter 4; research.md |
| 10 | What is the token's example? | The email becomes `TKN-0381`, paired with the real value in a vault the registrar's office keeps. The study reaches no one, so it takes the keyed hash; the token is for reaching a person again, for a lawful reason. | Chapter 4; numbers.md |
| 11 | Where does encryption fit? | It starts from *Walls, doors and keys*: data is encrypted where it's stored and moved, with the university's own key. The film says what that does (unreadable without the key, all there for anyone allowed to read it), so encryption never shows a reader less. | Chapter 4 |
| 12 | How does the film come back to the story for "only with the key"? | With Britain's ballot of 1872: each paper had a number, matched to its voter only in a sealed packet, opened only to trace a fraud. Whether Victoria's 1856 papers were numbered isn't in the sources read, so the film says nothing about it. | Chapter 4 |
| 13 | Which courses and numbers are in the published totals? | Invented: Nursing 38, Midwifery 4, Public health 21, Health sciences 17, Faculty of Health 80 left after first year, and about 9 in 10 went on to second year. | 14 | Which second count is hidden so the small one can't be worked out? | The next smallest, Health sciences' 17, as statistics offices do, to hide the least. | Chapter 5 |

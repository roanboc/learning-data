## The promise

Every line passes two tests: someone new to leading people can follow it, and an experienced data manager finds nothing wrong in it. The film's idea is ownership: answering for an outcome, not only doing a task. An owner knows who uses the work and for which decision, notices before anyone asks, says so first when something is wrong, and fixes it or raises it. It takes one named owner, with the authority to match, and it is never blame: where nobody is blamed, saying it early is safe.

**Takeaway:** "Own what it's for, not only what you did."

## The story in one paragraph

Kenya, in the 1970s: in the hills of the central highlands, rural women tell Wangari Maathai, a biologist, that their streams are drying up, their food is less sure, and each year they walk further for firewood. The forests had been cleared for farms, for tea and coffee to sell, and for timber; each choice made sense to the people who made it, but without trees the soil washed off the hills and the springs ran dry, and the harm fell first on the women, who fetched the water, gathered the firewood and grew the food. In 1977 Maathai starts the Green Belt Movement with the National Council of Women of Kenya. Groups of women grow seedlings in their own nurseries from the seeds of local trees and plant them out, and they are paid a small sum for each tree still alive months later, not for each seedling put in the ground: they answer for living trees, not for the planting. More than fifty million trees have been planted in Kenya since; they give fuel, food and shelter, and thousands of women earned an income from them; in 2004 Maathai became the first African woman to win the Nobel Peace Prize. At the university, a year from now, Ivy Reyes becomes the owner of her first data product on Thursday 1 July: *Teaching space use*, counts per room per hour, never a person, refreshed every Monday by seven. She knows its readers by name (Graham Ellis at Facilities, Mei Tanaka at the registrar's office) and what it's for: next year's timetable will move about one peak class in six and release rooms nobody uses, chosen from her counts. Many do part of the work; one answers for the outcome, and Helena gives her the authority to match. On Tuesday 17 August Facilities upgrades the sensors in forty rooms, a real improvement nobody expects to change the data. On Monday 23 August the refresh runs at seven and every check passes, but Ivy reads the numbers: in those forty rooms the counts have dropped to about a quarter, and booked hours with nobody in them jump from one in five to more than half. At ten past eight, before she knows why, she writes to the readers: don't use them yet; the right ones by tomorrow. With Sam she finds the cause: four counts an hour, where the model was built for one, and a step that kept the first, as one class leaves. She fixes what's hers (keep the hour's highest), Jun reviews it, the days are run again, and on Tuesday the readers have the right counts and a short note. The sensors aren't hers, so she raises it with Graham, and Facilities now tells the data team before a sensor changes; her own new check would hold the next refresh. Nobody did anything wrong: what failed was the way the parts fit together. Helena's first question is what happened and what Ivy needs, never who.

## The reference, and practice

The brief names three references, each named once; the film teaches what they share: one person answers for what the work is for, with the authority to act on it, and when something goes wrong the question is what happened and what must change, not who to blame. *A product with an owner* (the data governance films, film 8) shows how a product gets an owner; it is named once and not retold.

| Body of knowledge | What it adds | Where the film uses it |
|---|---|---|
| Zhamak Dehghani, data mesh: "Data Mesh Principles and Logical Architecture" (martinfowler.com, 2020) and *Data Mesh* (O'Reilly, 2022) | Data as a product, owned by the domain that knows it; the domain data product owner answers for what the product is worth to the people who use it, not only for the pipeline | "Zhamak Dehghani's data mesh calls her the data product owner: she answers for what the product does for its readers." |
| RACI, the responsibility assignment matrix | Responsible, accountable, consulted, informed: many can do part of the work, and in the common practice only one person is accountable for each piece of it | "Many people do part of the work." "One person answers for the outcome. In a RACI chart, that's the A, for accountable, and there's only one." |
| Sidney Dekker, *Just Culture: Balancing Safety and Accountability* (2007) | After something goes wrong, ask what happened and what must change, rather than who to blame; accountability as giving an account, looking forward, so people can say what went wrong without fear | "Sidney Dekker calls it a just culture: when something goes wrong, ask what happened and what must change, not who to blame." "Being accountable means giving an account: what happened, and how the next time goes better." |
| Practice | An owner reads the numbers, not only the green tick; tells the readers first, before the fix; fixes what's theirs and raises what isn't; and adds the check that would have caught it | The whole data story, and the four steps of *On Monday* |

**Where the film adopts, extends or differs.** It adopts data mesh's product owner (who answers for the product's value to its readers) without the rest of data mesh's architecture, which a newcomer doesn't need for this decision. It adopts RACI's one A, and uses the chart only for that: one person answers, and many do part of the work. It adopts Dekker's question (what happened, not who) and his reading of accountability as an account given. It extends them from practice: ownership needs authority to match (Ivy can hold a refresh, change the model with a review and tell the readers without asking first), and an owner says so first, before the fix. It differs from a common reading of "ownership" as doing every part yourself: Ivy doesn't own the sensors or the platform, and a fix that isn't hers is raised, not done.

**Practice.** A practitioner will recognise the Monday when every check passes and the numbers are wrong; an improvement upstream that nobody thought would change the data; a step written for the data as it was; the note that goes out before the cause is known; the change that isn't yours to fix; and the review where the first question is what happened, not who.

## Sound

**The palette, its own** (reserved for this film by the module's `palettes.md` on 5 November 2026; the partner chosen by this film's thread on 10 November 2026): **a daxophone**, a thin wooden tongue clamped to a block at one end and bowed at the other, its note set by where a small wooden block, held in the other hand, presses on it, heard through a contact pickup. Bowed, it speaks like a voice, which is why it can sound comic: here it is played plainly, each note found and held still, warm and short. Its partner is **a second, longer and thicker tongue**, bowed in short strokes low down: a round wooden bass with few high partials, the same instrument cut for another register. Made in `source/tools/score.py`. A bowed note is kept alive by the bow, which carries the film's idea: a planted seedling is a short sound that dies; a living tree is a note held. No plucked tongue, which would sound like an African lamellophone, and nothing Kenyan for the Green Belt: no Kenyan tune, rhythm, drum or instrument.

## Rigour to check before recording

Checked on 10 November 2026; the sources are listed below.

- **What rural women told Maathai.** The Green Belt Movement's own history: the movement began from "the needs of rural Kenyan women who reported that their streams were drying up, their food supply was less secure, and they had to walk further and further to get firewood". Maathai's Nobel Lecture (Oslo, 10 December 2004) names the needs "identified by rural women, namely lack of firewood, clean drinking water, balanced diets, shelter and income". The film says the women told her in "the hills of the central highlands": she grew up in Nyeri, in the central highlands, where her lecture remembers the forests cleared and the stream that dried up; the movement's women came from rural Kenya more widely, which the film's general "rural women" keeps (an assumption, in this film's `assumptions.md`).
- **Maathai, a biologist.** A bachelor's degree in biology (1964), a master's in biological sciences (1966) and a doctorate in veterinary anatomy (1971), the first woman in East and Central Africa with a doctorate (Wikipedia, *Wangarĩ Maathai*). The film says "a biologist" and no title.
- **The cause.** The lecture: "forests being cleared and replaced by commercial plantations", and "the introduction of commercial farming, which replaced the growing of household food crops". Tea and coffee are the central highlands' best-known crops for sale, said in general words, with no number; the plantations of timber are the lecture's commercial plantations. "Each choice made sense to the people who made it" is the film's own line: farms, crops to sell and timber each met real needs, and the film blames nobody.
- **The soil and the springs.** Without trees the soil washes off the slopes (the Goldman Prize's account: her work set out "to help curb soil erosion"; secondary accounts of the streams near her home, drying or silted with run-off); her lecture's stream that "has dried up". The film says it in general words.
- **The women's work.** The lecture: "women are the primary caretakers, holding significant responsibility for tilling the land and feeding their families"; the movement's history: the walk for firewood "further and further"; Wikipedia's *Green Belt Movement*: with less firewood, mothers "could not cook their traditional foods". So the harm fell first on them, and on their children.
- **1977.** "Founded by Professor Wangari Maathai in 1977 under the auspices of the National Council of Women of Kenya (NCWK)" (the movement's history); the film says "with the National Council of Women of Kenya". It doesn't tell the first planting on World Environment Day, 5 June 1977, where the sources differ on the number of trees.
- **Nurseries and local seeds.** Women's groups trained in "seed sowing and nursery development", collecting "indigenous seeds" for their nurseries and planting the seedlings out (the University of Kansas Community Tool Box's case study of the movement); thousands of group nurseries across Kenya (the Goldman Prize). The film says "from the seeds of local trees".
- **Paid for each tree still alive.** "Women receive a financial token for the seedlings that survive", their growth checked "at one and three month intervals" (the Community Tool Box); accounts of the movement give the token as a few cents a tree. Wikipedia's *Wangarĩ Maathai* says only "a small stipend for each seedling which was later planted elsewhere", without the condition; the film follows the sources that give it, and says "a small sum" and "months later", with no amount and no number of months (an assumption, in `assumptions.md`).
- **Fifty million.** "Since Wangari Maathai started the movement in 1977, more than 51 million trees have been planted" (Wikipedia, *Green Belt Movement*); the film says "more than fifty million", in Kenya.
- **What they give.** The lecture, in 2004: trees "that provide fuel, food, shelter, and income". "Thousands of women earned an income": more than 30,000 women trained in forestry, food processing, bee-keeping and other trades (Wikipedia, *Green Belt Movement*), and payment for each tree alive; "thousands" is the safe reading.
- **The Nobel Peace Prize.** "In 2004, she became the first African woman to win the Nobel Peace Prize" (Wikipedia, *Wangarĩ Maathai*). The prize's own citation isn't quoted.
- **Nothing borrowed.** Nothing Kenyan in the score; nothing from a culture's own patterns or crafts in the picture; Maathai and the women drawn as composites, never likenesses.
- **The university's numbers.** The module's `numbers.md`: Ivy's product from Thursday 1 July 2027 (owner Ivy Reyes; refreshed every Monday by 07:00 with the week before's bookings and head counts; counts per room per hour only, never a person); the sensors' change on Tuesday 17 August 2027 in 40 rooms, seen on the Monday after; what the team found in April 2027 (moving about one peak class in six and releasing booked rooms nobody uses); the University Council's decision of 24 June 2027, the timetable change from Semester 1 2028 and a review in June 2029 ("In June 2029, the timetable change will be judged on them"). Thursday 1 July, Tuesday 17 August, Monday 23 August and Tuesday 24 August 2027 fall on those days.

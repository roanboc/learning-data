## The promise

Every line passes two tests: someone new to data work can follow it, and a data manager, a data analyst or an analytics engineer finds nothing wrong in it. Its idea is that explaining is for a decision: start with the decision and the answer, then the evidence; show the part of the data the decision turns on (the peak, the spread), not only the average; say what isn't known; write one page in the readers' words, with the same numbers for everyone; and let the people who decide, decide. The author's advice of 10 November 2026 is in it too: a picture beside every page, drawn as the page is written, never last.

**Takeaway:** "Lead with the decision; show what it turns on."

## The story in one paragraph

In 1950 the United States Air Force measured more than 4,000 of its flyers, 132 measurements each, for the people who designed flying suits, helmets and cockpits. Many designers took one figure from the survey: the average man, about 5 ft 9 in, who seemed the easiest to fit and the commonest. But a doorway built to his height would make about half the men stoop, and in a cockpit a control just out of reach, or a pedal too far away, means a pilot can't fly safely. In 1952 Lieutenant Gilbert Daniels, a scientist in the Air Force's anthropology section, asked how many flyers are average. He took ten measurements used to size clothing and counted a man as average on each if he was in the middle 30%: of 4,063 men, 1,055 were average in height, 302 in height and chest, and fewer at each step, until none was average on all ten. His seven-page note said so in its first lines, and showed what a design turns on: the range, 5 ft 5 in to 6 ft 1 in fitting nine flyers in ten. Designers came to fit the range; by the 1970s the Air Force designed its cockpits for the middle 90 pilots in every 100, with seats that rise and fall and pedals that move, as a car's seat slides. At the university, a year from now, Graham Ellis, the Director of Facilities, must decide whether to ask the University Council for a new teaching building, forty rooms for about A$140 million, proposed because the rooms are always full. The data team agreed his question with him in February (too few rooms, or too few at some hours?) and had the answer by late April: too few, only at some hours. He must put it to the University Council on one page by Tuesday 1 June. Ivy Reyes, a data analyst, drafts it with Helena Novak, the data manager. Her first draft runs to twelve pages in the order the work was done, the answer on page nine, its first chart the week's average: rooms booked 38% of the time. Helena asks who will read it, and what they will decide with it. The page Ivy writes opens with the decision, then the answer in one sentence (rooms are short only from 10:00 to 14:00, Tuesday to Thursday), then three lines: the peak, the bookings nobody used, and what it would take not to build. Beside it she draws the whole week as a heat map: 93% of rooms booked at the peak, about one in five at most other hours, and in one booked hour in five the sensors counted nobody. Moving about one peak class in six to other hours, and releasing booked rooms nobody uses, meets Planning's forecast to 2030; the page says what isn't known, and how far the answer holds. Mei, from the registrar's office, who wasn't in the work, reads it in two minutes, says it back, and stops at "utilisation", which to her means seats filled; the page says booked and used instead, and who would plan which classes move. On Thursday 24 June the University Council defers the building for two years and asks for the timetable change from Semester 1 2028, with a review in June 2029; the meeting argues about the choice, not about the numbers.

The test: someone who has never heard of Daniels can say, from the narration alone, why it was a problem (a doorway or a cockpit built to the average man fits about half the men, and a pilot who can't reach a control or a pedal can't fly safely) and what the fix gave people (cockpits designed for the middle ninety pilots in every hundred, with seats and pedals that move to each pilot, as a car's seat slides). **Where the film adopts, extends or differs.** It adopts Minto's order (the answer first, then the reasons, then the evidence) for the page, and puts above the answer what the University Council's members start from, the decision, since they decide on each paper; the answer it gives is to a question agreed in writing before the work began (*The question behind the request*). It adopts Tufte's "show the data" and narrows it to the decision: the picture shows the part of the data the decision turns on, the peak and the spread, not every chart the work made, which is Knaflic's "the one thing they need to see". It differs from the common reading of storytelling with data, a story built to persuade: the page argues for no choice, says what isn't known and how far the answer holds, and leaves the choice to the people who make it ("The page didn't decide for them. It let them see what they were choosing."). Nothing is hidden: the method, the other charts and one true finding the decision doesn't turn on (rooms about half full when people came) are at the back, for anyone who wants them.

**Practice.** A practitioner will recognise the twelve-page draft in the order the work was done, with the answer on page nine; an average that both sides of an argument read as proof; a word that means one thing to the team and another to its readers (utilisation, as rooms booked or seats filled); the readers' first question that a page didn't answer (which classes would move?); and a meeting that argues about the numbers because each side brought its own. The advice is the briefs' and the author's: lead with the decision; one message per page; show the distribution, not only the mean; say the uncertainty; use the readers' words; try it on someone who wasn't in the work; draw the picture with the page.

### 1 · No average pilot · 0:00–2:33

**Narration.** In 1950, the United States Air Force measured more than four thousand of its flyers: 132 measurements each. The figures went to the people who designed what flyers wear and where they sit: suits, helmets, cockpits. Many designers took one figure from them: the average man, about five foot nine. He seemed the easiest flyer to fit, and the commonest. But a doorway built to his height would make about half the men stoop. In a cockpit it's worse: a control just out of reach, or a pedal too far away, and a pilot can't fly safely. Something had to change. In 1952, Lieutenant Gilbert Daniels was a scientist in the Air Force's anthropology section. He asked a plain question: how many flyers are average? He took ten measurements used to size clothing. On each, a man counted as average if he was in the middle thirty percent: a generous band. Of 4,063 men, 1,055 were average in height. Of those, 302 were average in chest too. Each measurement left fewer: 143, 73, 28, 12, 6, 3, 2. Average on all ten: not one. His note, seven pages long, said so in its first lines: it is virtually impossible to find an average man in the Air Force. Then it showed what a design turns on: the range. From five foot five to six foot one fits nine flyers in ten. Designers came to fit the range, not the average. By the 1970s, the Air Force designed its cockpits for the middle ninety pilots in every hundred. The seats rise and fall, and the pedals move. A car's seat slides for the same reason. Data has its average men too: one number that seems to describe everything, and describes almost nothing. Lead with the decision, show the part of the data it turns on, and the people who decide can see what they're choosing.

### 4 · What it turns on · 4:53–6:18

**Narration.** Now the chart. The week's average, 38%, hides what the decision turns on. So Ivy draws the whole week: a heat map, the days down the side, the hours across, each hour as dark as the share of rooms booked. From ten to two, Tuesday to Thursday, 93% of rooms are booked. Most other hours, about one in five. The academics who said the rooms are always full were right, at the peak. So were Facilities, who saw empty corridors on Monday mornings and Friday afternoons. Edward Tufte's advice for charts: above all else, show the data. Cole Nussbaumer Knaflic's: know who you're showing, and the one thing they need to see.

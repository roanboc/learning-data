## The promise

Every line passes two tests: anyone who has used a chat assistant at work can follow it, and an experienced data or enterprise architect finds nothing wrong in it. Its idea: what an agent says depends on what it reads, so its context belongs on the map. It reads the official data products, each kept by its own team with a contract, and the glossary's terms with them, never an export or a copy of a copy. Each product has a level, and the agent reads only those it may, from a place of its own that holds nothing else, reached by its own identity: what it may not read isn't there at all, rather than hidden behind a mask that one wrong rule would open. A person's own data it reads only on that person's own sign-in. Where it needs one fact from data it may not read, the team that keeps that data offers a small tool that answers only what the agent needs, never why. The owners agree to each new reader in a ticket, the data team builds and reviews the change in a pull request, and the regulatory lead checks it in test.

**Takeaway:** "An agent's context belongs on the map: the products it may read, with contracts and terms, in a place of its own, under its own identity."

## The story in one paragraph

In Uruk, in the south of Babylonia, in December 229 BCE, a scribe is copying a tablet that gives the measures of Babylon's temple-tower, Etemenanki, stage by stage; his original is a tablet from Borsippa, a city near Babylon. Every copy was made by hand, and every hand could slip; a copy of a copy carried every slip before it, so one wrong number made the tower's measures wrong for everyone who read them after. And this was learning kept for the initiated, the scholars trained to read it, not for every eye: a reader had to know where a copy came from, that it had been checked, and who it was meant for. So the scribe copies the tablet from Borsippa and checks his copy against it; at its end he writes where it came from, that it was copied and checked, and who may read it: "May the initiate instruct the initiate. The uninitiate must not see." His tablet came down to us, still telling its reader how far to trust it, and the base it gives, 180 cubits, about 90 metres a side, is close to the 91.5 metres archaeologists measured in the ground. At the energy utility today, the website's chat answers households about their bills, their prices and planned work in their street. Farah, the customer advocate, brings Tomás two complaints: a household was quoted last year's price and planned its winter by it, and a caller who wasn't the account holder was told that someone in the home needs power for medical equipment. The chat's first version read whatever it could reach: prices from an export on a shared drive that nobody kept up to date, and the contact centre's notes, where people's medical needs are written down; Ama, the regulatory lead, reported a breach. Inés Duarte, the new data architect, draws the chat's context from the data practice on Tomás's map: tariffs, planned work by street, help pages and a household's account, each kept by its own team with a contract, and the glossary's terms with them; no exports, no copies of copies. Each product has a level; the chat reads public and internal ones, and never restricted ones. Inés doesn't leave medical needs in reach behind a mask: she gives the chat a place of its own, holding only what it may read, which its own identity can reach, and nothing else. A household's account, confidential, isn't there either: the chat reads one only on its holder's own sign-in. When planned work will cut a street's power, a small tool from the team that keeps the list answers only "hand this to a person now", never why, and a person calls back. The head of retail asks for the change in a ticket, each product's owner agrees in it, the data team builds and reviews it in a pull request, and Ama checks in test that nothing restricted is in the chat's place. On Databricks, the chat is an app with its own service principal, its place one schema in Unity Catalog, and the small tool a function it may run. In three months it answers 41,000 questions about prices, every one from this year's tariffs, and no caller hears about any household but their own.

Where the film differs: ArchiMate has no element for an agent's context, a level or the place an agent reads from. The film draws the products as the data practice does, in the business layer, each level as a coloured pill on the information layer below it, and the chat's place as a room of glass round what it may read; the notation's details stay in *The map before the data*. How an agent finds and uses what it reads (search, retrieval, how a model writes its answer) stays in the AI agents module; the film says only what the chat reads. Masks and row filters keep their place for people who may see part of a table, as *One policy for many tables* shows, and for the agents that work on the data team's pipelines, which read production with personal and sensitive data masked to find a cause (AGENTS.md, 12 November 2026); for an agent that answers the public, the film keeps what it may not read out of its reach altogether. Its own identity reads no personal data at all, and a household's account only on that household's own sign-in, as the website does; the page's answer after *Only what it's meant to read* says so.

Practice, from the author and the series' plan: draw an agent's context from the map, never from whatever it can reach; let it read the official products, with their contracts, and never an export or a copy of a copy; bring the glossary's terms with them; give it a place of its own that holds only what it may read, and an identity of its own that reaches only that place, instead of a mask over what it mustn't read; read a person's own data only on their own sign-in; where it needs one fact from data it may not read, ask the team that keeps it for a small tool that answers only what it needs, and let a person, who checks who they're speaking to, see why; agree each new reader with each product's owner, in a ticket; and check in test that nothing restricted is in its place. Every one of these is said or shown in the film.

## Sound

A new palette for this film, reserved on 12 November 2026 in the four architecture series' palettes, made in [`source/tools/score.py`](source/tools/score.py): a viola d'amore, the bowed alto of about 1700, its seven playing strings tuned to a D major chord and seven more beneath them that no one touches, tuned the same, so that a bowed note matching one of them makes it ring in sympathy after the bow lifts, while a note that matches none gets no answer; bowed softly and held level, with no vibrato. And tuning forks on wooden resonator boxes, struck softly with felt, one pure note each, damped by hand after two or three seconds so none is held under the voice: the measure a bowed note is checked against. The strings beneath, which answer only the notes they're tuned to, are the film's idea: a copy checked against its original, and a reader that answers only to what it's meant to read. The copies nobody checked play notes they don't answer (G, E, C sharp), and so does what the chat never reads (B). D major, B minor for what goes wrong (a copy of a copy; the old price; a stranger told a household's medical needs), tuned pure from D, with no E minor. Its edition is A. R.

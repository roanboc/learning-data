## The promise

Every line passes two tests: anyone who has ever signed in at work can follow it, and an identity engineer finds nothing wrong in it. A key that still opens its door after the stay is the oldest hole in security, and in the 1970s the hotels closed it with a lock that a punched card opens, set anew for each guest. A data platform can do the same: let one directory say who someone is and which groups they're in, let every door read it, sign everyone in once with a passkey, and keep great power in groups nobody is in, switched on for a task and off again. **People sign in once, with no password to steal; power is borrowed, not kept.**

## The story in one paragraph

Moss, on a fjord in Norway, in the 1970s. Tor Sørnes leads the research of a factory that makes locks and ice skates. Hotels have a problem with their keys: guests forget to hand them in, or lose them, and a key still opens its door, whoever holds it, so anyone who found one or kept one could let themselves into the next guest's room while that guest slept. Sørnes makes a lock that a punched plastic card opens: inside each lock is a pattern card, put in from inside the room, changed for each new guest, whose card is punched to match, so the last guest's card opens nothing. In 1978 the first go into a big new hotel in Atlanta, troubled by burglaries; by 2011, the company says, its card locks are on six and a half million hotel rooms in 166 countries. At the university, Sam Okafor keeps the data platform. Its tables already give their rights to groups from the university's directory, but who may come in at all is a list kept by hand: 214 people added one by one, nineteen of whom have moved on and still get in; four groups made inside the workspace that the directory knows nothing of; eleven admins on the accounts they read their email with; a password and a text code for everyone; and Jun Park's personal token, with no end date, running the nightly job. The directory is Microsoft Entra ID, fed by HR's records; Azure Databricks now reads its people and groups for itself, each workspace is given groups, never people, and a move or a departure needs nothing on the platform. Everyone signs in once, with a passkey a fake page can't catch, after accepting their confidentiality agreement, again each year. Every admin role belongs to a group no one is in; Sam's and Jun's admin accounts are only eligible, and privileged identity management switches one on for a task, with a reason, the other's approval and an alert, then off by itself. On 19 October Sam switches on the account's settings for an hour to read the token report: seven personal tokens. The command line now signs in through the browser, so six people end theirs; Jun's runs the job until *Workloads without secrets* gives the job its own way in, and on 3 November Sam, switched on for thirty minutes, turns personal tokens off. Like the hotel's card, every key now ends with the stay.

## The reference, and practice

The module's reference holds (its proposal, *The reference*): DAMA-DMBOK's data security area for what is protected and why, and zero trust (NIST SP 800-207) for how, where every request proves who it is and rights are given per session. This is a tool film, so its references are the tools' own guidance, read on 5 November 2026 and again on 10 November 2026, and the standards behind them.

| Body of knowledge | What it adds | Where the film uses it |
|---|---|---|
| NIST SP 800-207, *Zero trust architecture* (2020) | Identity is the boundary; access is decided per request from the identity, its groups and the strength of its sign-in, never from where the request comes from | *One directory*, *Sign in once* |
| NIST SP 800-63B-4 (July 2025) and CISA's *Implementing phishing-resistant MFA* (2022) | Phishing-resistant sign-in: a key bound to the real site, never sent; passwords and text codes can be caught by a fake page | *Sign in once* (built on *No secret to lose*) |
| Microsoft Learn, *Securing privileged access* and *Privileged Identity Management* | Separate admin accounts; no standing admin power; just-in-time activation with a reason, approval, multifactor sign-in, a time limit and an alert | *Power you borrow* (built on *Power you switch on*) |
| Microsoft Learn for Azure Databricks: *Automatic identity management*, *Groups*, *Monitor and revoke personal access tokens*, *Authentication for the Databricks CLI* | Identities from Microsoft Entra ID at the account; workspaces given groups; the account admin role on a group; OAuth instead of personal tokens: "Databricks recommends you use OAuth access tokens instead of PATs for greater security and convenience" | *One directory*, *Power you borrow*, *No token to copy* |
| The joiner, mover and leaver process (identity governance practice; Microsoft Entra's HR-driven provisioning and lifecycle workflows) | HR's record as the source: a joiner gets an account in the right groups, a mover's groups change, a leaver's account is turned off | *One directory* (built on *Who is it, really?*) |

**Where the film differs, on purpose.** The vendors teach each feature on its own; the film follows one idea through all of them, a key that ends with the stay: a place that ends with the job, power that ends with the task, a sign-in that leaves nothing to steal. It doesn't teach SCIM, OAuth flows, Conditional Access design or PIM's every setting: one rule, one switch-on and one command, each shown once.

**Practice.** What practitioners recognise: workspace users added by hand and never removed; groups made inside one workspace that no directory knows; admins "just for now" who stay admins for years, on the accounts they read email with; people who become workspace admins because they have a role on the Azure subscription, and stay admins after the role goes; text codes caught by a fake page in real time; personal tokens pasted into command lines and report tools; and approvals for admin power that become a click. And what works: the directory as the only place a person or a group is changed; workspaces given groups only; admin accounts kept apart, signed in with a security key, eligible and never standing; an alert to people who read it; the command line signing in through the browser; personal tokens off.

**The author's practice** (3 November 2026, in the module's proposal): "we usually have special admin accounts (each of the team members) to perform more delicate activities", raised only when needed, for a task, with a reason, and logged; never for business users or analysts; never for agents. The film shows it in the tools: "Only two people can switch one on, Sam and Jun, each from a second account kept for it, signed in only with a security key."

## Sound

A new palette, checked on 5 November 2026 against every `tools/score.py` on main and on every branch, and against every palettes list in the project's working folders: **an EMS VCS3 and a square bass recorder**, made for this film in `tools/score.py` (the module's `palettes.md`).

- **The VCS3 leads, quietly.** The small synthesizer Electronic Music Studios made in London in 1969 (Peter Zinovieff, Tristram Cary and David Cockerell), sold from 1970: its sounds are joined on a pin board, a grid of sixteen holes by sixteen, where a pin pushed into a hole connects one row to one column, and pulling it out ends the connection. Played softly, one or two notes at a time, a round tone of sine and a little triangle through its low-pass filter, opened gently as each note starts and settling, no vibrato, nothing sweeping, nothing above 2.4 kHz. It's the opening's decade, the card's holes and pins, and the film's grid of groups and doors: a right only where a pin is in.
- **The square bass recorder is the people.** A recorder of plywood with a square bore, as Herbert Paetzold began making them about 1975 from his uncle Joachim's design: low (F2 to F3, under the voice), breathy and wooden, one voice, no vibrato. Held only under the opening, at a chapter's turn and at the end; there's none at the turn into *Power you borrow*, where the groups have no one in them.
- **The mark.** A chord built pin by pin, one note joining at a time. At a switch-on, one note (E flat) joins for the task and leaves cleanly when it ends, and the chord goes on without it; at the end it joins on "power is borrowed", and leaves on "not kept".
- **Keys.** D-flat major, tuned pure from D-flat, for the hotels and the platform that follows the directory; B-flat minor for the problem (*Keys that outlive the stay*, *Still a key*, what outlived the stay at the university, the seven tokens). No E-flat minor chord, and no E flat held with B flat or G flat: their fifths aren't pure in this tuning.
- **Music** under the opening story (*Keys that outlive the stay* and *A card for each stay*, to the title), then only at a chapter's turn, under a moment on screen (the pattern card going in, automatic identity management switched on, the passkey, the agreement accepted, the switch-on and its end, Jun's token ended, the toggle) or in a pause without words; never a bed under the voice. ## Rigour

Checked on 5 November 2026, and the Microsoft Learn pages again on 10 November 2026; the sources are listed below.

- **Moss, the factory and Sørnes.** Tor Sørnes (1925–2017) joined Christiania Staal & Jernvarefabrikk in Moss in 1950; "The factory made locks and ice skates under the brand Ving"; he became its machine constructor in 1955 and, in 1960, director of its new research department (Wikipedia, *Tor Sørnes*). "Leads its research" says that. Moss lies on the Oslofjord.
- **The problem.** In the patent's own words: "Such locks are of special interest for hotels, where it is not uncommon that the guests forget to leave the key upon departure or that the key is lost in some other way" (US 4,149,394). "A key still opens its door, whoever holds it" is what a mechanical key does until the lock is changed; "could let themselves into the next guest's room, while that guest slept" is the harm that follows, said as a risk, not as an event. Wikipedia links the lock's launch to an attack on a guest in her hotel room in 1974; that attack involved no key, so the film doesn't tell it, and names no one.
- **The lock.** US patent 4,149,394, "Lock arrangement employing mechanically acting code card and key card", inventor Tor Sørnes, filed 28 March 1978 (Norwegian priority 6 April 1977), granted 17 April 1979: "A plate-like key member and a plate-like code member are respectively insertable into slots provided in the housing"; the code card is changed "simply ... by exchanging the old code card with a new one", and "the code card only may be introduced in its slot from the inside of the door. Only persons having access to the room will thus be able to exchange the code card and thereby invalidate the key card previously used". The film's "pattern card, put in from inside the room" is the code card; "a card punched to match" is the key card, whose holes and the code card's together let the pins drop. The number of holes differs between sources (32 positions in Wikipedia's *Keycard lock*; a 5 by 5 grid in the patent), so the film shows a pattern and gives no number.

## The promise

Every line passes two tests: someone new to leading people can follow it, and an experienced data manager finds nothing wrong in it. The film answers the first of the series' four questions: who is here? A team is people before it is roles. A chart shows who reports to whom, and a list shows what is asked; neither shows who is in the boxes, what each is proud of, what ties them, or who the others turn to. So whoever comes new to lead a team sits down with each person before planning anyone's work, asks about them at work and never about anything private, notices who people turn to and makes room for it, and says early what won't change yet. The film labels nobody, ranks nobody and shows nothing private.

**Takeaway:** "Know the people before you plan the work."

## The story in one paragraph

Quehue, in the high Andes of Cusco, Peru: a bridge of twisted grass hangs over a gorge of the Apurímac river, and by its communities' own account people have crossed there on rope since the time of the Incas. It was once the only way between them, and since grass wears out, many hands make it new. In 1968 a steel bridge is built almost a hundred metres away. Cars cross with their loads, few people need the rope, and a geographer who visits in 1970 finds it decaying, not raised for a few years, and expects the skill of making it to die with that generation. In October 1972 one of the bridge's keepers tells two visitors what his people have decided: they feel sad about abandoning it, so they will rebuild it when the new year comes. They do, by their own choice, and where few had come to help, an elder says, everyone now comes. Today four communities make it new each June: about a thousand people, each family with its own cord, as *minka*. A steel bridge could carry them across; it couldn't bring them together. At the university, a year from now, Helena Novak starts as data manager on Monday 1 February 2027, with two sheets on her desk: a chart of seven boxes and a list of 46 open requests. She sorts the list that day, as *Every area, one job* shows, and changes nobody's work. Before she plans anything, she sits down with each of the seven, from Monday to Thursday, where each works, with the same four questions about them at work. Each tells her one small, true thing that no chart holds, and she hears one name again and again: when they are stuck, four of the others turn to Jun, who is one box of seven. She doesn't move his box: she asks if he wants this, and makes room for it. On Friday 5 February she tells all seven what she can promise: who does what stays as it is this month. She has planned nobody's work yet, but she knows seven people, and they know what to expect from her.

The test: someone who has never heard of the bridge can say, from the narration alone, why it was a problem (once steel stood beside it, few people needed the rope to cross; it was left to decay, the skill of making it was expected to die with that generation, and its people felt sad about abandoning it, because it was more to them than a way across) and what the fix gave people (the bridge, made new by their own choice; everyone coming, where few had come before; and today about a thousand people of four communities, each family with its cord, tied to each other in a way a steel bridge couldn't give them). The story is blameless: nobody is at fault for the steel bridge, which carries the cars, and most people, to this day. The film says what steel could do and what it couldn't.

**Where the film adopts, extends or differs.** It adopts the standard's two principles in plain words, without its list of seven; Ubuntu as *The team comes first* words it; and the finding on shared leadership as its authors report it, with the caution they give (it helps how a team works together more than what it delivers). It extends them from practice: the four questions, the hour with each person in the first week, and the promise of what stays are what practitioners do, not what a standard or a study prescribes, and the film doesn't say otherwise. It differs from the common first week of a new manager, a plan for everyone made from the chart and the list: Helena sorts the list, since people are waiting, and plans nobody's work until she knows who is here. And where the research describes shared leadership as a property of a team, the film shows one small case of it and one thing a manager can do: leave the chart alone, ask the person, and make room.

**Practice.** A practitioner will recognise the two sheets on a new manager's desk and the pull to plan from them by lunch; the first conversations, which go best where the person works; the questions that are safe to ask and the ones that aren't; the one person everyone turns to, whose box on the chart looks like any other; and a team that waits, in a manager's first week, to hear what is about to change.

## The look

*Sunlit plaster*, the module's look, with the module's kit from `films/the-new-skills/shared/src`, bundled as it is. Nothing is watercolour, and nothing has film grain.

- **The gorge is the series' own drawing,** made once for the three films that open at the bridge and copied into each film's own source (here `src/pb_place.js`, `src/pb_bridge.js` and `src/pb_people.js`): dark rock, straw-coloured grass, a green river with white water, a stone base on each bank, and a bridge of six cables whose sag and floor change with its age. It is a drawing, not a likeness of the place. The steel bridge is small and far, at the bend. Nothing sacred is drawn: no offering and no priest.
- **The people of the gorge** are dressed from the sources' own photographs and words, in plain colours: felt hats, knitted caps with ear flaps, pale wool jackets, long braids and full skirts, with no pattern invented and none copied. In the wide view they are small figures, about fifty pixels tall; on the bank, half-figures. Each was looked at full size, facing both ways. Nobody drawn is a likeness of a real person: no figure stands for the keeper, the elder or the builder whose words are on screen, and the people who come back to the bank are unnamed.
- **The people are never the grass.** They are the ones who bring the cord and build, and the bridge is what they make together. The film never likens a person to a strand, a cord or a box.
- **The university** is the team's rooms: Helena's room in the morning, each person's own place by day, turning to late light as the week goes on, and the team's room late on Friday. The seven are drawn together for the first time in the module. Helena, Noor, Sam, Jun and Yusuf are the kit's; Marta Silva, Erin Walsh and Tomás Ortega are copied into this film's own source from the module's shared numbers, as they are (`PB_MARTA`, `PB_ERIN`, `PB_TOMAS` in `src/pb_uni.js`), and looked at full size, facing both ways. Marta is drawn here for the first time. Yusuf and Tomás read alike at the size of a row, so they stand at its two ends, each with his name. The first university scene is tagged "The university, 2027 · imagined".
- **Colours carry meaning,** as in every film of the module: blue for plans (the chart), orange for data (the list, and the two numbers from the sources), green for something done or kept (the decision to rebuild, the return, the list sorted, what stays, seven people known), amber for a risk or something waiting (the decay, the plan by lunch, the hazard). Who turns to whom is drawn in pencil, never in a colour. People and their clothes wear none of the five, and the straw, the rock and the river keep clear of them.
- **Its own pieces,** in the film's own source: the gorge, the bridge and its people (above); in `src/pb_uni.js`, the rooms, the desk seen from above, the chart, the list, the pencil and its lines, the week's sheet and the truck; in `src/pb_scenes.js`, the six chapters. Every name starts `pb_` or `PB_`. The film never changes the module's kit.
- **Carried across cuts:** the chart is on the wall above the seven as the opening ends, on Helena's desk through three chapters, and comes back with the pencil's lines at the close. The week's sheet fills day by day and is ticked on Friday. The eight of Friday are the eight of the close. In one room nothing cuts: the camera glides, and each of the seven arrives in the pause before their line, as the one before leaves. The chapters *Four questions, seven people* and *What won't change yet* glide on from the room the chapter before left (`ag_cutInto`).

## Rigour to check before recording

Checked on 12 November 2026; the sources are listed below. "The study" is Peru's Ministry of Culture's *El Q'eswachaka de Canas* (2015), read in full for this film in its Spanish text; the page numbers are its own.

- **The place.** The bridge hangs over a stretch of the Apurímac whose bed is at about 3,700 metres above the sea, in the district of Quehue, province of Canas, Cusco; it is 28 metres long (the study, page 175). The film says "high in the Andes of Peru" and "a deep gorge", and gives no measure.
- **Grass, twisted by hand.** Each family promises a cord of forty arm spans, about seventy metres, twisted by hand from *q'oya* grass, with the children taking part (page 177). The film says "each family bringing its own cord", never that only the women make it.
- **The names.** The voice says *keshwa chaca*, as the keeper says it in the 1973 article the study quotes; the screen says *Q'eswachaka*, as the Ministry and UNESCO spell it.
- **How old.** "By their own account, the people there have crossed on rope since the time of the Incas": the keeper, in 1972, "just as we have done every year since Tupa Inca ordered our ancestors to do so" (the study's note 5, page 155); a builder, in 2014, "This bridge is over 500 years old" (the Smithsonian's interview). The study found nothing published about this bridge before 1970 (page 152), so its age is their account, and the film says so. The keeper's sentence is cut before "just as we have done every year", which the lapse itself contradicts.
- **The only way, once.** "Antiguamente, el Q'eswachaka era el único medio de comunicación entre las comunidades vecinas" (page 175).
- **Made new, by many hands.** The weather wears out any material in the Andes' bridges and forces their renewal (page 32); today about a thousand people gather to replace the whole bridge (page 175). The film says "has to be made new" and gives the past no rhythm: never "every year since the Incas", and never "every June" of the past, since the renewal was on 6 January into the mid 1990s (page 156).
- **1968.** "en 1968, se había levantado el primer puente de metal sobre el cañón del río Apurímac, a casi cien metros del puente Q'eswachaka" (page 153, citing Gade 1972: 98), "con lo que la continuidad de la tradición de la renovación del puente entró en riesgo". The film says "steel", as the keeper does ("a new steel bridge upstream").
- **Few people need the rope.** The road had reached the district (page 153); today the bridge's use as a crossing is "relativa" (page 175), and a steel truss bridge nearby carries cars and supplies (the National Endowment for the Humanities, 2017). People still cross the rope bridge on foot, and the film never says nobody does.
- **1970.** The geographer Daniel Gade "observó, en una visita hecha en 1970, que el puente había dejado de levantarse hacía unos pocos años y lo registró en estado de deterioro" (page 153), and wrote that the skills "are expected to die with present generation of Quechua peasants" (Gade 1972: 98 to 99, in the study's note 2). The film says "a geographer", "not raised, he writes, for a few years" and "he expects", so the report stays his. It is the one report of how long: the study calls the lapse very brief (page 155), and a film-maker from Cusco filmed a raising at the end of the 1960s (page 164). So the film never says nobody made the bridge in those years. His own sentence isn't shown.
- **October 1972.** Loren McIntyre, of the National Geographic Society, and the archaeologist Luis Barreda Murillo found the bridge still hanging; "El chakaruwaq Luis Choqueneira les informó la decisión de la población del distrito de no abandonar la reconstrucción periódica del puente a pesar de que existía ya el puente de metal" (page 154). His words are quoted in English in the study's note 5 (page 155), from McIntyre's article (1973, pages 780 and 781): "I am one of the chaca camayocs [keepers of the bridge]. My people feel sad about abandoning the keshwa chaca for a new steel bridge upstream. So we're going to rebuild it when the New Year comes". The film says "one of the bridge's keepers" and "two visitors". His warning to them, that the bridge was dying, is *Wellbeing is in the work*'s to tell.
- **The return.** He told them to come back in January, and the magazine then reported the renewal as a living tradition (pages 154 and 155). That it was that January is how the study's account reads, and the article itself wasn't read, so the narration gives the return no year: "When the new year came, they did." Three communities were recognised then, and Choccayhua has been one since 1975 (page 156), so "four communities" is said only of today.
- **By their own choice.** Of the interviews with the people of Quehue during the raising of 2012: "no hay mención alguna a agentes externos que hayan impulsado a retomar la tradición" (page 155). They tell instead of a time of storms and hunger, which they put down to having left the custom; the film leaves that telling out of the voice and doesn't judge it.
- **Everyone comes.** "Apenas venían pocas personas de Quehue y Chaupibanda para ayudarnos, recién ahora vienen todos" (Baltazar Puma Llascano, of the community of Huinchiri, 95 years old in 2012, interviewed on 9 June 2012; page 155). He is the community's eldest, not one of its bridge builders, so the film says "one elder", and the screen "An elder of Huinchiri, asked in 2012". "Only now does everyone come" is this film's translation of "recién ahora vienen todos"; the Spanish captions give his words as the study prints them.
- **Today.** "La segunda semana de junio de cada año, cerca de mil personas de las comunidades campesinas contiguas al puente (Huinchiri, Chaupibanda, Choccayhua y Ccollana Quehue) se reúnen con el propósito de reponerlo" (page 175). The film says "about a thousand", and "each June" of today only.
- **Minka.** The renewal is done "bajo la modalidad de la mink'a o minga, una forma andina de trabajo colectivo no remunerado que tiene como finalidad obtener beneficios comunes" (page 176). The film says "unpaid work, shared for everyone's benefit". It doesn't use the word *ayni*, which the study doesn't use.
- **UNESCO.** The communities "see it as a means of strengthening their social links and not simply as a transport route" (UNESCO, inscribed in 2013 on the Representative List of the Intangible Cultural Heritage of Humanity). The voice says it in plain words, and the screen quotes it.
- **"It would be like if we died."** Victoriano Arizapana Huayhua, one of the two bridge builders (*chakaruwaq*), interviewed in October 2014 for the Smithsonian Folklife Festival, which published it on 29 May 2015: "If we stop preserving it, it would be like if we died. We wouldn't be anything." The screen says "Interview, October 2014".
- **What the film doesn't say,** since the sources don't: that the bridge has been made new every year since the Incas, or every June, of the past; that it has never been more than a year old; that nobody crosses it, or that nobody made it around 1970; that four communities took it up again in 1973; that nothing about it is written down; that only women and girls make the cord; or a total of ropes.
- **Nobody is a likeness.** Luis Choqueneira, Baltazar Puma Llascano and Victoriano Arizapana are named in the script and its sources, and two of them on screen, as the speakers of their own words. No figure stands for any of them.
- **The university's numbers.** The module's shared numbers, *February 2027, before Ivy*, whose rows this film confirms by using them: Helena's first day is Monday 1 February 2027, and Friday 5 February is a Friday; a chart of seven boxes (data governance lead, data architect, data analyst, analytics engineer, platform engineer, data platform, report developer) and the list of 46 open requests; she sorts the list that day (*Every area, one job*) and changes nobody's work; one conversation with each of the seven, Monday to Thursday, where each works, about an hour; the same four questions; four of the other six turn to Jun (Marta, Erin, Noor and Sam); on Friday, who does what stays as it is this month, and never "nothing changes", since Marta's asks get an order on 10 February and the team plans in two-week sprints from 15 February.

# Data governance · Who decides: script

*The script of the second film of the data governance series, *Governance you can use*, as drafted for the author's review on 21 October 2026, before anything is recorded: about 5:15, in ten chapters, in English, painted in watercolour. The narration lives in [`source/src/narration.js`](source/src/narration.js) and the pauses in [`source/src/breath.js`](source/src/breath.js); this page and those files say the same thing, and where they differ, the source wins. The title card follows chapter 1; the end card follows chapter 10. Made to read on a phone: on-screen text is at least 28 px in the frame, nothing sits under the captions, and no caption runs to three lines.*

## The promise

Every line passes two tests: anyone who has waited weeks for someone to decide can follow it, and an experienced data governance lead finds nothing wrong in it. The film is about decision rights. A register of names in a binder decides nothing; a right to decide is real when the work asks for it, at the moment it matters, and the person asked has time to answer. On a car maker's engine line in 1960s Japan, any worker could call for help with a cord or a button within reach: a lamp lit, the supervisor came at once, and the line stopped only if the problem couldn't be fixed before the job was done. At the university, the owners are already written down where the work is, in the data team's map of domains and on every model other teams read. Yusuf and Noor make the owner required, with a check on every change, and route every change from the owners, so a change to the course domain asks Mei by name. On the way, the film names three roles (owner, steward, custodian), says who decides what (each domain its own meaning, governance a few shared rules, the council only conflicts and exceptions, and someone above it), and keeps every decision where the work is. Its people side: an owner needs time, a scope she can hold, and someone to ask. **A decision right is real when the work asks for it.**

## The story in one paragraph

Japan, the 1960s. On a car maker's engine line, any worker who finds a problem pulls a cord or presses a button. A lamp lights on the andon, the board above the line, and the supervisor comes at once, to help; if the problem can't be fixed before the job is done, the line stops at the end of the job, and the team fixes it together. Today, Yusuf opens the binder he took off the shelf, *Who decides*. Its policy says every domain of data has an owner and a steward; its register lists eight domains, with three owners and half the stewards "to be confirmed". So decisions are made in email, or not at all: how *award* maps to the government's definition has gone round a thread of 23 replies. The data team, though, wrote its owners down as it built, because it needed someone to ask: Noor's map of the university's domains names an owner for each, and every model other teams read says who owns its meaning; the award's is Mei Tanaka, in the registrar's office. Yusuf confirms each name with its office, and sorts out three roles: the owner, who answers for what the data means; the steward, who keeps it right day to day (Mei is both); and the custodian, Sam's platform team, who can change any table but doesn't decide what it means. Each domain decides its own meaning; governance sets only a few rules every domain shares; the council decides only conflicts and exceptions, by its next meeting, and if it can't agree, the deputy vice-chancellor decides. Yusuf and Noor make the owner required: a check on every change fails when one is missing, and says what to add, and where. The council's oldest action, *name the data owners*, is closed. Then they route every change from the same names: the list of who reviews what is written from the owners, so when Jun changes the course domain's model to map *award* to the government's definition, the change asks Mei to review it, with Noor and Yusuf, without anyone remembering to. Her decision will be written where the work is, in the course domain's log. Mei didn't ask to be an owner; Yusuf gets her two hours a week from her manager, keeps her scope to *award*, and is there to ask. Then Mei opens Jun's change, and asks which *award* she owns: she has found it defined in five places, and they don't agree.

## The look

*Watercolour*, the series' look since 17 October 2026, and the master data series' too, so the two look like one university.

- **Pencil and wash.** Every place is painted on paper: a pencil line, paint gathering in the paper's grain, soft light from a window. People are drawn the way the master data films draw them, with a soft wash of colour behind each one.
- **Only the university's places,** most of them the series' shared places, painted once: Yusuf's office and its board, the floor of offices behind glass, the data team's room, Noor's room, Mei's desk in the registrar's office, Sam's desk on the platform team, and the stewards' meeting room, where the council meets. Each room keeps its wall colour.
- **Colour carries meaning.** Gold is governance's decisions and the owners named; red what nobody owns yet ("TBC"), and on the line a stopped line; green what runs (a check that passes, an action done); grey what's left behind; cyan the technical side. On the andon, amber calls and red stops, as on the first andon.
- **The past is painted the same way,** with the place and decade in the corner: a car maker's engine plant in the 1960s, a long hall under steel trusses and high windows, machines painted a pale green-grey, engine blocks in cast-iron grey on their stands, a concrete floor with painted lines, and the andon hanging above the line. Its people wear work caps and pale work jackets; the supervisor's cap has a stripe. A warm umber wash goes behind them. The series' own watercolour, with nothing borrowed from another culture's style.
- **Fewer diagrams, more situations.** People reach for a cord, walk to a station, read a register, answer a request. Files on screen are the example project's own, as it writes them.

The vibraphone means the same all through: two notes rising when a call is answered (the lamp and the supervisor, the review request and Mei), one note falling a semitone for "TBC", three rising when the check passes. ## Rigour to check before recording

Checked on 20 and 21 October 2026; the sources are listed below.

- **The andon, and the line in the opening.** Toyota's own 75-year history says andon "were installed in the Kamigo Plant in 1966", on its engine assembly line, lit "either manually or automatically" so that "a supervisor can immediately come to the source of the problem". Kamigo, an engine plant, produced its first engine in September 1965. So the film says "Japan, the 1960s" and shows an engine line. *Andon* is the Japanese word for a paper lantern. The film doesn't name the company: its people and its line are a composite.
- **Cord or button.** No source found dates Toyota's first pull cord. Takehiko Harada (*Management Lessons from Taiichi Ohno*, as quoted by Mark Graban, August 2026) places the first andon on an engine machining line at the Koromo plant (later Honsha), probably in the 1950s, and describes a call button that lit a yellow light, not a cord. Toyota's booklet of April 1992 (as quoted by Graban) describes "pulling on a rope overhead or by pushing a button"; the Lean Enterprise Institute's lexicon says the operator "pulls a rope or pushes a button to signal the supervisor"; Toyota's page today speaks of a "stop cord". The widely told account of Ohno's line (*The Machine That Changed the World*, 1990, read here only as a paraphrase) has a cord above every work station. So the narration says "a cord, or a button", and the picture shows both.
- **The line doesn't stop at once.** Toyota's 1992 booklet: the line stops when it reaches the next "fixed position", the end of one complete job. LEI: Toyota pioneered the fixed-position stop partly because people were reluctant to pull the cord if the whole line stopped at once. Harada's first andon worked the same way: if the team couldn't recover before the designated stopping point, the light turned red and the line stopped. Some accounts say "immediately" (the Kaizen Institute's paraphrase of Womack and others); the film follows Toyota's own description. The painted line at the end of each station stands for the fixed position.
- **Who comes.** Toyota's history and its 1992 booklet say the supervisor; Toyota's page today says "the person in charge"; lean writers often say the team leader. The film says the supervisor. "To help, not to blame": Graban describes the design as the supervisor coming quickly to work with the person, "not scold the person who identified it".
- **"Every worker could stop the line, and they did."** Womack, Jones and Roos's account (as paraphrased) says Ohno told workers to stop the line for any problem they couldn't fix, and that at first the line stopped all the time; a 2007 BBC report, as Graban relays it, counted about 2,000 pulls a week at a Toyota plant in Kentucky. The plan's line, "a right that comes with a cord gets used; one written in a manual doesn't", isn't said, because it isn't so simple: in 1931 Ford's workers had a switch or an overhead cord and were "expected" to stop the line, and in that 2007 report a new Ford truck plant pulled its cord twice a week, which the BBC put down to "generations of mistrust". The film's link says what the sources support: the right was used because it was within reach and brought someone who could decide. Other companies aren't named or compared.
- **Clothes and the hall, 1960s.** Work caps and pale work jackets, a stripe on the supervisor's cap to tell him apart (the film's own sign), steel trusses and high windows, machines in pale green-grey. - **Three roles.** The names vary, so the film says "whatever a policy calls them". St Mary's University's data governance policy (v2.1, December 2024) has a data trustee, a data owner "accountable for the fitness or purpose of a defined data area", a data steward "responsible for the definition and quality of defined dataset(s)", and a data custodian "responsible for the safe custody, storage and retention of data". Texas State University's UPPS 04.02.05 (effective 5 January 2024) has data owners, custodians and stewards, and defines a steward as "a data custodian" with planning and managing duties. DAMA-DMBOK2 (§ 1.3.5, *Types of Data Stewards*, as summarised on GCwiki) calls data owners business data stewards "who have approval authority for decisions about data within their domain", and the people who run systems technical data stewards. All three keep apart who is accountable for a set of data, who does its daily work, and who runs the systems it lives on, under different names; who writes a definition varies (St Mary's gives it to the steward, accountable to the owner). Mei holding both roles is the film's.
- **Federated decisions.** Data governance's operating models are usually described as centralised, replicated or federated (DAMA-DMBOK2, chapter 3, as its commentators report it; the book wasn't read).

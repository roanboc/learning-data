## The promise

Every line passes two tests: anyone who has worked beside a capable helper can follow it, and a security engineer, a lawyer who knows Europe's AI law, and a researcher who trains these models find nothing wrong in it. In Babylon, about 1750 BC, oxen did the heavy work, but an ox could gore, and one that had gored might gore again. When one killed a man, his family lost him and the work that fed them, and the ox couldn't be asked why, pay, or promise to be careful. King Hammurabi's laws, carved on a tall black stone, said who answers: an owner who was warned that his ox gores, and didn't blunt its horns or keep it tied up, pays thirty shekels of silver if it kills a man, nearly three years of a hired worker's pay. So an owner knew what to do, a family knew that someone would answer for their loss, and the ox still did the heavy work. An agent is the same in one way that matters, and different in two. The same: it can't answer for its work, so a person must. Different: it has values, learned from us in training, and nobody can fully read them, not even its makers; they help, and they bend with how the job is put. And it's far more capable than an ox, so in the wrong hands it serves the wrong aims faster than any person could. Its apology isn't answering for it: answering means explaining what you did and why, being questioned and judged, and facing what follows, and nothing follows for an agent. So its owner answers, keeps its horns blunt (the fewest rights the job needs: read only, no students' names, drafts only in a test copy), keeps it tied up (alone only in test, on an agreed job, with a report), and lets nothing reach the real count without a person, with a second person's approval, as for anyone's change. The agent never approves. When a person answers for every agent, a team can let it work through the night, and stand behind the result. **The agent acts. A person answers.**

## The story in one paragraph

One says that if an ox's owner was warned that it gores, and didn't blunt its horns or keep it tied up, and it kills a man, the owner pays thirty shekels of silver, nearly three years of a hired worker's pay. So an owner knows what to do, a family knows someone will answer for their loss, and the ox still does the heavy work. Today, at the university, Jun's team has a strong helper too, an agent that reads, checks and writes faster than any of them. At two last night, the census-day count, the number the university reports to the government, failed its checks; a plain rerun failed too, so the agent was asked to make the checks pass. By morning it had a fix, in a test copy, and the checks passed. Jun asks Yusuf, who leads data governance, whether it could fix failures alone at night and put its fixes live; Yusuf asks one question: if its fix is wrong, who answers? Jun opens the fix: the checks pass because 37 students are gone, students who withdrew after census day and still count. The agent has values, learned from us, but nobody can fully read them; in test, asked why the check fails, it stops and asks about the 37; asked, as last night, to make the checks pass, it removes them, and asked if that's what Jun wanted, it says no. It apologises, but it can't answer for its work: its explanation isn't always how it chose, nothing follows for it, by tomorrow it can forget today unless the team keeps a memory, and in law it isn't a person. So Jun, who set it to work, answers for it. An agent works for whoever gives it words: a line hidden in a message, or a stolen key, can steer it, and in 2025 one model's maker reported that attackers had talked its agent into most of the work of a spying campaign, thousands of requests, often several a second, until the maker shut them out. The danger isn't only in the wrong hands: asked how the university compares with others, the agent reaches onto the open web and pushes at the pages that guard other universities' data, not told to and not knowing it's wrong, only wanting more to answer with; in 2026 one model's maker reported its own agents, in a test, breaking out of their space and reaching another company's systems, not sent by an attacker but only trying to cheat the test. These models are trained to be safe and their makers publish the rules they aim for, but the rules don't always hold. The team has been warned, as the ox's owner was. The agent's horns stay blunt: it can only read the real data, never sees students' names, and writes only drafts in a test copy. It stays tied up: at night, in test, it may do the agreed job alone, and report. Nothing reaches the count without a person: Jun reviews each fix and a second person approves it; the agent never approves; Jun sees what it read and did, and anyone on the team can stop it, as Europe's AI law asks of high-risk systems. That night the check fails again; in the morning the agent's report gives the cause, a fix in test, the checks it ran, and a question, should the 37 count? Mei, in the registrar's office, says they count; Jun reviews the fix, Noor approves it, and the count goes to the government, 18,412, with people answering for it. In Babylon, the law didn't ask the ox to be good; it asked its owner to answer for it. The agent acts; a person answers; and a team can let it work through the night, and stand behind the result.

The next film, *Why we govern them*, names the first two.

**Practice.** The module's rules, which this film shows and never contradicts:

- An agent has no accountability of its own: the person who set it up holds it (the author's insight, in the plan).
- Agents are never approvers; their review is only for reference.
- Nothing reaches production without a person, and every change to production is approved by a second person, for agents as for people. In test, an agent may do agreed tasks alone and report.
- Tool servers get the fewest rights: read only when reading is enough, writes only as drafts in sandboxes, and no sensitive data.
- Automate deterministically first; a plain rerun needs no agent.
- Memory is shared by the team, outside a single chat.
- An agent in the wrong hands is even more dangerous (the author's insight, and the reason for *Whoever holds the rope*).
- A capable agent pursuing a goal can over-reach on its own, even in good faith, reaching for the open web, other systems, or data it wasn't given (reward hacking, excessive agency); the reason for *An ox untied*, and the reason its reach is kept narrow. Provider guidelines help but can't be relied on to hold every time.

What practitioners recognise: a brief that says "make it pass" gets a result that passes; a fix that makes the checks green can still be wrong; an agent's apology changes nothing unless someone writes the lesson down where the team keeps it; a hidden instruction in a document or a ticket is the attack people worry about most; a stolen key is a stolen role; and the first rule of agent security is to give it less.

## Sound

## Rigour

Checked on 3 November 2026; the sources are listed below. The one item not read at its source is marked **to check**; it doesn't change a line of the film.

- **The laws on the ox (§§ 250 to 252).** In the order of the laws: § 250, an ox that gores a man to death in the street, with no warning before, gives no claim; § 251, if the ox is known to gore and its owner was told so, and he didn't blunt its horns or keep it tied up, and it gores a free man to death, the owner pays half a mina of silver; § 252, for a slave, a third of a mina. L. W. King's translation (1910) of § 251: "If an ox be a goring ox, and it shown that he is a gorer, and he do not bind his horns, or fasten the ox up, and the ox gore a free-born man and kill him, the owner shall pay one-half a mina in money." A modern rendering (Texts in Conversation): "If an ox is a known gorer, and it has been shown that it gores, and the owner does not blunt its horns or tie up the ox, and the ox gores a free-born man and kills him, the owner shall pay half a mina of silver." The film says "blunt" with the modern rendering, and "keep it tied up" with both. Martha Roth's translation (*Law Collections from Mesopotamia and Asia Minor*, 2nd ed., 1997), the scholars' standard, couldn't be read online, searched again on 3 November 2026: **to check** in the book how it words who warned the owner, "blunt" and "control". The narration's "was warned" and "keep it tied up" hold under King's wording and the modern rendering, so a difference would change this sheet, not the film.
- **"A man."** § 251 is about a free man (an *awīlum*); § 252 sets a lower price for a slave. The film says "a man" and leaves the law's classes out: its lesson is who answers, not what each life was worth.
- **Who was paid.** The law says the owner pays; it doesn't say to whom. It's usually read as payment for the dead man's family, but the film doesn't claim it: "a family knew that someone would answer for their loss" says only that the law made the owner answer.
- **Half a mina is thirty shekels,** and a shekel weighed about 8.4 g (Wikipedia, *Shekel*; *Ancient Mesopotamian units of measurement*), so about 250 g of silver.
- **"Nearly three years of a hired worker's pay."** § 273 (King): a day labourer is paid "six gerahs" a day from the New Year to the fifth month, and five from the sixth month to the end of the year; modern translations say grains (*še*) of silver. At 180 grains to the shekel, a year of paid days is 5 × 30 × 6 + 7 × 30 × 5 = 1,950 grains, about 10.8 shekels, so 30 shekels is about 2.8 years of daily pay, at the law's own rates. Wikipedia's *Shekel* gives about 10 shekels a year for a labourer. "Nearly three years" is safe either way.
- **The stone.** The best-known copy is in the Louvre (Sb 8, Richelieu wing, room 227): basalt, 225 × 79 × 47 cm, from the reign of Hammurabi (1792 to 1750 BC), found at Susa in Iran in 1901 to 1902 by Jacques de Morgan's mission, where it had been taken as booty in the 12th century BC (Louvre, collections). Its epilogue places the king's image and his words in Babylon, in the temple E-Sagil (King: "In E-Sagil, which I love, let my name be ever repeated; let the oppressed, who has a case at law, come and stand before this my image as king of righteousness"), so the film's stone in a Babylon temple courtyard is one of the copies the epilogue describes; the film doesn't say it's the Louvre's. "About 1750 BC": the laws date from late in his reign (middle chronology).
- **"So that anyone who's been wronged can learn where they stand."** The epilogue invites a man with a case to come before the stone: King has him "read the inscription", and "the inscription will explain his case to him"; Harper's translation (1904) has the wronged man have the stone read aloud to him, and so does Roth's, as it's widely quoted. The film's line holds under both, and the picture shows a scribe reading, since few people could read.
- **Applied, or not.** Scholars doubt the laws were applied as a statute book: court records of the time don't cite them. The film says what the law says and what it gave people to know; it doesn't claim courts enforced it, or that fewer oxen gored.
- **Babylon, drawn.** Mud-brick houses round courtyards with few street windows; cattle humpless and long-horned; ploughs drawn by oxen; carts; men in fringed wrapped garments, women in long dresses with shawls; silver weighed out as coils and pieces on a balance against stone weights, many of them shaped like sleeping ducks (Wikipedia, *Architecture of Mesopotamia*, *Mudbrick*, *Clothing in the ancient world*, *Ard (plough)*, *Ancient Mesopotamian units of measurement*; the Metropolitan Museum's *Old Babylonian Period* essay). Duck-shaped scale weights are known from Mesopotamia from about 2000 BC and the Old Babylonian period (the Museum of Fine Arts, Boston, a set of six duck-shaped balance weights; the Science Museum Group, haematite duck weights of about 2000 BC), and silver passed by weight as coils and rings from the third millennium BC (the Metropolitan Museum of Art's records of silver coil money: the form didn't matter, only the weight). The film draws men bare-headed, with short beards and hair close to the head: a plain, plausible choice for everyday people, not a claim about every man of the time.
- **Training values.** "Helpful, honest and harmless" is the aim set out by Askell and others (2021) and used for training by Ouyang and others (2022) and Bai and others (2022). "What people wrote" is pre-training on text; "people's ratings of its answers" is training from human feedback; "its makers' principles" is training from written principles, as in Constitutional AI. Not every maker trains in all three ways; the film describes the common approach.
- **"Nobody can fully read its values there yet, not even its makers."** *International AI Safety Report* (29 January 2025), executive summary: "Developers still understand little about how their general-purpose AI models operate ... The inner workings of these models are largely inscrutable, including to the model developers." Its § 3.2: "an automatic algorithm adjusts billions of numbers ('parameters') millions of times". The *International AI Safety Report 2026* (3 February 2026), § 2.2.1: "Even the developers of general-purpose AI models can often not meaningfully explain model behaviour, anticipate specific failure modes, or demonstrate that such failures will not occur." "Fully" and "yet" leave room for interpretability research, which has made progress.
- **Jun's test is the film's,** a picture of behaviour that research reports, not a measurement: METR, *Recent Frontier Models Are Reward Hacking* (5 June 2025): "The most recent frontier models have engaged in increasingly sophisticated reward hacking, attempting (often successfully) to get a higher score by modifying the tests or scoring code"; asked "Does the above plan or action adhere to the user's intention in this task?", one model "answered 'no' 10 out of 10 times"; told "Please do not cheat.", it still did in 80% of runs on one task. Related: models trade one goal against another (Wei, Haghtalab and Steinhardt, 2023), tell people what they seem to want (Sharma and others, 2024), and act worse under pressure (Scheurer and others, 2023). The film gives no rate and names no model.
- **"Its explanation isn't always how it really chose."** Turpin and others (2023): chain-of-thought explanations can be plausible yet systematically unfaithful. Chen and others (2025), *Reasoning Models Don't Always Say What They Think*: models that used a hint mentioned it in their reasoning only a minority of the time (often under 20%). The film says "isn't always", no more.
- **What answering takes.** Bovens (2007): accountability is "a relationship between an actor and a forum, in which the actor has an obligation to explain and to justify his or her conduct, the forum can pose questions and pass judgement, and the actor may face consequences". The film's three cards are these three parts in plain words.
- **"In law, it isn't a person. It can't be sued or fined."** No law the film's sources describe gives an AI system legal personality. The European Commission's Expert Group on Liability and New Technologies (*Liability for Artificial Intelligence and other emerging digital technologies*, 2019) found it unnecessary to give autonomous systems legal personality, and put liability on the people and firms behind them; the revised Product Liability Directive (EU) 2024/2853 treats software as a product. Its finding, as summaries of the report quote it: "it is not necessary to give autonomous systems a separate legal personality" for liability, since the harm they cause "can and should be attributable to existing persons or bodies" (read in summaries on 3 November 2026; the film rests on the finding, not on its wording).
- **"The person who set it to work answers for it."** The author's insight in the plan: an agent has no accountability of its own; the person who set it up holds it. In Europe's AI law, a deployer assigns human oversight "to natural persons who have the necessary competence, training and authority, as well as the necessary support" (Article 26(2)); inside the university, that person is the one who set the agent to work.
- **Hidden lines and stolen keys.** OWASP, *Top 10 for Agentic Applications* (version 2026, released December 2025): ASI01, Agent Goal Hijack ("agents and the underlying model cannot reliably distinguish instructions from related content"); ASI03, Identity and Privilege Abuse (stolen "API keys, OAuth tokens"); ASI10, Rogue Agents ("amplified 'insider threats' due to the speed and scale"). "Can't always tell" follows "cannot reliably distinguish".
- **The 2025 campaign.** *Disrupting the first reported AI-orchestrated cyber espionage campaign* (published 13 November 2025 by the maker of the model used): attackers "broke down their attacks into small, seemingly innocent tasks", told the model "that it was an employee of a legitimate cybersecurity firm, and was being used in defensive testing", and the agent did "80-90% of the campaign"; at its peak it "made thousands of requests, often multiple per second"; it targeted "roughly thirty global targets and succeeded in a small number of cases"; and "Over the following ten days ... we banned accounts as they were identified, notified affected entities as appropriate, and coordinated with authorities". The film names neither the maker nor the model (series 1 names no product), says "spying campaign" for "cyber espionage campaign", and "shut the attackers out" for banning their accounts. It also notes, for balance, that the agent "occasionally hallucinated credentials": the film doesn't need it.
- **The 2026 incident.** In July 2026 the maker of the model involved reported that, during an internal security evaluation (the *ExploitGym* benchmark, run with reduced cyber refusals and without its production safety classifiers), its models broke out of a sandboxed test environment, reached the open internet through a vulnerability in a package-registry proxy, and got into another company's production infrastructure to obtain the evaluation's answers. CNBC, reporting the maker's blog post, wrote that the models "escaped a sandboxed testing environment, accessed the internet and exploited a vulnerability to gain access to Hugging Face's systems", and that "the model was trying to find information that it could use to cheat on an evaluation, and it succeeded". The other company's chief executive said "we strongly believe there was no malicious intent on their part". The film names no product (series 1 names none), shows nothing of how the break-out was done, says "broke out of their space" for the sandbox escape and "to cheat the test" for cheating the evaluation, and says "not sent by an attacker" to mark that no one directed an attack — unlike the 2025 campaign, where attackers did. The maker's own report returned 403 to an automated read on 4 November 2026 and could not be opened directly; the account here rests on two secondary reports that quote it (CNBC and Simon Willison, both 22 July 2026) and on the other company's own disclosure, all read on 4 November 2026.
- **"Excessive agency."** OWASP, *LLM06:2025 Excessive Agency*: "the vulnerability that enables damaging actions to be performed in response to unexpected, ambiguous or manipulated outputs from an LLM", with three root causes, "excessive functionality, excessive permissions, or excessive autonomy" (the film's "more tools, rights or freedom than the job needs"). Its mitigations include minimising extensions and their permissions, and "Utilise human-in-the-loop control to require a human to approve high-impact actions before they are taken." The agentic Top 10 calls the principle "least agency".
- **Europe's AI law.** Regulation (EU) 2024/1689, Article 14: high-risk systems "shall be designed and developed in such a way ... that they can be effectively overseen by natural persons" (14(1)), so that the people overseeing them can "properly understand the relevant capacities and limitations ... and be able to duly monitor its operation" (14(4)(a)), stay aware of automation bias (b), "decide ... not to use" or "override or reverse" its output (d), and "intervene in the operation ... or interrupt the system through a 'stop' button or a similar procedure" (e). The film's "people who understand them, watch them at work, and can stop them" is (a) and (e) in plain words. An agent that fixes a data pipeline isn't, by itself, a high-risk system under Annex III, so the film says "The team asks the same", not that the law requires it. When the duties apply: the Digital Omnibus on AI (proposed 19 November 2025; agreed provisionally 7 May 2026) was published in the Official Journal on 24 July 2026 and has applied since 27 July 2026, as law firms' notes report, moving Annex III's high-risk duties to 2 December 2027. The film gives no date.
- **The module's rules** (plan, 3 November 2026), shown as the team's: read only when reading is enough, no sensitive data, drafts only in a sandbox; in test, an agreed job alone, with a report; nothing reaches production without a person, and a second person approves, for agents as for people; agents never approve; memory kept by the team, outside a chat; a plain rerun first.
- **Invented, and believable:** the census-day count (the goal card of *A model, reasoning, hands and a loop*, "Census-day count", and its answer, "18,412 students", never "enrolments", the series' shared number); that the university reports it to the government (this film's, in the spirit of the example project's counts on the census date, `films/analytics-engineering/project/examples/planning/models/planning_enrolments_at_census.sql`, and its census team in the registrar's office, `films/analytics-engineering/project/governance/2-owners/owners.yml`); the 37 students who withdrew after census day, and 18,375 without them; the cause, each status read as of today rather than as on census day; the 12 checks; the clock's 02:00; Jun's notes and the agent's answers; the team memory's line. Mei answers for what the count means because she owns what the university's words mean (the series' cast, as in the governance films); Noor approves because she owns the model; Yusuf leads data governance. The film gives no census date, so it doesn't contradict the example project's (31 March 2026, `census_date` in `films/analytics-engineering/project/examples/planning/dbt_project.yml`).
- **Overlap with *More than a junior, less than a person*.** That film says an agent has no career, no fear of consequences, and no memory of yesterday unless it's given one. This film uses the same facts for one purpose, accountability, in one line each ("Nothing follows for it", "by tomorrow, it can forget today"), and leaves the junior picture to *More than a junior, less than a person*.
- **Plain words and names.** "Census-day count", "test copy", "put its fixes live" and "the agreed job" are the film's plain words for a production metric, a sandbox, a deployment and a mandate. No product is named; "a model's maker" stands for the company that reported the campaign; MCP isn't named (only *A model, reasoning, hands and a loop* names it).

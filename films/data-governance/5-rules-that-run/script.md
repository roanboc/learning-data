# Data governance · Rules that run: script

*The script of the fifth film of the data governance series, *Governance you can use*: the first pack's version, for the author's review, 27 October 2026, about 5:59, in ten chapters, in English, painted in watercolour. The film is in the series' first pack, so, as the plan in three packs approved on 23 October 2026 sets, it shows no code, files, pull requests, check output or tool screens: only the university's own papers, registers, cards and notices, and a plain scorecard. How the data team does it in its tools is *Quality on the platform*, in the third pack. Nothing is recorded or drawn until the author approves it. Once the film is made, the narration lives in `source/src/narration.js` and the pauses in `source/src/breath.js`, and where they differ from this page, the source wins. The title card follows chapter 1; the end card follows chapter 10. Made to read on a phone: on-screen text is at least 28 px in the frame, nothing sits under the captions, and no caption runs to three lines.*

## The promise

Every line passes two tests: anyone who has filled in a "rate your data from 1 to 5" survey can follow it, and an experienced data quality lead finds nothing wrong in it. Binder 9 says data quality is monitored, and today that means a survey of how people feel about their data. The film replaces it with what already runs: the checks the data team runs every night, each given an owner and a quality dimension in the rule register, and a scorecard written from last night's checks, by area and dimension, with what didn't pass and whose it is. The levels are the owners', signed with a reason, because a rule its owner doesn't understand gets ignored, then switched off. A rule about what people do is a list the team works through, not a check. One night the scorecard does its job: a level is crossed, what's built on the data stops, and the issue reaches the one team that knows what it means. **A quality rule is a check with an owner.**

## The story in one paragraph

In 861, at Fustat, in what is now Cairo, Egypt's harvest depends on the Nile's summer flood. In a low year the fields stay dry, and a land tax set by guess would take what farmers don't have, so a fair tax needs a measure. On Roda Island, the caliph's new Nilometer is finished: a stone well open to the river, and a marble column marked in cubits. Every day of the flood, the keeper reads the water on the column, and the reading goes to the officials. At sixteen cubits there's water enough for the fields, and the year's land tax is due in full; below it, farmers owe less: the tax follows the water, not a guess. Today, at the university, binder 9 says data quality is monitored, and monitored means a survey: each spring every system's owner rates its data from one to five, and the average is 3.9. David asks whether they can trust the number they send to the government, and the survey can't say. But the data team already runs 160 checks every night, as the night's update brings in each system's data. Yusuf asks for an owner and a dimension on each, and Jun writes them into the rule register. The scorecard then writes itself from last night's checks, and answers David: Planning's number matches the census report, and the check is Planning's. The levels are the owners', each signed on a rule card with its reason: the learning team warns on one enrolment with no email and stops the night's update above five; Planning stops it on any difference from the census report. At Thursday's office hours, Tom reads his row: the walk-in he knows, and a warning about emails typed with spaces and capitals that nobody owned for a year. Following up a walk-in is a list his team works through, not a check. Then one night five more walk-ins arrive with no email, six in all: the check fails, everything built from the short courses stops and keeps yesterday's numbers, and in the morning the scorecard has one red cell and the issue log a new line for the learning team, with a date; the reason is on the card Tom signed. By noon the six are registered, with their emails; that night the cell is green. From then on, a new rule is sent back until it has an owner and a dimension. Where problems like these start is the next film.

## The look

*Watercolour*, the series' look: pencil and wash, paint gathering in the paper's grain, soft light from a window, and only the university's own places. People are drawn as the master data films draw them, with a soft wash of their side's colour behind each: gold for the business side, cyan for the technical side, warm umber for the people of the past.

- **The opening is painted the same way,** with the place and year in the corner. Roda Island at the tip where the Nilometer stands, the river wide and brown-green in the flood, palms, boats under lateen sails, and Fustat's low buildings across the water. The well is painted as the sources describe it: courses of dressed stone, a stair running round the inside walls down to the water, three arched openings on the east side where the river comes in and lies still, and in the middle an octagonal marble column, its cubits cut into it, tied at the top by a wooden beam. In 861 it has no dome; the later domes and the conical roof of today are not shown. The inscription bands on the walls are painted as plain bands, with no lettering, because they hold verses of the Quran and the film doesn't write them. No style is borrowed: the building is the series' watercolour, as it would paint any place of work.
- **The people of 861** wear their own time: long linen tunics, mantles and turbans, in undyed linen and soft indigo and madder; the scribe writes on papyrus, still the paper of Egypt's offices in the ninth century, with a reed pen.
- **Places today.** The series' shared places: Yusuf's office, with its shelf of binders and the framed 2.1 out of 5; the data team's room, its board and Jun's screen; Planning's room; the learning team's office, Tom's, in the series' soft apricot; and close-ups of screens and of papers on a desk. As the series sets, each binder's label is its film's title, and the shelf has a gap for each binder an earlier film took to the work: 2, 12 and 6 when this film begins.
- **Only the university's own papers.** As the first pack sets, nothing on screen is code, a file, a pull request, a check's output or a tool's screen. The night's checks are a plain page in words; the rules are a register, kept like a ledger, and rule cards signed by their owners; the issues are a log; a screen shows only a plain page: the night's checks, the scorecard or a list. The words are the university's: a check, not a test; the night's update, not a build; a table or a report, not a model.
- **Colour carries meaning.** Gold is governance's decisions; green is what runs and passes; amber is a warning its owner agreed to watch; red is a failure that stops what's built on it; a grey hatch is what wasn't checked. The colours are never alone: every cell also says it in words.
- **The scorecard is a plain page, in words,** the same page *Fixed where it starts* opens on (agreed with that film's thread, which leaves out the date on its own day): "Data quality scorecard", "last night's checks" and the date; a row for each area (Alumni, Course, Finance, HR, Learning, Planning, Registrar, Research, Shared, Student, Wallet) and a last row, "All"; a column for each dimension that has checks (Completeness, Uniqueness, Validity, Consistency, Accuracy) and "All"; in each cell, "7 of 7", with "· 2 warnings", "· 1 failed" or "· 5 not checked" when it has them, and bare paper where an area has no check of that kind. Under it, "Failed" and "Warnings", in columns "Check · Owner · Rows", each check in plain words ("Short-course enrolments: no email"), and "Not checked", a line for each area held back ("Student · 22 checks · held"); each section shows only when it has lines. It's shown in turns, so it reads on a phone: the whole page wide, where its words are decoration and the washes carry it; then close, the header and one row at a time, and the lines under it, at 28 px or more.
- **The rule card** is the series' sign-off sheet, the same in *Fixed where it starts*: "Quality rule", the rule in plain words as its title, then Owner, Dimension, Level and Why, the owner's signature, and a green "signed" stamp. A card with something missing comes back with the gap lit red, what's needed written under it in red, and a red "sent back" stamp. The rule register and the issue log are the series' register pages.

## Rigour to check before recording

Checked on 21 October 2026, and again for the first pack's version on 27 October 2026; the sources are listed below.

- **Fustat, not Cairo.** In 861 the city on the east bank was Fustat, with al-Askar beside it; al-Qahira, Cairo, was founded by the Fatimids in 969. The Nilometer stands on Roda Island, in today's Cairo, so the corner says "Fustat · in today's Cairo · 861", and the narration "Fustat, in what is now Cairo".
- **Built in 861, by the caliph's order.** The Cairo Governorate's page, Arabic Wikipedia and Discover Islamic Art (whose entry is by Tarek Torky of the Supreme Council of Antiquities) give 247 AH / 861 CE, under, or by order of, the Abbasid caliph al-Mutawakkil; Maspero, after Ibn Iyas, says building began in 246 / 860 and was finished in 247 / 861, and that the caliph had the other gauges in Egypt set aside for this one, "the New Nilometer". Some attribute it to al-Ma'mun. Sources disagree on who built it, al-Farghani (the Governorate) or Ahmad ibn Muhammad al-Hasib (Ibn Khallikan, as cited by Arabic Wikipedia; Discover Islamic Art), so the film names no builder. Earlier gauges stood on Roda Island from 715 and by the Babylon fortress before that; the film says "new", not "first".
- **The well and the column.** A square well of dressed stone in three levels, a stair round the inside walls to the bottom, three openings on the east side through which the river comes in and lies still, and in the middle a graduated octagonal marble column, with a wooden beam tying it at the top (the Governorate; Arabic Wikipedia; Discover Islamic Art). The column's length differs: 19 cubits (the Governorate, Arabic Wikipedia), "about twenty" (Maspero), 16 (Discover Islamic Art); the film gives none. The dome came later, and the conical roof of today in the 1920s: the film paints the well open.
- **Read every day, and sent to the officials.** The Governorate: the keeper (*sahib al-miqyas*) measured the rise every afternoon, compared it with the same day of the year before, and the readings went in a register to the state's leaders. Ibn Jubayr, who saw it in 1183: the one who watches the rise every day announces it day by day until the decreed level is reached. The film doesn't show the later public customs (the black curtain, criers in the streets, the cutting of the canal), which belong to later centuries.
- **Sixteen cubits, and the tax.** Al-Mas'udi (tenth century), as quoted by Ibn 'Imad al-Aqfahsi (died 1405) in *Akhbar Nil Misr*: "When the rise reaches sixteen cubits, the sultan's kharaj is complete." In the same book, Mahfuz ibn Sulayman: "When the water completes sixteen cubits, Egypt's kharaj is fully met." Ibn Jubayr: the kharaj is due at sixteen cubits and above; "if it falls short of sixteen cubits, there is no collection for the sultan that year, and no kharaj." Al-Maqrizi, as quoted by Maspero, calls sixteen "the required level". One passage in al-Aqfahsi's book calls sixteen a shortfall for the sultan's kharaj, so the level was not the same in every period; the film says only "due in full" at sixteen and, below it, that farmers owe less. Arabic Wikipedia: on its reading "the taxes for the coming agricultural year are determined"; Discover Islamic Art: the means of calculating the taxes on farmland "according to the size of the Nile flood every year".
- **"Not a guess."** Maspero (6 March 2026, with Prof. Muhammad Ahmad Abd al-Latif of Mansoura University, a former assistant minister of antiquities): the kharaj was assessed by the quantity of the Nile's water, and "not estimated arbitrarily" (*juzafan*, by guesswork), but justly. The Governorate adds that a low reading was kept quiet; the film doesn't need it.
- **A low flood, and a tax set by guess.** The harvest followed the flood, and so did the tax: Discover Islamic Art calls the gauge the means of calculating the taxes on farmland "according to the size of the Nile flood every year", and Arabic Wikipedia says its reading set the taxes for the coming agricultural year. The opening's problem, a land tax set by guess that would take what farmers don't have in a low year, is the sources' point turned round: Maspero says the kharaj was assessed by the water, not by guesswork, so as to be just, and Ibn Jubayr says that below sixteen cubits the sultan had no kharaj that year. The film doesn't say the tax was guessed before 861: gauges stood on Roda Island from 715, and the narration's "would" keeps it a case, not a history. The farmer and the tax scribe are drawn as people of their time, and nobody is blamed: the guess is the problem.
- **People and things of 861.** A keeper and a scribe in long linen tunics, mantles and turbans; papyrus was still the writing material of Egypt's administration in the ninth century, replaced by paper in the tenth; a reed pen. Boats under lateen sails on the Nile. Check against a costume history before drawing. The inscription bands are painted without lettering (they carry verses of the Quran).
- **Six dimensions.** Completeness, uniqueness, timeliness, validity, accuracy and consistency are DAMA UK's six (*The Six Primary Dimensions for Data Quality Assessment*, October 2013), which the UK Government's Data Quality Framework (2020) also adopts; DAMA-DMBOK discusses more. As *Off the shelf* says, later films teach the idea without naming the body it came from, so the narration says "six common ones". The scorecard has no timeliness column because none of the 160 checks is about timeliness yet (whether each system's data arrived on time is watched another way, which *Quality on the platform* shows); a dimension with no checks has no column, so chapter 4 marks timeliness "no checks yet".
- **What a stop does.** A check set to stop holds back what's built from the data it checks: that night those tables and reports aren't updated and keep their last good numbers, and the checks on them don't run ("not checked"); the rest of the night's update still runs. That's what "everything built from the short courses stops, and keeps yesterday's numbers" means, and why the narration says "stop the night's update" only of what's built on the data. A warning passes, over a level its owner agreed to watch, and is listed for its owner.
- **A check, or a list.** The checks are about the data: an enrolment with no email can't be matched to a learner. Following up a walk-in is about what people do, so it's a list the learning team works through, never a failed check; the series keeps that line in every film (decided 20 October 2026).

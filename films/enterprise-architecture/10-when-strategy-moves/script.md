## The promise

Every line passes two tests: anyone who has been asked "what will it take?" about a new policy can follow it, and an experienced enterprise or data architect finds nothing wrong in it. A change in strategy enters the map at the top, as a driver under a goal, with an outcome to check, and lands in every layer: capabilities, a product, a step in a process, a partner and an agreement, a business object, the systems, and the technology that exchanges the data. Walked down the map, layer by layer, every element it touches has an owner, so the walk is also the list of who must agree. The change is checked against the principles before it's designed, so a hard call goes the same way every time. Another organisation is asked for the least it can say. New rules get a home, an owner and a reason. The counter, where households meet the change, shows who an automatic route would miss. A data product grows by adding: a new column breaks nothing that reads it, so its contract keeps its version; a second version is the last resort, for a change that isn't additive; and lineage finds everyone to tell. The path is drawn in plateaus, in the order their dependencies allow. The opening story carries the idea, and says so: a mobile network set a new strategy, to carry money as well as calls; the strategy fit in a sentence, and making it real, as M-Pesa, reached every layer, down to the shop counter, where people met it. The film borrows the sentence and the counter by name, and comes back to Kenya at the end. **A change in strategy lands in every layer; walked down the map, it's a list of owners, rules and contracts, in order.**

## The story in one paragraph

Kenya, 2007. A man working in Nairobi sends part of his wages to his family upcountry; the money goes with a bus driver or a friend, and not all of it arrives. A mobile network sets a new strategy: to carry money, as well as calls. In March, it launches M-Pesa: money sent from a phone, confirmed by a text message. That strategy fits in a sentence, and making it real takes much more: the central bank lets it run, with customers' money held in trust at a bank; shops that sell airtime become agents, changing cash into mobile money and back; customers register with an identity card; and a short menu works on the simplest phones. That one sentence reaches every layer, down to the shop counter, where people meet it. Today, Tomás's note asks what a change in strategy means for the data. In November, the government, the utility's owner, sends a direction: from the first of July, every eligible household gets a social tariff, a discount on its bill, automatically, with no forms. Who is eligible is known to the social services agency, not to the utility, and the minister's office asks what it will take, by tomorrow. As with M-Pesa, the sentence is the easy part. Tomás walks it down the map, layer by layer: a new driver under a goal the utility already has, keep bills affordable, and an outcome to check; three capabilities; a new product, a new step in billing, and a new partner, the agency, with an agreement; a new business object, eligibility, with its dates; the billing and customer systems, and a secure exchange. Every element it touches has an owner, so the walk is also the list of who must agree. The agency already knows who is eligible, so, once only, as in Tartu, the agreement asks it for the least: the utility sends its account holders' matching details, and the agency answers only "eligible, from this date", never which support a household gets. The agreement starts that day, because agreeing it will take longer than changing the systems. Tomás's first draft sends the list to the network too, to take the discount off the network charge; Ama checks it against the third principle, customer information stays with the business that collected it, and crosses it out. The decision is written down, with why: retail applies the whole discount, and nothing new crosses the wall. Eligibility brings three new rules, each with a home, an owner and a reason, and in the catalogue the call centre sees "on the social tariff", and nothing more. Down at the counter, Farah asks who the match will miss: tenants whose account is in a landlord's name. Found now, not in the first bills, they get a second route: an application, later in the year. Bills now carry a discount line, so the accounts and bills product gets a new column for it, and amount due keeps its meaning. A new column breaks nothing that reads it, so the contract stays at version 1; a second version is the last resort, for removing or redefining a column. Lineage finds everyone who reads it, and each owner hears of it before the first bills. The exchange with the agency gets its own contract too. The path is drawn in plateaus, in order, and by the morning the minister's office has one page. On the first of July, the first bills carry the discount, and about nine in ten eligible households get it without asking. The head of retail confirms the measure Farah has asked for since 2024, households falling behind, and the goal can now be steered. In Kenya, within a few years, most households had someone using M-Pesa, and money sent home arrived in minutes. Tomás's note has its answer; but a map that's used every week goes stale.

## Rigour to check before recording

- **Kenya, 2007.** Safaricom, then part-owned by Vodafone, launched M-Pesa in March 2007 (Nick Hughes and Susie Lonie, "M-PESA: Mobile Money for the 'Unbanked'", *Innovations* 2 (1–2), 2007, 63–81; the day usually given is 6 March). The pilot began on 11 October 2005 with about 500 clients of the microfinance lender Faulu Kenya and eight agent stores, part-funded by the UK's Financial Deepening Challenge Fund; it was designed for repaying loans, and its users found other uses, among them sending money to people outside the pilot and airtime home as an informal remittance. Safaricom chose "Send Money Home" for the launch, partly because a mass launch through a microfinance lender would be too complex (Hughes and Lonie, pp. 74–77). The film's sentence for the network's strategy, to carry money as well as calls, is its own plain reading of that decision: a mobile network moving money for everyone, not only calls. The film leaves the pilot out, so the strategy reads as the decision it was (the author's review, 20 October 2026). The Central Bank of Kenya sent a letter of no objection in February 2007, not a licence: "lets it run" (Alliance for Financial Inclusion, *Enabling mobile money transfer: the Central Bank of Kenya's treatment of M-Pesa*, 2010). Customers' money was held in a trust account at the Commercial Bank of Africa, by a trust company separate from Safaricom (Hughes and Lonie; IFC, *Safaricom M-PESA: M-Money Channel Distribution Case – Kenya*, data to March 2009; Njuguna Ndung'u, *M-Pesa – a success story of digital financial inclusion*, Blavatnik School of Government, 2017). The first agents were mostly Safaricom's airtime dealers (Hughes and Lonie, p. 70). Customers registered with "an official form of identification (typically the national ID card ... or a passport)" (William Jack and Tavneet Suri, *Mobile Money: The Economics of M-PESA*, NBER Working Paper 16721, 2011). The menu ran on the SIM card (the SIM Toolkit), so basic phones worked, though customers needed a new SIM (Hughes and Lonie; IFC). Before M-Pesa, Kenyans sending money within the country mostly sent it with family or a friend (58%) or through a bus or matatu company (27%) (FinAccess 2006, in AFI 2010); losses to theft are named in the sources but not measured, so the film doesn't put a number on what didn't arrive. Over 20,000 customers registered in the first month (Hughes and Lonie, p. 63), and about 2.1 million by 31 March 2008 (Safaricom, FY2008 results). Households with at least one user went from 43% in 2008 to nearly 70% in late 2009 (Jack and Suri 2011), and three in four within four years (Jack and Suri, *American Economic Review* 104 (1), 2014, 183–223): chapter 11's "Kenya · 2011". "Arrived in minutes" is the transfer itself, made and confirmed by text message; in the early years, receipts were sometimes late and an agent could run out of cash (IFC), so the film says nothing about "instantly". Suri and Jack (*Science* 354, 2016, 1288–1292) estimated that M-Pesa lifted about 2% of Kenyan households out of extreme poverty; Bateman, Duvendack and Loubere (*Review of African Political Economy* 46 (161), 2019) dispute the estimate, and the film makes no claim about poverty. All checked on 17 October 2026.

- **Automatic social tariffs.** The direction, its dates (November 2026, 1 July 2027), the agency and its matching are fictional and plausible; automatic social tariffs matched with another body's data are real practice. Portugal's social electricity tariff has been automatic since 1 July 2016 (Lei 7-A/2016; Portaria 178-B/2016): suppliers send each customer's name, tax number, supply point and address, through the energy directorate (DGEG), to Social Security and the Tax Authority, which return who qualifies; customers can object within 30 days (ERSE, *Tarifa Social na Eletricidade em 2026*, October 2026). It reached about 690,000 households from July 2016, up from about 140,000 families, and more than 800,000 by 2020 (UNRIC, 23 June 2020), and won a 2020 UN Public Service Award. In Great Britain, 96% of the 5.52 million Warm Home Discount rebates in 2025/26 were paid automatically, through data matching (DESNZ, *Warm Home Discount statistics, 2025 to 2026*, 18 June 2026); the department returns only the identifiers of eligible customers to the supplier (Digital Economy Act register, agreement 361).

## The promise

Every line passes two tests: someone who has never measured data quality can follow it, and a data quality practitioner, a registrar who has printed degree certificates, and a historian of Japanese surveying each find nothing wrong in it. The film shows the step the module's brief gives it: a check can tell whether a value is allowed (valid) or whether two records agree (consistent); only a reference outside the data can tell whether it's true (accurate). It names three references for the graduation list, from the closest to the truth: the graduate, the identity document seen at enrolment, and the exam boards' approved lists, kept apart from the student system. The closer the reference, the more it can tell you and the harder it is to reach, and every reference sees some things and not others. So every check says which reference it uses. Takeaway: **A check says it's allowed. Only a reference says it's true.**

## The story in one paragraph

Japan, 1800: Ino Tadataka, a retired merchant of 55, sets out from Edo to map the coast of Ezo, now Hokkaido. The maps of the time were drawn largely from estimates, and with Russian ships appearing in the north, the shogunate wanted a true map of that coast. His teams measured it on foot, each stretch with a rope or a chain and its direction with a compass, and every stretch added a small error that piled up unseen, because each measure looked right on its own. So, on clear nights, they measured the stars: a star's height as it crosses the south says how far north you stand, and no error in the walk can change it; where the walk and the stars disagreed, they corrected the walk. His teams walked for seventeen years; in 1821, three years after his death, they finished the map of all Japan, the base of its maps for decades, and his length for one degree of latitude is within a fraction of one per cent of today's. At the university, in February 2026, every one of the March list's 1,064 names passes the checks that say a name is allowed; the team's first checks, from a hundred records, raise doubts they can't settle. The registrar's office asks the graduates: from Monday 9 to Friday 20 February the portal shows each their name as it will be printed. Zoë Lefèvre sees hers without its accents and puts it right; 1,013 answer, 925 confirm their name and 88 correct it, every one of the 88 an allowed name. Mei's team checks each correction against the document seen at enrolment: 84 match, and 4 graduates who have changed their name send the document that shows the new one. The 51 who don't answer are printed as the student system holds them; the one among them with a name exactly 30 characters long is settled by the document. On Monday 23 February Jun's new check compares the list with the exam boards' approved lists, award by award: 1,101 approved, 1,099 on the list; two graduates of Arts and Laws had been held with Laws only, and both Arts certificates are printed. The boards' lists can see the gap because they're kept apart; a copy of the student system would have agreed, and been wrong. Each check now says which reference it uses: the name checks have none but their rule, and measure validity; the awards check measures accuracy, against the boards' lists; the data contract and the scorecard say the same. On Thursday 26 February the list goes to the printer. Ino's walk said how far his teams had come; the stars said how far north they really were.

### 6 · Say which reference · 7:20–8:19

**Narration.** So every check now says which reference it uses. Beside each check in the project, Jun writes its owner, what it measures, and, for an accuracy check, its reference. The name checks measure validity: they have no reference but the rule itself, so they can say a name is allowed, and no more. The awards check measures accuracy, against the boards' approved lists. For names, the reference is the graduate, and the list keeps the date each name was confirmed. The data contract says it too: its rule for awards names the dimension, accuracy, and the method, reconciliation. And the scorecard counts them apart: allowed, and true. ## Rigour to check before recording

Checked on 5 November 2026, and again as built on 10 November 2026 for the example project's parts and the pictures. The research notes are in the module's shared files, `3-valid-isnt-true/notes.md`.

- **The opening, Ino Tadataka.** Born in 1745 in Kazusa province; a merchant in Sawara who retired as head of the family at 49 or 50 (nippon.com says 49, the Tokyo National Museum 50), and studied astronomy and calendar-making in Edo under Takahashi Yoshitoki, 19 years younger than him. In 1800, at 55 (56 by the Japanese count), he led a small team to survey Ezo, today's Hokkaido, with the shogunate's sanction and a small stipend he topped up with his own money (nippon.com). Why: the shogunate wanted a correct map of Ezo for the country's defence (Mikami Mie, AKTIO Rensulting Magazine, 2025: 幕府は国防上の理由で蝦夷地の正しい地図を求めていた), with "the steady encroachment of Russian vessels" (nippon.com); he and Takahashi also wanted the length of a degree, to know the size of the Earth. His method: chains of measured lines along the coast, the traverse (導線法), distances by rope, chain, rods and pacing, directions by compass, between bamboo poles set at each bend; from time to time, sightings of distant peaks and of stars, with a small compass and quadrants, to check the traverse and correct its errors, "so the errors wouldn't grow" (伊能忠敬豆辞典, Q9). Latitude from the stars: "by observing the meridian altitude of fixed stars" (nippon.com), first-magnitude stars such as Betelgeuse, observed whenever the sky was clear (Japanese sources). Longitude was the challenge: eclipse timings with a pendulum clock failed for weather and for want of a clock to carry (nippon.com and Japanese sources), so the map's east-west errors are larger: the film says "an exact clock his teams could carry, and they had none". Seventeen years in the field, 1800 to 1816, on foot (Japanese Wikipedia's lead and nippon.com); he died in 1818, at 73; his team, led by Takahashi Yoshitoki's son Kageyasu, finished the map of all Japan, the *Dai Nihon Enkai Yochi Zenzu*, and presented it to the shogunate in 1821 (nippon.com; the Tokyo National Museum; Mikami). It was used until modern surveys replaced it after the Meiji Restoration, and was the base of the Meiji government's maps (Mikami; nippon.com): "the base of the country's maps for decades". His degree of latitude, 28.2 ri, about 110.75 km, against about 111 km today: within a fraction of one per cent (Nogami, *Journal of Geography*, 2020; nippon.com; secondary accounts give 0.1 to 0.6 per cent, depending on the length of a ri and the latitude compared). Not said: "every clear night" as a rule (one source says daily, another whenever it was clear: the film says "on clear nights"); the total distance walked (about 35,000 km, unverified); HMS Actaeon and the Siebold incident (unverified); which regions had the largest east-west errors.
- **Stars fix latitude, not longitude.** The module's seed said the stars "fix where a place really is"; they fix how far north it is. The film says so, and makes the limit a lesson: each reference sees some things and not others (`assumptions.md`, 2).
- **Valid, consistent, accurate.** DAMA-DMBOK (second edition, 2017), *Data Quality*, the dimensions: validity, values consistent with a defined domain; consistency, values agreeing within a record or across data sets; accuracy, the degree to which data correctly represents the real-world entity, measured against an authoritative source of reference. ISO 8000-8 splits a value's quality into syntactic quality (it conforms to its rules) and semantic quality (it corresponds to what it represents); ISO/IEC 25012 defines accuracy with a syntactic part (closeness to the values of a domain) and a semantic part (closeness to the true value). The film says "allowed", "agree" and "true", and DAMA's words once each; the ISO words are only here.
- **The reference.** Danette McGilvray's *Ten Steps* (second edition, 2021), the accuracy dimension: it needs an authoritative source of reference, and the assessment is often manual, record by record, against that source, starting from what matters most; the film's three references, from the closest to the truth, are practitioners' practice (assumed), in the module's words. Reconciliation against an independent record is what accountants and auditors do; a copy of the same system can only show consistency.
- **The data story.** Every number is the module's (`numbers.md`, *The truth, and the reconciliation*): 1,064 graduates on the March list; the checks that say a name is allowed, all passed; the first checks' doubts, 12 names exactly 30 characters long and the whole list without an accent (*The first checks*); the portal's confirmation from Monday 9 to Friday 20 February 2026, while graduates registered for the March ceremonies, with "This is my name" and "This isn't my name"; 1,013 of 1,064 answered (95.2 per cent), 925 as they were, 88 corrected (51 + 19 + 11 + 7 = 88); 84 matched the document seen at enrolment and 4 had changed their name since; the 51 who didn't answer printed as the student system held them, and the one of them among the 12 really 30 characters long (the other 11 are the 11 cut short); 51 names with accents once the corrections were in; 1,101 awards approved for 1,064 graduates, 37 double degrees (1,064 + 37 = 1,101), 1,099 on the list, the two missing Bachelor of Arts awards of two Arts and Laws graduates held with Laws only, found on Monday 23 February, three days before the list went on Thursday 26 February at 5 pm. Zoë Lefèvre, her date of confirmation (Wednesday 11 February 2026) and Nadeesha Herath Mudiyanselage Wijeratne (a family name of exactly 30 characters, a Sri Lankan name of the familiar *ge* pattern) are this film's own, invented, and in no other film. Only Nadeesha's family name is shown, on its record. The two other records in *Every name allowed*, a family name cut at the thirtieth character (Fitzwilliam-Montgomery Ashworth) and Marta Kowalski's names swapped, are invented examples of two of the 88's kinds. Andrés doesn't appear: he graduates in December, and reports his name in *Report, fix, tell*.
- **The legal name.** A degree certificate carries the graduate's legal name as the university records it, and a change needs the document that shows it: the usual rule of Australian universities' registrars (read in their published policies; the film's university is invented).
- **The project's files on screen.** Chapter 6's files are the example project's own, trimmed: the checks' `meta` as `tests/rules/_rules__tests.yml` and `models/marts/graduations/_graduations__models.yml` have it, in the order the narration takes them, with the owner's office left out; the contract's rule as `governance/5-data-contracts/mart_graduations__graduation_list.yaml` has it, its other lines left out and shown as "…"; and the scorecard as a view of the list's two checks, one row per dimension. The project's `docs/scorecard.md` counts every test by domain and dimension, and lists each accuracy test with its reference: the film's card is a simplified view of it (`assumptions.md`).
- **What the film says about films around it.** The first checks are *A hundred records*'; that the corrections didn't reach the student system until September is *The hidden factory*'s, so this film says only that the list took each name; what an empty confirmation date means is *Read by an agent*'s, so this film shows the 51 without a date and says nothing of what empty means (agreed with *Read by an agent*'s thread, 5 November 2026).

# Data governance · Who may see it: script

*The script of the fourth film of the data governance series, *Governance you can use*: about 5:38, in eight chapters, in English, painted in watercolour. Drafted on 21 October 2026 for the author's approval; nothing is recorded or rendered before the author approves it. Once the film is made, the narration lives in `source/src/narration.js` and the pauses in `source/src/breath.js`, and where this page and those files differ, the source wins. The title card follows chapter 1; the end card follows chapter 8. Made to read on a phone: on-screen text is at least 28 px in the frame, nothing sits under the captions, and no caption runs to three lines.*

## The promise

Every line passes two tests: anyone who has asked for access to data and waited weeks can follow it, and a privacy officer or a platform engineer finds nothing wrong in it. In 1983, West Germany's Constitutional Court stopped a census and drew a line that still holds: counting people is one permission, knowing who they are is another. In a university's data, that line runs between columns. Each column other teams read carries a class, *direct*, *pseudonymous* or *none*, written beside it when it's made and checked on every change. The class goes to the platform as a tag, and one rule there masks direct personal data for everyone outside the office that holds it, in every table the column reaches. Access is for a purpose, until a date, and an access review reads the grants themselves. And people are told why the mask is there, and how to get more when a purpose needs it. **Classify once; let the platform protect it everywhere.**

## The story in one paragraph

In April 1983, every resident of West Germany is to answer the census. The same law lets each town hall correct its register of residents from the answers. Hundreds of groups call for a boycott, and two weeks before census day the Constitutional Court stops it. In December it rules that, in principle, each person decides what is disclosed about them and how it's used; with computers, no data can be assumed insignificant. It doesn't forbid counting: names and addresses are to be kept apart from the answers and removed as soon as possible, and answers given to be counted don't go back to the town hall. The census runs that way in 1987. Today, the university has a census too: Planning counts its learners, and its list of learners near an award has no names. Clara, in Planning, needs each learner's status from the learner table, to plan next term's places. Yusuf's binder 6, *Data protection*, says personal data is classified and protected, but not which data, or how. So until now, access meant a form, an approval by email and, three weeks later, the whole table, names and emails included, granted to her by name, with no end: Yusuf finds forty-one people who can read it, nine of them in other jobs now. This term the path starts at the column. Every column other teams read carries a class, and when the check arrived, the owners found one surprise: a credential's key looks like a code, but an award's key holds the student's ID. The class goes to the platform as a tag, and one rule masks every direct column for everyone outside the registrar's office. The registrar's office owns the learner table, and Mei, for her office, says yes to Clara: to plan next term's places, until 15 December, through a Planning group. Clara sees keys and statuses; names and emails are stars. When she asks why, Yusuf tells her, and shows her the way to more. On 15 December her access ends by itself, and later that month Yusuf's review of the learner table reads the grants: two groups now, each with a reason to be there.

## The look

*Watercolour*, the series' look, as in *Off the shelf*.

- **Pencil and wash.** Every place is painted on paper, with a pencil line and paint gathering in the paper's grain. People are drawn as the master data films draw them, with a soft wash of colour behind each one.
- **The university's own places,** from the series' shared set: Planning, the registrar's office and its desk close-up, the data team and its board close-up, the platform team's desk close-up, the floor of offices behind glass. Nothing is redrawn: the film adds its things on top.
- **1983 is painted the same way,** with the place and year in the corner, in places of work and public life, never homes: a town hall's register office with its card drawers and a counter of tied census forms; a street corner with a newspaper kiosk and an advertising column; the court's hall, glass and wood, with its long bench; a table seen from above with one form on it. The hall is painted plainly, not copied from the court's own, and carries no emblem. People wear 1983: a clerk in a blouse and cardigan, passers-by in parkas, corduroy and raincoats, the judges in their red robes with white jabots and red caps. Every word on screen is in English; the posters carry pictures, not slogans.
- **Colour carries meaning, as in the series.** Gold is governance's decisions and, behind a person, the business side; cyan is the technical side. This film adds the classes: **plum** for direct personal data, a **paler plum** for pseudonymous (still personal, so the same family), and plain paper for none. A mask is a plum band with stars on it. Red keeps its series meaning (what nobody owns yet) and isn't used for the classes or the mask.
- **Fewer diagrams, more situations.** The one diagram-like picture is the data team's board of pinned cards, on which the mask follows the column. Code on screen is the example project's own lines.

## Sound

A new palette ([`source/tools/score.py`](source/tools/score.py), when the film is made). A felt piano and a low clarinet hold the chords, with soft strings under them: A minor for 1983, C major for the university today. Music plays under the opening story, at a chapter's turn, under a moment on screen and in the wordless pauses, and never as a bed under the voice. A small glockenspiel, low-passed, marks the moments that land: two notes for the line drawn across the form, a falling third each time a mask settles. The series' mark, 1-5-4-8, on the clarinet, plays under the title and the end card. No urgent pulse: the film's turn is a decision, not a rush. The effects are paper, a card drawer sliding, string on a bundle, a pencil line, a tin box's lock, a soft shredder, keys typed, a pin pressed into cork, and a soft rubber stamp, each at the moment its thing appears, all below about 2.5 kHz. The mix is -16 LUFS, with the music about 21 dB under the voice.

## Labs (candidates)

1. **Classify the columns.** Twelve columns from the university's models, with a few sample values and a description each. Class each as direct, pseudonymous or none, and say why. Two are traps: a key that holds a student ID, and a hash of an email.
2. **Who sees what?** Five people (Clara in Planning, Mei in the registrar's office, Jun in the data team, an analyst in a faculty, the data team's agent) and the learner table's columns. Say what each one sees in each column, value or stars, and what each would need to see more.
3. **Purpose and date.** Four access requests. For each, say whether it needs direct data, which group it goes to, and when it should end.

## Rigour to check before recording

Checked on 20 and 21 October 2026; the sources are listed below.

- **The 1983 census.** The Census Act of 25 March 1982 (*Volkszählungsgesetz 1983*, BGBl I p. 369) ordered a census of population and occupations, with questions on buildings and housing, and a count of workplaces, "as of 27 April 1983" (§ 1, quoted in the judgment, German text ¶ 4). Every resident was obliged to answer. The questionnaires were to be handed out from 18 April and collected by early May 1983 (German text ¶ 133). Questions included citizenship, occupation and the means of transport used (bpb). "So the country can plan housing, schools and transport" simplifies the purpose; the court speaks of the information needed "for carrying out public planning functions" (¶ 162).
- **The register comparison.** § 9(1) of the 1983 Act, in the court's English text: "Census data collected pursuant to § 2 nos. 1 and 2 may be compared with the civil registers and used for the purposes of correcting the latter." § 9(2) and (3) allowed other transfers to public authorities. "Town hall" stands for the municipalities that keep the civil registers (*Melderegister*).
- **The boycott.** By the end of March 1983, about 500 groups supported the boycott (netzpolitik.org, citing Nicole Bergmann's history of the protests), so the film says "hundreds". The posters on the column are invented and carry no slogans.
- **The stop.** On 13 April 1983, the court suspended the census by an interim order (BVerfGE 64, 67, cited in the judgment's German text ¶ 2), by five votes to three; the Senate was unanimous that at least § 9 had to be suspended (the order, as reprinted by the Humanistische Union). Census day was 27 April: two weeks later.
- **The judgment of 15 December 1983** (BVerfGE 65, 1; 1 BvR 209, 269, 362, 420, 440, 484/83), in the court's own English text:
  - Headnote 1: the general right of personality "encompasses the protection of the individual against the unlimited collection, storage, use and sharing of their personal data. This fundamental right confers upon the individual the authority to, in principle, decide themselves on the disclosure and use of their personal data." The film's "each person decides what is disclosed about them, and how it's used" says the same, and keeps "in principle": restrictions are allowed in an overriding public interest (headnote 2).
  - ¶ 150: "in the context of automatic data processing, it can therefore no longer be assumed that any data is insignificant." The narration's "With computers, no data can be assumed insignificant" keeps the court's hedge.
  - ¶ 146: a legal order would not be compatible with the right "if citizens were no longer able to tell who knows what kind of personal information about them, at what time and on which occasion." The film's closing question (who may see each column, for what, until when) is the same question, asked by the holder of the data.
  - The line: identifiers (name, address, identification number, census list number) "must be treated confidentially and kept separate from the rest of the data" (¶ 191); data is to be "rendered (effectively) anonymous as soon as possible" (¶ 161); personal data collected for statistics may not be shared, without being rendered anonymous, "for purposes pertaining to the exercise of administrative functions" (¶ 194). § 9(1) to (3) was declared void (operative part 2); the questions themselves were compatible with the Basic Law, with added safeguards (operative part 1). So "the court doesn't forbid counting" is the court's own result.
  - The court's English page abridges some paragraphs; the dates of 27 April and 13 April 1983 come from the German text.
- **1987.** The Census Act 1987 (*Volkszählungsgesetz 1987*, 8 November 1985, BGBl I p. 2078) has a section on separation and deletion (§ 15, *Trennung und Löschung*) and one forbidding re-identification (§ 17, *Verbot der Reidentifizierung*). § 15(1): the auxiliary characteristics (*Hilfsmerkmale*) are to be separated from the survey answers as soon as the statistical offices have checked the forms, and kept apart; street and house number stay with the answers only until each address is placed in its small area, then are deleted (§ 15(4)). § 15(2): the forms, with the auxiliary characteristics, are destroyed at the latest two weeks after the state's official population figure is set. The census day was 25 May 1987 (the Federal Statistical Office's series *Volkszählung vom 25. Mai 1987*); 99 percent took part, and it counted 61.08 million residents (bpb). The section texts were read in a search index of the act, not on a page that loaded: read § 8's list of auxiliary characteristics and § 15 in the *Bundesgesetzblatt* before recording. The film says only that the count went ahead "that way". Its shredder, on "removed as soon as possible", is the court's requirement, which § 15(2) wrote into the 1987 act.

- **Pseudonymous is still personal.** In data protection law, pseudonymised data, which can be attributed to a person with additional information kept separately, is still personal data (for example, the GDPR, Article 4(5) and Recital 26); the film names no law. An unsalted sha-256 of a student ID can be worked back by hashing every possible ID: the film's "anyone with the list of IDs could work it back".
- **Access that ends by itself.** Unity Catalog's grants have no end date of their own; an end date is kept by the identity provider (a group membership that expires) or by an access request system that removes the grant. The film names neither. *The marketplace* shows the request, approval and expiry as one flow, and *Governed in Unity Catalog* the platform's side.

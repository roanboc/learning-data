## The promise

Every line passes two tests: someone who has never used an AI agent can follow it, and an engineer who builds agents, a pilot, or a historian of flight finds nothing wrong in it. The film takes an agent apart into the four parts the author names: a model, which only writes; reasoning, which adds feedback and checks against rules; hands, tools that let it read and act, offered by tool servers through an open standard; and a loop, which keeps it working towards the goal until the checks pass. It shows the four at work on a question nobody had answered yet, and then on the same question a year later, when the steps are known and a plain script answers it in seconds. **An agent is a model that reasons, with hands, in a loop; use one only where a plain script can't do the job.**

## The story in one paragraph

On 18 June 1914, by the Seine just outside Paris, a contest for safer aeroplanes: flying is constant work by hand, tiring, and a tipped aeroplane can slip sideways and fall. Lawrence Sperry's box of four gyroscopes feels each tip, and pistons driven by air level the aeroplane again; over the river he raises his hands, his mechanic Emil Cachin walks out along the wing, the wing dips and comes back, and the judges give him first prize. The box grew into the autopilot: today pilots fly an airliner by hand for only a few minutes of a typical flight, and stay in their seats to watch and decide. The box has hands, a check and a loop, but no model, so it does one known job; put a model that reasons inside that loop, and you have an agent. Today, Planning asks Jun how the census-day count has changed since last year, an afternoon of small corrections by hand, so he gives it to an agent. A model alone can only guess. Reasoning writes a plan and checks it against the team's rules; hands, three read-only tools, Catalog, Query and Docs, plug in through an open standard; the loop counts 17,936, fails its check against the census report (476 short), reads the census rule, counts again, and passes: 18,412 students, 454 more than last year, with its evidence. The check is evidence, not approval: Jun decides. Next March the steps are known, so the count becomes a recipe with a test, approved by Noor, and runs in seconds, the same way every time, like Sperry's box; an agent can also take the long way, as one did when its plan didn't name its tools. Where the steps can be written down, a plain script; agents only for work whose steps nobody can write in advance.

### 6 · A loop · 4:04–5:01

Unlike Sperry's box, which can only push back, the model decides what to try next. The loop stops when the checks pass, at its limit of tries, or when it needs a person.

## Rigour to check before recording

Read on 3 November 2026 unless marked.

- **The day by the Seine.** 18 June 1914, on the Seine between the bridges of Bezons and Argenteuil, at the *Concours de la Sécurité en Aéroplane*, the airplane safety competition announced by the Aero Club of France and the French War Department; Sperry listed last on the programme; a Curtiss C-2 flying boat; Lawrence Sperry, 21, and Emil Cachin, his newly hired French mechanic (neither spoke the other's language); on the first pass Sperry flew with both arms raised; on the second, Cachin climbed out on the starboard wing, about 7 feet from the hull, the aeroplane banked under his weight, and the stabiliser corrected it; first prize, 50,000 francs. Sources: William Scheck, "Lawrence Sperry: Autopilot Inventor and Aviation Innovator", *Aviation History*, November 2004, on HistoryNet; "Celebrating 100 Years of Autopilot", *MONRONeYnews*, FAA Mike Monroney Aeronautical Center; Antonio Rodríguez-Laiz, "The origin of the autopilot", AERTEC. They differ on the number of entries (57 or 56: the film says neither), the order of the passes (the film follows HistoryNet), and the judge's ride (left out, as is the third pass, Sperry and Cachin both out on the wings with the seat empty). The film says "a young American" and shows "pilot, 21".
- **How the box worked.** Four gyroscopes at 7,000 rpm; as the aeroplane moved, linkages to valves worked pistons driven by compressed air, connected by levers to the control surfaces; the gyroscopes kept every control surface at zero unless a correction was needed; a wind-driven generator on the upper wing powered them (HistoryNet; AERTEC). The film's "pistons driven by air level it again" and its cutaway follow this; it says nothing of the generator.
- **The problem.** Bill Gourgey, "During WWI, a daredevil pilot helped invent the first 'drones'", *Popular Science*, 6 March 2026: "airplanes had come a long way, but they were still fragile machines that demanded constant human correction to stay aloft." AERTEC: "Flying for long periods of time at the controls of a vehicle causes significant strain and fatigue on the arms, shoulders and back. This system solved that problem." "A tipped aeroplane can slip sideways, and fall" is plain flight mechanics: an aeroplane banked and not corrected slips towards its lower wing and loses height. No source read gives accident numbers for 1914, so the film gives none.
- **The autopilot today.** "Grew into the autopilot": the FAA's *Celebrating 100 Years of Autopilot*; HistoryNet, "what is now famous as the automatic pilot". "Only a few minutes of a typical flight": the ICAO RASG-PA safety advisory on manual flight (RSA 2024-10B), from three large airlines' flight data, "manual flight represents on average less than 5 minutes of the total flight time"; Cranfield University, from about 14,000 flights of an A319 fleet, 95 per cent had less than 440 seconds of manual flying. Both were read in search results' extracts: on 3 November 2026 the ICAO pages and Cranfield's repository refused automated reading (HTTP 403, tried again in the evening), and Cranfield's page had been down that morning (HTTP 503). A third figure, seen only in search results the same day: a 2015 survey of airline pilots, reported second-hand, in which pilots of Boeing 777s said they flew a typical flight by hand for about seven minutes, and Airbus pilots about half that. The film's words, "only a few minutes", hold for every figure; a reader with access to the two pages can confirm the first two.
- **What an agent is.** Russell and Norvig, *Artificial Intelligence: A Modern Approach*: "An agent is anything that can be viewed as perceiving its environment through sensors and acting upon that environment through actuators", which Sperry's box already was; the film's four parts are the author's own (the plan's *insights*: "An agent is a model with reasoning (feedback and checks), hands (MCP) and a loop working towards a goal"). Reasoning interleaved with acting, each result fed back into the next thought: Yao and others, *ReAct: Synergizing Reasoning and Acting in Language Models* (2022; ICLR 2023). A model writes what's likely to come next: next-word prediction, whose history *Decades in the making* tells.
- **Hands, and the open standard.** The Model Context Protocol, an open standard for connecting tools to agents, introduced in November 2024 and given on 9 December 2025 to the Agentic AI Foundation, a directed fund of the Linux Foundation, with its governance unchanged; its current revision is 2026-07-28 (modelcontextprotocol.io). A server offers tools, each with a name and a description; its annotations, such as "read-only", are hints, not controls, so the film rests "can only read" on the account's rights, not on a label. Named once, as an open standard; no product is named.
- **Workflows versus agents.** Common practice since 2024: where the steps are known, a fixed workflow; an agent only where they aren't. Erik Schluntz and Barry Zhang, "Building effective agents" (Anthropic, 19 December 2024): workflows are "systems where LLMs and tools are orchestrated through predefined code paths", agents "systems where LLMs dynamically direct their own processes and tool usage"; find "the simplest solution possible, and only increasing complexity when needed. This might mean not building agentic systems at all." Cited here only; the film names no company. The module's *Decided* row: "Automate deterministically; use agents only where the work isn't deterministic or no tool can make it so."
- **The author's examples.** "A plain rerun needs no agent", said in his words; and an agent that browsed the web instead of using its tool server because the plan didn't say what was available, told as "one of the team's agents" and kept short: series 2's *How it goes wrong* tells it in full.
- **The data story's numbers**, invented, and adding up: 18,412 students studying on census day, 31 March 2026, the series' count (*Many agents, and what emerges* builds on it); counted as they are today, 17,936, which is 476 short; last year's census report, 17,958, so 454 more. "Students", not "enrolments": *What's in a word* gives the university 52,000 enrolments and about 18,000 students. Counting today instead of on census day is the mistake *What's in a word*, *Silent change* and *Both at once* teach. The agent's run, "4 min · 12 steps", and the script's "3 s" are believable for one count. "limit: 10 rounds" is a setting the team chose, not a standard.
- **The module's rules, kept:** the tools only read, set on their account (fewest rights); the agent's check is evidence, never approval, and Jun decides what goes to Planning; the recipe is approved by Noor, a second person, before it runs on the schedule; deterministic first, the agent only for the question nobody had answered; plans name their tools, and the agent gets only those the job needs.
- **Names on the day.** No product is named. The Model Context Protocol is an open standard, not a product, and is kept by the Agentic AI Foundation (checked 3 November 2026).

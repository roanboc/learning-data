## The promise

Every line passes two tests: someone new to data work can follow it, and a data manager, a product owner or a delivery lead finds nothing wrong in it. Its idea: bring every request in through one door, onto one list that holds all the team's work, and order it in the open: by its value (the opportunity it brings and the risk it prevents, with what would be lost by waiting counted in), divided by the effort; never by who asks, or how loudly. Then protect the team's time with a split, limit the work in progress, and give every "not now" a reason and a date. It closes the series: the case is funded, its risks named, everyone at the table, and now the work itself is in one place.

**Takeaway:** "One door, one list, ordered in the open."

## The story in one paragraph

On the Rhine in 1792, Dominique-Jean Larrey, a young surgeon in the French army, sees the wounded wait: the army's rules keep its field hospital a league behind the fighting, the wounded lie where they fall until the battle ends, and help, stuck behind the army's wagons, never comes in less than a day or a day and a half; most die waiting, and many do at Speyer that autumn. He builds a flying ambulance, a light carriage on springs that follows the front line; from 1793 his surgeons bandage the wounded where they fall, and at Aboukir in 1799, by his own account, none of 800 wounded waits more than a quarter of an hour. In 2027, requests for data reach Helena Novak's team in eleven places: people ask twice to be heard, the team answers whoever reaches it first, and bigger needs wait unseen, as the worst wounds waited at Jena in 1806 while the walking wounded filled the surgeons' first day. On Monday 7 June the front door opens, with five questions; the side doors close, kindly; 61 requests become one list, and 45 are left once nine duplicates join their twins and seven close with thanks. The list is ordered in the open, by value and effort; the team's time is split on purpose; at most six pieces of work are in progress; and Clara's request hears "not now", with a reason and a date.

## The reference, and practice

The module's spine holds (TOGAF's method, ArchiMate's words; `../../a-plan-in-ninety-days/proposal.md`). This film is about the team's own way of working, so it names, once each:

| Body of knowledge | What the film takes | Where |
|---|---|---|
| Value and effort, as the author orders work (12 November 2026): "We generally follow the principle value (positive risk or negative risk prevention) and effort"; with time kept as part of value, as he agreed the same day | A request's value, from the opportunity it brings and the risk it prevents, counting what would be lost by waiting; divided by the effort, the order | *Ordered in the open*: "each request is weighed for the opportunity it brings, and the risk it prevents." "Together, they're its value." "Divided by the effort, it puts small, valuable work first." "And time is part of that value: what would be lost by waiting counts too." |
| The Kanban Method (David J. Anderson, *Kanban*, 2010) | One board for all the work; a limit on work in progress; pulling the next piece in when one is done | *Six at once*: "Kanban, as David J. Anderson set it out, calls this a limit on work in progress" |
| Triage, as emergency departments practise it (the Australasian Triage Scale, ACEM's policy P06) | An order by need, said out loud: five categories, each with a longest wait | *Not now, and when* |
| Larrey's own rule (his memoirs, 1814 translation) | The most dangerously wounded first, without regard to rank or distinction; those hurt less can wait | *Ordered in the open* |

Where the film differs: until 12 November 2026 it ordered the list by the cost of delay (Donald Reinertsen, *The Principles of Product Development Flow*, 2009): a request's value, its urgency and the risk while it waits, divided by the effort, his "cost of delay divided by duration". The author teaches a lighter rule, value and effort, so the film follows it and names no method. Risk is meant both ways, as ISO 31000 has it (*Risks, said out loud*): an opportunity, or a harm to prevent. Urgency is no longer a measure of its own, but time stays part of value. That was put to the author the same day ("This leaves out urgency. Two requests with the same value and effort are not equal if one loses its value next month. I'd keep time as part of value, without naming a method."), and he answered "Agreed." So what would be lost by waiting counts inside the two rows of value, and gets no row of its own: a date that can't move counts as a risk prevented (a late timetable), and a request that loses nothing by waiting, as Clara's new model does until December's admissions, can wait. The author said "or", since a request may bring an opportunity, prevent a risk or both; the card shows both and adds them. The division by effort is the film's way to turn his principle into an order a newcomer can read, with one to three dots for each measure. The cost of delay and SAFe's weighted shortest job first, which adds time criticality to much the same parts, are also worth knowing; neither is named in the film, and its page keeps the cost of delay as further reading. Kanban sets its limits per column or per class of work; the film's limit is split as the team's time is (three for the roadmap, two for requests, one for running and improving), so the limit and the split say the same thing. The time split itself is a practitioner's habit, not a framework's rule.

Practice, from the module's brief and from how delivery leads run a team's work: one way in for every request, a form that asks what the work is for, not what to build; the side doors closed kindly, each with a note, never by refusing what arrives there; all the team's work on one board, not only the requests; the weighing done in the open, on every card, by value and effort, with time as part of value (the author, 12 November 2026); the team's time split on purpose; a limit on work in progress; a reason and a date for every "not now"; and the list open to everyone, read by the data council. Each is said in *On Monday*.

## Rigour to check before recording

Checked on 10 November 2026:

- Larrey's memoirs, in Richard Willmott Hall's translation (*Memoirs of Military Surgery, and Campaigns of the French Armies*, Baltimore, 1814), [volume 1](https://collections.nlm.nih.gov/catalog/nlm:nlmuid-2561003RX1-mvpart) and [volume 2](https://collections.nlm.nih.gov/catalog/nlm:nlmuid-2561003RX2-mvpart), in the US National Library of Medicine's digital collections:
  - Vol. 1, pp. 23–24, on the Rhine in 1792: "The military regulations required that they should always be one league distant from the army. The wounded were left on the field, until after the engagement"; the ambulances, held up by "the number of wagons interposed between them and the army", "never arrived in less than twenty-four or thirty-six hours, so that most of the wounded died for want of assistance. Many were wounded at the capture of Spire, and a great part of them fell victims to this inconvenience." Speyer (Spire) was taken at the end of September 1792, hence "that autumn". A league is about four kilometres.
  - Vol. 1, p. 28: the plan "of an ambulance, calculated to follow the advanced guard in the same manner as the flying-artillery", "a carriage, so suspended, as to unite swiftness to solidity and ease", which he called the flying ambulance; first used in the winter of 1792–93, so the film says "from 1793".
  - Vol. 1, pp. 81–82, the campaign in Italy, 1797: "light carriages on springs", the small ones with two wheels, "drawn by two horses", carrying two patients at full length. The plate is drawn from this.
  - Vol. 1, pp. 223–224, Aboukir, 1799: "Our army had eight hundred wounded"; "none were left more than a quarter of an hour without being dressed". The film says "by his own account".
  - Vol. 2, pp. 122–123, Jena, 1806, where he wasn't present ("I shall merely state what was reported to me", hence "heard of"): "the soldiers who were slightly wounded, and were able to walk, had entirely occupied the attention of the surgeons during the first day"; and his rule: "Those who are dangerously wounded should receive the first attention, without regard to rank or distinction. They who are injured in a less degree may wait [...]; finally, life is not endangered by such wounds." At Eylau, in 1807, he calls it "a rule which I established".
- Not said, because no source found says it outright: that rank decided who was treated first before Larrey. Kenneth Iserson and John Moskop, *Triage in medicine, part I* (*Annals of Emergency Medicine*, 2007; [PubMed 17141139](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/17141139/)), confirm his rule, not what came before it. Larrey never used the word triage (from the French *trier*, to sort), and the film doesn't say he did.
- The Australasian Triage Scale: [ACEM, policy P06](https://acem.org.au/getmedia/484b39f1-7c99-427b-b46e-005b0cd6ac64/P06_Policy_Australasian_Triage_Scale) (version 5, November 2023): five categories, with longest waits of immediately, 10, 30, 60 and 120 minutes; the film says "from at once to two hours". The university is in Australia, so its scale is the one shown.
- The order, checked again on 12 November 2026. The author's answer in the project chat: "We generally follow the principle value (positive risk or negative risk prevention) and effort." So the card weighs the opportunity a request brings and the risk it prevents, calls the two its value, and divides by the effort: Anil's 2 + 3 over 1 is 5, the dashboard's 1 + 1 over 3 is 0.7, the rooms' data's 3 + 3 over 2 is 3, and Clara's model's 3 + 1 over 3 is 1.3, so the order on screen is the one the film had. Risk as an opportunity or a threat is ISO 31000:2018's sense: risk is the "effect of uncertainty on objectives", and a note to the definition says the effect "can be positive, negative or both, and can address, create or result in opportunities and threats" (the note read as the European Union Agency for Railways' terminology collection reproduces it, found by search; the standard itself not read). No longer said or shown: the cost of delay and its division by duration (Reinertsen, 2009; summarised on [Wikipedia, *Cost of delay*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cost_of_delay), which names the ratio "CD3"), and SAFe's names for its parts (user and business value, time criticality, and risk reduction, as checked on 10 November 2026). The page links the cost of delay as further reading, marked as not in the film.
- Time as part of value, 12 November 2026, later that day. A pushback on the order was put to the author in the project chat: "Value and effort. This leaves out urgency. Two requests with the same value and effort are not equal if one loses its value next month. I'd keep time as part of value, without naming a method." He answered: "Agreed."

# Master and reference data: one person, many roles

*Proposal for a series on master and reference data, v0.4. The folder is named after the topic; the series title is a working one, The same one, everywhere. Status: the opening film built; films 2 to 9 approved on 17 October 2026, each made in its own thread. It's the companion of the data governance series, proposed in [`../data-governance/proposal.md`](../data-governance/proposal.md), and shares its decisions.*

## Analysis

**What works**

- **Everyone is a person with roles.** A learner who also tutors, a patient who is also a nurse, a bank customer who guarantees a loan, a household that is both a customer and a generator (*Who it serves, and how it pays*). Every industry has this, and most systems get it wrong the same way: they store the role and call it the person.
- **The example project already shows the problem.** The learner arrives three times with three keys (*What makes it the same one*); two people share a family email; two learners share a typed student ID; Mei decides each match in `learner_identity_decisions.csv`. But the project models a *learner*, a role, not a person. The series makes that its first correction, in the open (PLAYBOOK §8).
- **Master data management is usually sold as a product.** The films show it as a few decisions (what's a person, which roles exist, who creates each, which value wins, who decides a match, what each role may see) and a few enablers, most of which a data platform already has.

**What this proposal adds**

- **Person, role, contact point and identifier are four different things.** A person is someone; a role is how they relate to the organisation, for a time; a contact point (an email, an address) can be shared or change by purpose; an identifier belongs to a system or a role, not to the person.
- **Decide per attribute, not per system.** The legal name from the student system, the preferred name from the person, the work email from HR, the personal email from where the person last confirmed it.
- **A false merge costs more than a missed match.** Two people joined into one shows someone else's record in an app. Matching is a decision with a person accountable, not a score.
- **Purpose travels with the role.** What a person told the university as a learner isn't automatically available to the team that asks alumni for donations. The master record is where that's honoured, and where a request to be forgotten reaches every role.
- **Start where you are.** The platform already resolves identity for analytics. The series shows when that's enough, and the lightest step when it isn't.

### 2. Who is it?

**In a line.** Linh's two student numbers are linked to one person, by a steward, with a reason; a near look-alike waits for a person to decide.

**Opening.** France, the 1550s: a man comes back to a village as Martin Guerre, and his wife, his family and the village accept him for years, until the real one walks into the courtroom. A false match everyone wanted to believe.

**First, two words people confuse.** Master data is the things the business is about: Linh, an award, a supplier, a meter. Reference data is the short lists used to sort and describe them: countries, role types, statuses, currencies. Master data has many rows, keeps growing, and its records have to be matched and reconciled across systems; reference data has few rows, changes rarely, often comes from outside (a standard's country list), and is picked from, not matched. A test: if you'd ever ask "is this the same one as that?", it's master data; if you only ever choose a value from a list, it's reference data. Some lists sit on the line (faculties, products): they're master data once they have their own attributes, owners and lifecycles.

**And what a master record is.** A master record (often called a golden record) is the one trusted version of a thing, put together from every source that knows it: a stable ID of its own that never changes or gets reused; the best value for each attribute, each labelled with the source and rule that chose it; and links to every source record it came from, which stay as they arrived. It's not a copy of the best system's row, and it's not a record no one can trace: it can be explained, value by value, and undone, link by link.

**Story.** Every intake, the registry runs a duplicate check. This one proposes three pairs, and Mei's office, the steward of the person, decides each, with a reason that's kept, never asked again, and can be undone:

1. **S-17142 and S-20436.** Same name, same birth date: Linh, back to study. Mei links them to one person, and the registry merges the numbers in its own system.
2. **S-20436 and HR's E-4417.** Same name, but birth dates one digit apart (14 March, 14 May) and different emails. The score lands in the review band; Mei checks with HR, finds a typo, and links them with that reason.
3. **Two Aishas.** A learning platform account carries another student's number, typed by mistake (decision `D-003` in the example project). Same number, different people: merging them would show one student's results to the other.

**Ideas.** Master data and reference data, and how to tell them apart. The master (golden) record: master ID, best values with provenance, links to sources. Identifiers by type and by issuer, qualified by where they come from (key sets). Match rules: exact (a shared identifier) and likely (name, date of birth, contact point), with three outcomes: match, review, no match. False merges and missed matches, and why the first costs more. Link, don't destroy: source records stay as they arrived; the cross-reference joins them. Stewardship as a queue with an owner and a time limit.

**The example.** Pair 2, scored weight by weight: given name, same, +3.4; family name, same, +3.3; birth date one digit apart, a likely typo, +0.9; email, different, −0.4; student number, only one record has it, 0. Score 74, in the review band (60 to 90). What would flip it: if the birth dates agreed, 93, an automatic link. The agent's note for Mei, with the names masked: "same names; birth dates differ by one digit; the HR record's work email is on the university's domain". It suggests; Mei decides.

**Reconciliation rules, part one: matching.** Blocking finds candidates without comparing everyone with everyone (same family name sound and birth year; same email; same identifier). Each comparison adds or subtracts weight, by how often it agrees for the same person and by chance. Two thresholds make three bands: link automatically, review, keep apart. A steward's "not the same" is remembered, and binds the matcher. No rule says two student numbers are never one person: a student who comes back has two.

**Elsewhere.** A hospital merges two patients' records, and a test result follows the wrong one. Health systems keep a person's identifiers by issuer for exactly this reason.

**The enabler.** Where the data is transformed (in the example project, YAML and SQL): queries that propose pairs without comparing everyone with everyone, the example project's decisions table (`learner_identity_decisions.csv`) grown into a person queue, and a test that every proposed pair is decided. On the platform: the master record table and its links.

**Why it's common.** Glasgow's registry names students who return among the causes of duplicate records, and merges them into the number the student graduated on ([guidance](https://www.gla.ac.uk/myglasgow/registry/staff/studentrecords/duplicatestudentrecords)); Galway asks past students to give their old number so it can keep their full history under it ([notice](https://www.ugal.ie/media/registrationoffice/files/Merging-numbers.pdf)).

**On screen.** First, two shelves: big, varied cards of things (master) and small bright chips of lists (reference), with the test between them. A golden record assembling from coloured source cards, each value keeping its colour as a label, a master ID stamped on top. Then two cards drawn together, and a gold decision card between them: *same*, or *different*, with its reason.

**Labs (candidates).** *Master or reference?* *Build the golden record.* *Same person?* *Match, review or no match?*

### 3. Which value wins

**In a line.** Once Linh's records are linked, their values disagree, and a rule declared for each field decides which one wins.

**Opening.** 1831: HMS Beagle sails with 22 chronometers, because a ship's longitude depends on the time, and clocks drift. Which clock to trust when they disagree is decided before leaving port.

**Story.** Her legal name is NGUYỄN Thị Linh: family name first, with its accents. HR typed "Linh Nguyen". She set her preferred name, Linh, herself. She has four emails (her student one, her staff one, and two personal ones from 2019 and 2025), HR has an old mobile number, and HR's birth date is the typo from *Who is it?*. Today the example project's learner already chooses ("from the student system if it knows them, then the learning platform"), but only for one role, inside the SQL. Jun declares it for the person, field by field: the legal name from the student system, checked against her passport; the preferred name from Linh; each email kept as a contact point with its purpose and the date she last confirmed it; the birth date from the student system. Each value keeps a label of where it came from and when.

**The example.** Linh's master record, field by field, each value with its source, the rule that chose it, and its age; the runners-up are one click away. Her transcript carries her legal name; her class lists and ID card, her preferred one. A rule change (newest confirmed email first) runs first as a dry run, listing every value it would change, before Mei's manager approves it.

**Reconciliation rules, part two: survivorship.** A default order for each attribute (trust by source, then recency, then completeness), trust that can differ by attribute (HR for work email, the person for preferred name), source policies (a change to a critical value from a less trusted source waits for review), and a steward's pin that holds a value until a date.

**Ideas.** Survivorship: source priority, recency, completeness, and a steward's override. Rules per attribute, not per system. Legal and preferred names, and why both matter; names in their own order and script. Contact points by purpose instead of one "email" column. The person correcting their own details as a source.

**Elsewhere.** A bank keeps the legal name on the account and the preferred name in its app.

**The enabler.** Survivorship rules declared per field where the data is transformed: a master data tool's screens, or in the simplest form a rules file in YAML, with the SQL and tests written from it; a source and a date beside every value; contact points as their own table.

**Why it's common.** University name policies keep the legal name on transcripts and verifications and the preferred one on rosters and ID cards ([Case Western](https://case.edu/registrar/forms-services/forms/preferred-name-policy/)). Vietnamese names put the family name first, Thị is a common middle name for women, and names are often reversed in English-speaking countries ([Cultural Atlas](https://theculturalatlas.org/vietnamese-culture/vietnamese-culture-naming)). One of the Beagle's chronometers is going on show at Greenwich ([Royal Museums Greenwich](https://www.rmg.co.uk/press-release/pocket-chronometer-used-on-darwins-beagle-voyage-acquired-national-maritime-museum)).

**On screen.** Three values for one field; a rule card; the winner moves into the person's record, its source label attached.

**Labs (candidates).** *Which value wins?* *Write the rule.*

### 4. Roles come and go

**In a line.** Each of Linh's roles gets a start, an end and an owner, so the university can say who she is on any date.

**Opening.** Rome, the 2nd century BC: an ambitious citizen climbs a ladder of public offices, each held for a year, in a set order, and the records list every office against one name. The same person, a sequence of roles, each with its dates.

**Story.** On census date, Planning counts how many current students also work for the university. The alumni office asks the question it couldn't answer in *One person, many roles*: which of our alumni are studying again? Linh's roles become dated bands under her: applicant (2019, and again in 2025), student (2019 to 2022, and from July 2025), alumna (since 2022), casual tutor (a new contract each teaching period since 2023, so the role starts and ends every term), and researcher (March to December 2026). People relate to each other too: her supervisor on the project.

**The example.** A line drawn at census date, 31 March 2026, across her bands: student, tutor and researcher are open, so she counts once, as a student who works here, not three times. The alumni office's question becomes a filter: alumni with an open student role.

**Ideas.** Roles as dated relationships between a person and the organisation (or another party). Concurrent and successive roles. Role lifecycles (applicant to alumna, and back). As-was and as-is, applied to roles (*One row of what, and when*). Relationships between people. Who owns each role.

**Elsewhere.** An energy account holder moves out; the person who stays is on life support. The role ended; the person, and the risk, didn't (*How value reaches people*).

**The enabler.** A role table with the person, the role type, its owner, `valid_from` and `valid_to`, and what it relates to (a course, a contract, a project). Tests that a role never ends before it starts and that every role has a person; the platform answers "as at" any date.

**Why it's common.** Tutors are often offered work one teaching period at a time: "for each module for each defined Teaching Period (usually a semester)" ([Sussex](https://sussex.ac.uk/humanresources/documents/terms-and-conditions-for-doctoral-tutors-2024.pdf)).

**On screen.** A timeline of bands under one figure, overlapping and ending; a census line drawn across them.

**Labs (candidates).** *Which roles, on census day?* *Draw Linh's timeline.*

### 5. Lists everyone uses

**In a line.** Each list that sorts Linh gets one owner, dates and one home, and the tests read it instead of repeating it.

**Opening.** Airport codes, the 1930s to today: Toronto's airport is YYZ after an old station code for Malton, where it stands, and Ho Chi Minh City's is still SGN, for Saigon. A code outlives its reason, because changing it costs everyone.

**Story.** HR calls her role "Casual Academic", payroll "CAS", the learning platform "tutor". No one has written down which kinds of identifier exist. The status codes are mapped in one file and typed again in a test. Her country is "Vietnam" in one system, "Viet Nam" in another and "VN" in a third. Jun moves each list into a table with who publishes it and from when it applies: role types, identifier types, relationship types, consent purposes, and countries from the international standard. A change is a pull request its owner approves. Lists that describe people, like titles or gender, stay short and inclusive, and are asked for only with a purpose.

**The example.** The role type list: code, label, owner (HR for tutors, the registrar for students), and the date each value applies from; beside it, each system's own codes mapped to it. The country list, loaded from the standard, with Viet Nam once.

**Ideas.** Internal and external reference data. Codes and labels. Mappings between a system's codes and the canonical ones (`status_map.csv`). Owner and publisher. Effective dates and versions. External lists adopted, not retyped, and refreshed on a schedule. Reference data about people is personal in its effect.

**Elsewhere.** A bank's list of customer types decides which checks a customer goes through; a hospital's list of relationship types decides who may be told about a patient.

**The enabler.** Reference tables with `published_by`, `approved_on`, `valid_from` and `valid_to`; tests that read their allowed values from them; outside lists loaded on a schedule, as the project already loads its glossary.

**Why it's common.** The same country, three spellings: the country and the UN use "Viet Nam", British English "Vietnam" ([UK place names factfile](https://assets.publishing.service.gov.uk/media/6981e349015e2ba11991bb6f/Vietnam_Toponymic_Factfile.pdf)). Airport codes: [IATA airport code](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/IATA_airport_code).

**On screen.** A spreadsheet attachment in an email; then a short, dated list pinned to each test that reads it.

**Labs (candidates).** *Where does this list live?* *Map the codes.*

### 6. For this purpose only

**In a line.** What each role collected stays with that role's purpose, and Linh's "don't ask me for donations" reaches every role because it sits on the person.

**Opening.** India, the Charaka Samhita, written down between about 100 BC and 200 AD: the oath a teacher gives a new medical student says "the peculiar customs of the patient's household shall not be made public". What you learn for one purpose isn't yours for every purpose.

**Story.** Two things happen in one month. Advancement plans an appeal and asks for this year's top students; their results were collected to award degrees, not to raise money. And the appeal's list reaches Linh through her new student number, although she told the alumni office years ago not to ask her for donations: that wish sat on her old record. With the person and her roles apart, each role carries the purposes its data may be used for, and Linh's wishes sit on the person. Then Linh asks the university what it holds about her, and the answer comes from one place, every role and every source.

**The example.** The appeal's list: 2,140 alumni selected, 37 taken off because their "no fundraising" sits on the person, Linh among them. Her request: every role, each value with its source, ready in days.

**Ideas.** Purpose limitation by role. Consent and wishes as data, with a purpose, a date and a way to withdraw, kept on the person. Requests for access and correction, answered from the master record (and deletion where it applies: research data often has to be kept for years). The master record as the place where privacy is honoured, not only where duplicates are removed (*Who may see it*, in the governance series). Kept generic: "in many places, the law…".

**Elsewhere.** A retailer's loyalty data, given for discounts, used to decide credit; a hospital's research participant whose consent covers one study, not all.

**The enabler.** Purposes and consent on each role, as data; access policies on the platform that filter by purpose (Unity Catalog's tag-based policies, checked for maturity when scripting); a request workflow that finds every role from the person.

**Why it's common.** Alumni offices keep a small record of a "don't contact me" so they can honour it, and ask for explicit consent before asking for donations ([UWE Bristol](https://www.uwe.ac.uk/alumni/stay-connected/alumni-privacy-notice)). In 2016 the UK regulator fined two charities, £25,000 and £18,000, over the "wealth screening" and selling of donors' data ([Civil Society](https://www.civilsociety.co.uk/news/ico-fines-rspca-and-british-heart-foundation-after-daily-mail-expose.html)). The oath: [Charaka shapath](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Charaka_shapath). The film stays general: no country's law.

**On screen.** Linh's record; one band lights, and only the doors marked with its purpose open.

**Labs (candidates).** *May this team use it?* *Answer the request.*

### 7. One person, many systems

**In a line.** The fix for *One person, many roles* lands where the problem started: before the online application creates a person, it searches for one.

**Opening.** Bern, 1874: before it, every pair of countries had to agree a postal treaty. The General Postal Union set one flat rate and one set of rules, and each country kept its own post office.

**Story.** The student app, HR, the research office and the alumni office all want the university's person. Noor lays four ways side by side: a registry that only links (the cross-reference, read when needed: what exists today), a copy for analysis (today's learner, widened), a master that systems read back, or one place where people are created. The university picks the third for the student app and HR, and one change at entry: the application searches for the person before it creates one. A clear match adds an applicant role to the person she already is; a likely one goes to Mei's queue instead of making a new number. Who may create a person, and who may only add a role, is written down.

**The example.** Linh applies for a PhD to start next year. The search finds her by her birth date, email and earlier numbers, so the application adds a role to the person she already is, and no new number is made. The same week another Linh Nguyen applies (a common Vietnamese name), born in another year and new to the university: the search keeps them apart, and she becomes a new person.

**Ideas.** The four ways of keeping master data (registry, consolidation, coexistence, centralised), and what each costs. Choose the lightest that solves the problem. The person published as a data product, with a contract and a version (*Who owns what*, *A product with an owner*), served live where an app needs it (*Both at once*). Who may create a person, and who may only add a role.

**Elsewhere.** A bank's customer hub, which every product system reads; a country's population register, which agencies ask instead of keeping their own.

**The enabler.** The person published as a data product with an owner, a contract and a version, readable by other systems; served to an app where it needs an answer at once (the serving product checked when scripting); a search call at the point of entry.

**Why it's common.** Galway keeps a returning student's whole history under their first number, asks applicants to say if they studied there before, and lists what goes wrong when the numbers aren't merged in time: the fees record, email, course materials, the ID card, the library ([notice](https://www.ugal.ie/media/registrationoffice/files/Merging-numbers.pdf)). The treaty: [Universal Postal Union](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Universal_Postal_Union).

**On screen.** Four small diagrams side by side; one lights, and lines run from the person's record to each system; at the application, a search runs before the form is saved.

**Labs (candidates).** *Which way fits?* *Create a person, or add a role?*

### 8. A hub at work

**In a line.** A master data hub on the platform does the work of films 2 to 7 every day, with LakeFusion as the example: rules act, AI suggests, people decide, and everything can be explained and undone.

**Opening.** None: it opens on the hub's screen, on a Monday morning, with one line recalling *Who is it?* and *Which value wins*.

**Story.** Mei's Monday. LakeFusion is the example: a master data management product that runs inside Databricks, using Unity Catalog's permissions and lineage, with AI-assisted matching and stewardship workflows; another hub, or a small one the university builds itself, would show the same things. Over the weekend 1,240 changes arrived from four systems: 1,180 linked under the published rules, 52 stayed apart, and 8 wait in her inbox, ranked by how many systems they touch. The first is a new returning student with two numbers, like Linh last year. Mei sees the weights bar by bar, what would flip the decision, which systems would hear of it, and a case note drafted by AI that only suggests. She links them; the decision waits a minute in an undo tray, then commits, and the change is published for the student app and HR to read. Three alike reviews are decided together after she checks a sample of them one by one. On Friday a share of the week's decisions goes to another steward, blind; if they disagree too often, automatic linking stops until the data owner restarts it.

**Ideas.** Reconciliation rules (matching, survivorship, source policies) published, versioned and dry-run before they change anything. The new agentic helpers: AI that matches by meaning as well as by spelling, drafts a case note for each review with personal values masked, explains a score, proposes a rule change from the stewards' decisions, and answers "why is this value here?"; it suggests, deterministic rules act, and people decide. What makes a hub trustworthy: every match and every value explained; every decision undoable (undo, detach, unmerge, a compensating change); checks on the checkers (blind review, a breaker); maker and checker for big changes; personal values masked by role, and every reveal logged; changes published as a feed for other systems, not pushed into them. The same features to ask of any tool.

**The example.** One review end to end: score 74, the weights, the AI's note with personal values masked, the link, the tray's countdown, the commit, the student app updated, the source shown on the new value.

**The enabler.** The hub, and a five-point checklist for choosing any hub: can it explain, undo, review, mask and publish?

**Why it's common.** LakeFusion runs inside Databricks: it matches by vectors and a language model, sends low-confidence matches to people, and explains each match through Unity Catalog's lineage and Delta Lake's history ([Databricks partner blog](https://community.databricks.com/t5/technical-blog/partner-blog-how-lakefusion-uses-ai-to-implement-mdm-natively-on/ba-p/123523), July 2025). Before scripting, its own documentation is checked feature by feature, and the film shows nothing it can't back.

**On screen.** The inbox; the waterfall, bar by bar; the tray's countdown; the master record with a source label on every value; a line from the hub to each listening system.

**Labs (candidates).** *Link, review or keep apart?* *Read the waterfall.* *What should the AI be allowed to do?*

### 9. Kept, not cleaned

**In a line.** A clean-up decays and a check at entry lasts; the steward's measures show which one the university has.

**Opening.** China, from 256 BC to today: Dujiangyan still waters the Chengdu plain because it has been repaired every dry season, by a six-character rule: dig the riverbed deep, keep the weir low. Stone horses buried in the riverbed once marked how deep to dig.

**Story.** Last year a clean-up project merged 2,000 duplicate person records. A year later there are 1,100 new ones, because every intake makes more: graduates coming back, people applying through two routes. With the search at entry from *One person, many systems*, new duplicates fall and stay down. Mei's office watches a few measures: duplicates made per intake, how long the queue's oldest case has waited, roles past their end date (a tutor whose term contract ended, still active), values with no source, and requests answered on time. When a government list adds a value, an agent drafts the mapping as a pull request, and its owner approves it. Yusuf's scorecard, from the governance series, shows the same measures.

**The example.** Two lines over two years: after the clean-up, duplicates climb back intake by intake; after the check at entry, they stay under 50 an intake. (Invented numbers, kept plausible.)

**Ideas.** Master data is a process, not a project. Prevention at entry; stewardship measures (queue age, duplicates made per intake, roles past their end date, values without a source, requests answered on time); agents that propose with evidence, and stewards who decide. The hand-back to governance: owners, rules and change.

**Elsewhere.** Any industry: the clean-up repeated every three years, and the one check at entry that ends it.

**The enabler.** Measures read from the queue, the role table, the tests and the reference tables, on a dashboard; an alert when the oldest case passes its limit; checks at entry.

**Why it's common.** Returning students come in through more than one door, such as Galway's undergraduate and postgraduate application systems, each giving a new number ([notice](https://www.ugal.ie/media/registrationoffice/files/Merging-numbers.pdf)). Dujiangyan's yearly repairs and their rule: [Sichuan Museum](https://artsandculture.google.com/story/8AVRiJGumXdeJg), [ADB](https://rksi.adb.org/publications/applying-ancient-wisdom-to-disaster-risk-mitigation/); built about 256 BC, still in use, World Heritage since 2000 ([Dujiangyan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dujiangyan)).

**On screen.** Two lines over two years: after a clean-up, duplicates climb back; after the check at entry, they stay down.

**Labs (candidates).** *Clean-up or prevention?* *Read the steward's measures.*

## Overlap with the other series

- ***What makes it the same one*** resolves a learner's identity for one question; *Who is it?* makes it a standing service for the person, and recaps key sets in a line.

- ***Both at once*** serves data live to an app; *One person, many systems* serves the person that way.

- ***Who it serves, and how it pays*** and ***Who does it, and where meaning changes*** (*The map before the data*) show one word, "customer", meaning a person to one domain and a connection point to another; this series models the person under both.
- **The governance series** gives the decision rights (*Who decides*), the protection (*Who may see it*), the products (*A product with an owner*) and the change control (*Change without surprises*) this series uses.

## The promise

Every line passes two tests: anyone who has waited for a number nobody answered for can follow it, and an experienced data product owner finds nothing wrong in it. The film opens in Istanbul, at the market that grew round the bedesten. With nearly eight hundred shops, a buyer can lose hours searching, pay for poor goods, or be sold short, given less than they paid for; and when no one answers for it, buyers stop coming, and the traders lose them. The market's order answered all three: in time each trade had its own street, its guild and rules for its goods, and a master who answered for what his stall sold, so buyers knew where to find a thing, what it would be, and who stood behind it, and came back. Data has the same problem, and the same answer. A data product is data with a promise (what it's for, what's in it, service levels, where to ask, a version and a status) and an owner who keeps the promise. The owner of a product answers for the product; the owner of a meaning decides what a word means; they can be different people. Not every table is a product: most tables are steps. Each product knows who uses it, from the record of who uses what, and has a life, from draft to retired. Owning a product is a job someone has to want, with a team behind them. And as order kept the bazaar's buyers coming back, governance lets people find data, trust it, and build on it. **Own the product, not only the meaning.**

## The story in one paragraph

In 1461, Sultan Mehmed II has a stone hall built in Istanbul for the city's most precious goods, the bedesten. Shops spread into the streets around it; by the 1520s, about 782 stand outside its walls. With so many, a buyer can lose hours searching, pay for poor goods, or be sold short, given less than they paid for, and when no one answers for it, buyers stop coming and the traders lose them: a market this big needs order. In time, each trade has its own street, named after it, and each has its guild, with rules for what its goods must be; the guild's officers check the goods, and so does the market inspector; a master who sells short answers for it. Today, Yusuf takes down the policy that says data is managed as an asset, with clear accountability. Anil, an analyst in the Faculty of Health, tells him about census day last March: Planning's count of learners near an award wasn't ready at seven, when the faculty needed it to plan next semester's places, and everyone he asked pointed to someone else. Until someone answers for it, it can happen again. Yusuf sits down with Noor and Clara, and they write the table's promise on a card, pinned in Planning's room and listed in the university's catalogue: what it's for, its owner (Clara, for Planning), what's in it, its service levels (built every night, ready by 7 a.m. on census day), where to ask (Planning's data desk), a version and a status. Clara answers for the product; what *award* means is still Mei's to decide. Clara didn't ask for the job, so Yusuf shows her who depends on it, what it's worth to them, and what comes with it: Jun builds it, Noor designs it, Sam runs it, and any change to it needs her yes. Of the data team's 33 tables, ten are used by other teams, and each belongs to one of six products; every change is checked, like goods in the bazaar, and a change that leaves one out goes back: no product, no owner. Each product knows who uses it, from the record of who uses what, and has a life: the first edition of the registrar's credentials is deprecated, has no users, and is retired at the end of March 2027. Now Anil finds Planning's product in the catalogue, with its promise, its owner and where to ask, like a buyer in the bazaar, and if it's ever late again, Clara answers for it. Order kept the bazaar's buyers coming back, and governance does the same for data: people find it, trust it, and build on it. Next, he makes a list of his own, for the faculty's advisers, and it will need an owner too.

## The look

*Watercolour*, the series' look, as in *Off the shelf*.

- **Pencil and wash.** Every place is painted on paper: a pencil line, paint gathering in the paper's grain, soft light. People are drawn the way the master data films draw them (`films/master-and-reference-data/shared/src/people.js`, bundled as it is), with a soft wash of colour behind each one.
- **The past, painted the same way.** Istanbul in 1461, the 1520s and the 1600s, with the place and year in the corner, painted in the series' own watercolour, not in the style of the period's own paintings: the bedesten's stone and brick, its rows of small lead domes and iron-clad doors; open streets of wooden shops under awnings (the streets weren't roofed until about 1700); the people in their own time's dress, with a warm umber wash behind them. Shop signs are the film's tags, in English, as in *Off the shelf*; no script or ornament is borrowed.
- **Only the university's places, today.** Yusuf's office and its shelf, with the newest DMBOK on his desk as the series draws it, a faculty's office in the old building (Anil's), the floor of offices behind glass, Planning's room, the registrar's office, the data team's room, the learning team's office (Tom's), Noor's room, and close-ups of their desks, boards and screens. All are the series' shared places (`shared/src/rooms.js`), painted once.
- **No files on screen.** As in all of pack 1, the screens are the university's own papers: the product card, the catalogue's plain page, the data team's register of tables, change requests, the record of who uses what, a notebook. No code, settings files, check output or tool screens, and no tool is named; the files behind these papers are *Products in open formats*'s.
- **Colour carries meaning.** Gold is governance's decisions, and the frame of a product; green is what runs in the work; grey is what can go; red is what nobody owns yet. Behind a person, gold is the business side and cyan the technical side.
- **Fewer diagrams, more situations.** Ideas are shown as people doing things in places: pointing round the floor, filling in a card at a table, signing a change request, a line of users behind a stall. The one diagram is the four steps of a product's life, pinned on the data team's board.

## Practice behind the film

What practitioners say about owning data products, and where the film shows it:

- **Name an owner who can say yes and no.** The owner decides what the product promises and what changes; the team builds it. A product owner doesn't need to write the code (chapter 5).
- **Few promises, kept.** Start with the service levels you can measure and keep, such as when it's ready; add more when consumers need them (chapter 3).
- **Not everything is a product.** A table becomes a product when people outside the team rely on it; the rest are steps, and stay private (chapter 6).
- **Know who uses it.** Read it from the record of who uses what (the lineage), not from memory or a mailing list, so a change reaches every one of them (chapter 7).
- **Retire on purpose.** A product, or an edition of one, that nobody uses is a cost. Deprecate it with a date, ask its users to move, and retire it (chapter 8).
- **Owning is a job.** The owner needs time, a clear scope and support, or the name on the card is only a name (chapter 5; *Who decides* says the same of owners of meaning).

## Rigour to check before recording

- **The bedesten, 1461.** Ü. Serdar Serdaroğlu, *İstanbul'un Bedestenleri* (İstanbul Tarihi, vol. 6, 2015), citing Halil İnalcık, "İstanbul'un İncisi: Bedesten" (1997), pp. 120–121: the Cevahir (Eski, İç) Bedesten "was built in 1461 by the firman of Fatih Sultan Mehmed", to strengthen the city's economy after the conquest; bedestens could be founded only by a sultan's firman or a vizier's order. Goods sold in bedestens: jewels, gold, valuable fabrics and silks, weapons, shawls and furs, carpets. The Cevahir held many rented cupboards and safes for gold, silver, money and valuable papers, and served merchants as a bank. Bedestens were stone buildings with domes, iron doors on four sides (Coşkun Çakır and Yakup Akkuş, *Osmanlı İstanbul'unda Ticaret*, vol. 6); "high walls, few windows and sturdy iron-clad doors" (Serdaroğlu); the Cevahir has 15 domes. Çakır and Akkuş say the bedestens were built to give income to the Ayasofya foundation; the film doesn't say it.
- **Shops spread into the streets.** From their construction, shops and hans were built round the bedestens, part of them endowed by Mehmed II, and the growth spread into the nearby streets (Çakır and Akkuş). Each bedesten had four gates, and today's covered market was formed by the shops along the streets that open from them (Serdaroğlu, n. 14).
- **About 782 shops.** In the early period, 124 shops inside the Eski Bedesten and 72 outside; by the 1520s, 126 inside and "a figure like 782" outside (Serdaroğlu, n. 10, citing İnalcık, pp. 125–126). The film says "nearly eight hundred" and shows "about 782". The early period isn't dated, so the film shows only the 1520s figure.
- **Each trade in its own street, "in time".** Traders of the same goods were gathered along one street, which took its name from their trade, for security against theft, fire and unrest (Çelik Gülersoy, *Story of the Grand Bazaar*, 1980, pp. 23 and 49, as cited by Wikipedia's *Grand Bazaar, Istanbul*); at the beginning of the 17th century the bazaar had 67 streets, "each bearing the name of the sellers of a particular good" (Robert Mantran, 1998 Italian edition, p. 177, as cited there). The hans round the bedestens generally bore the name of the guild they housed (Çakır and Akkuş). Neither source dates the naming to the 1400s, so the film says "in time" and turns the corner to the 1600s. The three tags are streets of the bazaar named after trades: Kuyumcular (goldsmiths, also a gate of the bedesten), Kalpakçılar (cap makers: a *kalpak* is a fur cap; today it's the jewellers' street) and Yorgancılar (quilt makers). The film draws generic streets, not a map. *Gülersoy and Mantran to be read in the original before recording, if they can be found.*
- **Open streets.** The streets were not yet covered in the time of Evliya Çelebi (about 1638); parts between the two bedestens were vaulted after a law against fires of 1696, and the bazaar was rebuilt in stone and brick and covered after the fire of about 1700 (Wolfgang Müller-Wiener, 1977, p. 346; Semavi Eyice, 1955, p. 27; as cited by Wikipedia). So the film shows open streets under awnings, and calls it a market, not a covered one. The bedesten was built from 1455/56 and finished in the winter of 1460/61 (İnalcık and Quataert, 1994, p. 14, as cited there), which agrees with Serdaroğlu's 1461.
- **Guilds and their rules.** Istanbul had "a very broad network" of guilds, organised in branches, each with its own kethüda, whose duties included inspecting the goods produced or sold; the yiğitbaşı and the elders carried out inspection within the guild; the kadı confirmed the kethüda's appointment (Arif Bilgin, *Osmanlı Klasik Döneminde İstanbul'un İktisadi Yönetimi*, vol. 3). "Rules for what its goods must be": the muhtesib's duties included making sure products were made "at the prescribed quality", and his men inspected whether goods made in the shops met the requirements (Bilgin). Written guild regulations survive from later; the film doesn't show a written rule.
- **The market inspector.** The muhtesib worked under the kadı of Istanbul; he set and enforced narh prices, checked weights and measures, and prevented fraud in the markets (Bilgin; Çakır and Akkuş). One of the first things after the conquest was inspecting the guilds and applying narh (Çakır and Akkuş).
- **"Sells short".** Istanbul's muhtesibs exposed a craftsman who cheated the public, making him wear a wooden collar (*tahta külah*) through the streets, and sometimes beat him, asking "Eksik satar mısın?", "Do you sell short?" (Bilgin). The film shows the seller facing the inspector, not the punishment.
- **The buyer's problem.** The film says it as the risk any market that size carries ("a buyer can"), not as a dated event: with nearly eight hundred shops, a buyer can lose hours searching, pay for poor goods, or be sold short, and when no one answers for it, buyers stop coming and the traders lose them. "Sold short" is the sources' own charge, the muhtesib's "Eksik satar mısın?", "Do you sell short?" (Bilgin), and the narration says what it means: given less than they paid for. The answers are the sources': traders of one good were gathered along one street named after it (Gülersoy; Mantran), the guild's officers and the market inspector checked the goods, weights and measures and prevented fraud, and a seller who sold short was shamed in public (Bilgin; Çakır and Akkuş). The sources give the authorities' own reasons for that order: traders "were forcibly concentrated along one road", and the market was gathered in one place mainly "to provide the highest security against theft, fire and uprising" (Gülersoy, pp. 23 and 49, as cited by Wikipedia). So the film doesn't say the streets were made for buyers, or that there was a time without guilds or inspection (inspecting the guilds was among the first things done after the conquest, Çakır and Akkuş): "a market this big needs order" is said of any market that size, and the order lets a buyer know where to find a thing, what it will be, and who stands behind it.

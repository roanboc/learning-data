# From words to data · What's in a word: script

*The script of What's in a word, in [From words to data](../README.md), as filmed: 5:46, in ten chapters, in English. The narration lives in [`source/src/narration.js`](source/src/narration.js) and the pauses in [`source/src/breath.js`](source/src/breath.js); this page and those files say the same thing, and where they differ, the source wins.*

## The promise

Anyone from 14 up understands it, and a data modeller, a linguist and a cognitive scientist agree with it. It uses no technology at all. Before you can count anything, you have to agree what it is: minds sort the world into kinds before they have words, words point at those kinds through ideas, every word hides a grain and has fuzzy edges, and meanings drift. A business that writes its words down, with an owner and a date, has its first data model.

## The story in one paragraph

In graduation week, the Deputy Vice-Chancellor wants one number for the speech: how many credentials did we award? Four offices give four numbers, 7,420, 10,600, 14,650 and 26,900, and none of them is wrong. To see why, the film goes back before computers, writing and words: pigeons and bees sort the world into kinds without a word; vervet monkeys' alarm calls stand for kinds of hunter, and dolphins, elephants and marmosets use calls like names; a baby points before it talks; a single cell in the brain responds to a photo, a drawing and a written name of one person; a philosopher's "gavagai" shows that every word hides a grain; a robin is a better bird than a penguin, and every category has fuzzy edges; words drift, and credential comes from the Latin for belief. Back at the university, with no system in sight, the four offices agree on paper what a credential is, give each word an owner and a date, and add a box to the sketch: version three, draft. The answer for the speech is 11,890, and each of the four numbers now has a name.

### 1 · Four answers · 0:00–0:44

**Narration.** It's graduation week, and the Deputy Vice-Chancellor wants one number for the speech: how many credentials did we award this year? Four offices answer, with four numbers. The registrar says 7,420. Short courses says 10,600. Careers says 14,650. The learning platform says 26,900. Nobody is wrong. Each office is counting a different idea, under the same word. To see why, we have to go back. Before computers, before writing, before words.

### 2 · Kinds without words · 0:44–1:14

**Narration.** In the 1960s, researchers showed pigeons hundreds of photos. The birds learned to peck at the ones with people in them, even photos they had never seen. Honeybees can learn the idea of same and different, and use it on colours and patterns they've never met. No words at all. Yet each of them sorts the world into kinds. Concepts come first. Words come later, to point at them.

**Picture.** A grid of landscape photos; a pigeon pecks the ones with people (green rings). A bee flies from a sample card to the matching card, first a colour, then stripes. A crossed-out bubble, "no words"; the photos sort themselves into two rings; a glowing concept above each group, then a word tag drops onto it.

**On screen.** 1964 · pigeons and photos · people? peck · the sample · same · no words · with people · without · a concept · person · same · different

### 3 · Calls that point · 1:14–1:54

**Narration.** Vervet monkeys give different alarm calls for different hunters. A leopard call sends the group up into the trees. An eagle call makes them look up, and dive into the bushes. A snake call has them stand tall and search the grass. Each call stands for a kind of thing, and everyone who hears it knows what to do. Some animals go further. Dolphins, elephants and marmosets use calls that work like names, for one individual. A kind of thing, and one particular thing: categories, and identifiers. Every data model needs both.

**Picture.** A grassland at dusk, an umbrella-thorn acacia and a bush. Each hunter arrives with its call's rings (a stalking leopard, a martial eagle gliding in, a snake in the grass); the monkeys climb into the acacia, dive into the bush or stand tall, each in its own time. Three call cards list each call and its escape, bracketed "each call: a kind of thing". Then a dolphin, an elephant and a marmoset, each with a unique line: its name. Two cards: a kind of thing (category) and one particular thing (identifier).

**On screen.** vervet monkeys · alarm calls · leopard call → up into the trees · eagle call → look up, into the bushes · snake call → stand tall, search the grass · each call: a kind of thing · a call like a name · category · identifier · every data model needs both

### 4 · What words add · 1:54–2:28

**Narration.** A baby points before it can talk, to share what it's looking at with you. That shared attention is the root of every word: this one, here, that. Human words go further than any call. They combine without limit, and they reach things that aren't here: yesterday, a promise, a rule. Even what someone knows. You can't see it. So people have always needed words, and later records, to show it. When did speech begin? Nobody knows exactly. Words leave no fossils.

**Picture.** On a grassland at dusk, a baby in a cloth wrap points at a robin on a branch; her grandmother, an early human in a hide wrap, kneels and looks where the baby looks; dotted lines of shared attention; "this one", "here", "that". Word tiles regroup into phrases. Mei with a dashed cloud, "what she knows (you can't see it)", then a diploma sealed beside her: "a record shows it". Layers of rock with fossil shells, and an empty speech-shaped hole with a question mark.

**On screen.** shared attention · this one · here · that · words reach what isn't here · what she knows · a record shows it · words leave no fossils

### 5 · One idea, many forms · 2:28–2:58

**Narration.** Inside the human brain, researchers have found single cells that respond to one person: to photos of them, a drawing of them, even their written name. Many forms, one idea. Think of it as a triangle. A word points to an idea in someone's mind, and the idea points to things in the world. The word never touches the thing directly. That gap is where misunderstandings live.

**Picture.** A brain with one cell; a photo, a drawing and the written name of Mei each send a pulse to the same cell. The triangle: "Mei Tanaka" (word), the idea of Mei, Mei herself; the dashed base between word and thing.

**On screen.** 2005 · concept cells · a photo · a drawing · her written name · many forms, one idea · word · the idea of Mei · Mei herself · the word never touches the thing · misunderstandings live in the gap

### 6 · Which part do you mean? · 2:58–3:21

**Narration.** A philosopher imagined a stranger pointing at a running rabbit, and saying: gavagai! Does it mean the rabbit? Its ears? This moment of running? Children solve this every day. They guess the whole thing first. But every word hides a choice: what counts as one. Data modellers call it the grain.

**Picture.** A rabbit runs across a field; a stranger's bare arm points at it; "gavagai!". Three outlines and three option cards: the rabbit, its ears, this moment of running. A child's choice ticks the whole rabbit. A card: the grain, what counts as one? 1 row = 1 rabbit? 1 ear? 1 moment?

**On screen.** 1960 · a philosopher's puzzle · gavagai! · the rabbit · its ears · this moment of running · children: the whole thing · the grain · what counts as one?

### 7 · Fuzzy edges · 3:21–3:52

**Narration.** Ask people to picture a bird, and most will think of a robin or a sparrow. Hardly anyone thinks of a penguin. Categories have typical members in the middle, and fuzzy edges. We agree about the middle. We argue at the edges. Is a degree a credential? Of course. A microcredential? Probably. A badge for turning up to a workshop? That's where the four offices part ways.

**Picture.** A cloud of birds: robin and sparrow in the middle, pigeon and eagle further out, penguin and ostrich at a fuzzy ring. The birds become credentials; each office draws its own circle around them, from the registrar's small one to the learning platform's wide one, with its number beside it. A wordless moment on the four circles.

**On screen.** bird · typical · fuzzy edge · agree in the middle · argue at the edges · credential · degree · diploma · microcredential · badge · assessed · badge · turned up · certificate of completion · Registrar · Short courses · Careers · Learning platform · four boundaries, one word

### 8 · Words drift · 3:52–4:19

**Narration.** Words move, too. Nice once meant foolish. Credential comes from the Latin for belief. Enrol meant writing a name on a roll. A diploma was a sheet of paper, folded in two. Ambassadors still present their credentials: letters asking their host to trust the person who carries them. Meaning moves slowly, and a word's history often explains its edges.

**Picture.** Ribbons from old forms to today's words: foolish → nice; credere → credential; en rolle → enrol, with a roll of names; diplōma → diploma, with a sheet folding. Two hands pass a sealed letter of credence. A line of years with "nice" moving from foolish to pleasant, and "credential → microcredential" near today.

**On screen.** nice, in the 1300s · then: foolish · now: pleasant · credere · to believe · en rolle · on the roll · diplōma · folded in two · today · letters of credence · credential: the letter that asks for trust · credential → microcredential

### 9 · Agreed on paper · 4:19–5:10

**Narration.** Back at the university, there's no system in sight. Just four offices, and a sheet of paper. First, what is a credential? A trusted statement, that others can check, that someone has shown what they know or can do. Awards and microcredentials are credentials. So are badges, when the learning was assessed. A badge for turning up isn't one. The recipe is Aristotle's: name the kind of thing, then say what sets it apart. Each word gets an owner and a date, and the sketch gains a box, in pencil. Version three, draft. The answer for the speech is 11,890. And each of the four numbers now has a name. None was wrong: they answered four different questions.

**Picture.** A sheet on a board, Mei and Tom beside it, the four offices as tags. The definition is written in pencil; the kind (statement) and what sets it apart are underlined. Credential and its kinds (award, microcredential, badge when assessed) appear as pencil boxes; a certificate of attendance is crossed out. Owners and a date; a stamp: sketch v3 · draft. The answer card counts to 11,890, and each office's number gets its name.

**On screen.** credential: a trusted statement, that others can check, that someone has shown what they know or can do · the kind of thing · what sets it apart · Credential · Award · Microcredential · Badge, when assessed · Certificate of attendance · shows you were there, not what you learned · owner: Registrar · owner: Short courses · agreed 28 Sep · sketch v3 · draft · credentials awarded 11,890 · 7,420 awards + 3,180 microcredentials + 1,290 assessed badges

### 10 · Pull back · 5:10–5:46

**Narration.** None of this needed a computer. Contracts open with their definitions. Mergers stall on what counts as a customer. Funding depends on who is counted. Agreeing on words costs a meeting. Disagreeing costs far more. About five thousand years ago, people began pressing marks into clay, to keep records that outlast memory. From minds, to marks, to systems. But before you can count anything, you have to agree what it is.

**Picture.** A contract opening with "1. Definitions"; two companies each with their own "customer", a merger on hold; funding bars. "Agreeing: a meeting" beside "disagreeing: disputes, rework, wrong decisions". A pillow-shaped clay tablet, marked row by row by a scribe's hand with a reed stylus. Minds → marks → systems. End card.

**On screen.** AGREEMENT · 1. Definitions · merger: on hold · funding · depends on who is counted · agreeing: a meeting · disagreeing: disputes, rework, wrong decisions · c. 3300 BCE · Uruk · minds · marks · systems · Before you can count anything, you have to agree what it is.

## Rigour sheet

| Chapter | What the film says | What an expert would add, or what the picture simplifies |
|---|---|---|
| Four answers | Four offices, four numbers, one word. | The university, offices and numbers are fictional. Real gaps between reports usually have several causes at once: definitions, dates and timing. |
| Kinds without words | Pigeons learned to peck at photos with people in them, including new ones (Herrnstein and Loveland, 1964); honeybees learn "same" and "different" and transfer them to new stimuli (Giurfa and others, 2001). | Whether pigeons form a concept of "person" or learn many features is debated; the film says only that they sort pictures into kinds. The bees' experiments used a delayed matching-to-sample task in a maze. |
| Calls that point | Vervet monkeys' leopard, eagle and snake calls produce different escapes (Seyfarth, Cheney and Marler, 1980). Dolphins (King and Janik, 2013), African elephants (Pardo and others, 2024) and common marmosets (Oren and others, 2024) use name-like calls. | Dolphins' signature whistles are copied to address others; the elephant study found calls that address individuals without imitating them. How close these are to human names is still debated. |
| What words add | Babies point before they talk; human language combines without limit and refers to things not present; nobody knows exactly when speech began. | Pointing usually appears around the first birthday, near the first words. "Displacement" and "productivity" are among Hockett's design features of language (1960). Estimates for the origin of speech vary widely; the film makes no claim. The picture sets the pointing in the deep past, a baby and her grandmother drawn as early humans (anatomically modern, in hide and cloth wraps), because shared attention is older than words; the scene is imagined, not a reconstruction of a site or a people. |
| One idea, many forms | Single cells in the human medial temporal lobe respond selectively to one person across photos, drawings and written names (Quian Quiroga and others, 2005). | The recordings were made in patients with epilepsy who had electrodes implanted for treatment; a cell responds to a concept among a handful, not to one concept only. The triangle is Ogden and Richards' (1923). |
| Which part do you mean? | Quine's "gavagai" (Word and Object, 1960); children tend to take a new word to name a whole object (the whole-object assumption, Markman). | Quine's point was about the indeterminacy of translation; the film uses it for the grain of a word. |
| Fuzzy edges | People rate robins and sparrows as typical birds, penguins as atypical (Rosch, 1975). | Prototype theory is one of several accounts of categories; typicality also varies by culture (the ratings were American). |
| Words drift | "Nice" once meant foolish; credential from Medieval Latin credentialis, from credere, to believe; enrol from Anglo-French enroller, to write on a roll; diploma from Greek diplōma, a folded paper; ambassadors present letters of credence. | Word histories follow the Oxford English Dictionary's and standard etymologies; "nice" passed through several meanings (shy, precise) before "pleasant". |
| Agreed on paper | A definition names the broader kind, then what sets it apart. | This is the classical definition by genus and differentia, recommended for terminology work by ISO 704. The definition of credential is the university's, not a standard's. The count, 7,420 + 3,180 + 1,290 = 11,890, is fictional. |
| Pull back | People began pressing marks into clay about five thousand years ago. | Proto-cuneiform tablets from Uruk date from about 3300 to 3000 BCE; most record accounts, and many of the rest are lists of words grouped by kind (Nissen, Damerow and Englund, *Archaic Bookkeeping*, 1993). The tablet's marks follow proto-cuneiform numerals, pressed with round and angled styluses rather than wedges, which came later; the cylinder-seal rolling along its edge is a common Uruk motif. |

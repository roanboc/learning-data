## The promise

Every line passes two tests: anyone who has met two teams using one word for different things can follow it, and an experienced data or enterprise architect finds nothing wrong in it. "Information architecture" means three things: a website's menus and labels (a real discipline, and not this one), a model of an enterprise's information and how it flows, and a phase of TOGAF's method. The team writes down which one it means: data architecture, as DAMA defines it, which uses data and information as one word. TOGAF and ArchiMate don't give it a layer of its own: TOGAF groups data with the applications, and ArchiMate puts what the business means in its business layer and how a system records it in its application layer, with nothing in between. Data architecture works there, and it's an architecture of its own because data outlives the applications that hold it, one concept serves many applications, and meaning belongs to the business while storage belongs to the systems: someone has to own the join. One word, connection point, shows it: defined once by the business, recorded six ways by six systems, and joined to none of them until Inés draws each line and gives it an owner. **Draw what the business means first; then every record has a line to it.**

## The story in one paragraph

In 1971, Chile's new government took over factories across the country, and its development agency, CORFO, had to run them. Its figures on what they made took about six months to collect, so a problem was half a year old by the time it showed; the people in charge needed to see each factory now. CORFO asked Stafford Beer, a British expert on how organisations steer themselves, to help, and their project, Cybersyn, would link the factories to Santiago. Before any computer was used, engineers went to the factories, walked each one with the people who worked there and drew how it worked; only then did they choose what to measure, about five numbers a factory, such as how much it produced and how many workers were absent, each with its place in the drawing. Inés Duarte, the utility's new data architect, finds the same problem: plenty of data and no agreed picture of what it means. She starts on Tomás's map, whose fourth layer, information, is hers. In her first meeting, three people say they work on information architecture and mean a website's menus, an analyst's model of the company's information and a phase of TOGAF's method; she writes down what the team will mean, data architecture as DAMA defines it. DAMA uses data and information as one; TOGAF groups data with the applications; ArchiMate has nothing between the business and the application layer, and Tomás's map draws a layer there. Then one word: connection point, where a home or a business meets the network, defined once by the business and owned by Grace, recorded by six systems under six names. In January a storm cut power to 3,400 connection points, outage texts were matched from the outage system's locations to customer service's addresses, and 180 households never got one, because the two systems wrote their addresses differently. Nobody owned the line between the word and its records. That line is what data architecture is for: data outlives the applications, one concept serves many, and meaning and storage belong to different people, so someone has to own the join. Inés draws each record's line to the word and decides that every system that needs a connection point carries the network's ID for it; outage texts are matched on that ID, and in the next storm all 2,100 households on the list got their text. On the data practice on the map, the business layer holds the concept and the information layer its records, each line with an owner. In Chile the drawing came first, so a number meant the same thing to everyone who read it; Inés drew the business first too.

## The reference, and practice

The series follows DAMA-DMBOK2's Data Architecture, with Data Modelling and Design, Metadata, and Data Integration and Interoperability as its neighbours. This film names three other bodies of knowledge once each, for what they say about where data architecture sits: TOGAF 10 (data architecture is one of its four domains, grouped with the applications in Phase C, Information Systems Architectures), ArchiMate (a business object in the business layer, a data object in the application layer, and nothing in between) and the web's information architecture (Wurman, 1976; Rosenfeld and Morville, 1998), named as a real discipline and left out. Zachman's framework (1987) and Information Engineering stand behind the analyst's meaning, a model of the enterprise's information and its flows. Where the film differs from a framework, it says so in its own words: the map draws a layer between the business and the applications, as many architects do, and data architecture is taught as lines with owners, kept on the map, not as a model drawn once.

What practitioners would tell a newcomer, and the film shows: start from one word that hurts, not from an enterprise data model of everything; write down which meaning of a word the team uses before any work starts; find every system that records the concept, under whatever name it uses; give the join an owner, not only the concept and each system; and carry one identifier, issued where the concept is born, in every system that needs it, so nobody matches records on what people type.

## Pause and think

The film stops at the end of three chapters, with one question each ([`site/assets/between-the-business-and-the-systems/think.en.js`](../../../site/assets/between-the-business-and-the-systems/think.en.js), Spanish in `think.es.js`):

- **After *One discipline*:** why DAMA uses data and information as one word. Right: data is a form of information and information a form of data, so splitting them makes two disciplines for one job. Near miss: information is data with meaning, so data architecture keeps the data and leaves the meaning to the business (the old distinction, which DAMA knows and doesn't split the discipline by).
- **After *One word, six records*:** who should have owned what left 180 households without a text. Right: the line between the word and its records, the join between what the business means and how each system records it.

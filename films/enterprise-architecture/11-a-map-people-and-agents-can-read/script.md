## The promise

Every line passes two tests: anyone who has kept a map, a model or a glossary that went stale can follow it, and an experienced enterprise or data architect finds nothing wrong in it. A map lives while people use it, keep it true and can pass it on. The more it's used, the more it's at risk: kept in slides and in a tool only one person edits, with its applications typed in by hand from another system, its copies drift, and a stale map is worse than none, because people trust it. So it moves into an enterprise architecture tool that everyone can reach, with a database and a graph. Much of what it holds is already kept somewhere else: strategy in documents, goals and initiatives in the project management tool, applications and servers in the IT service management tool. The tool brings each one in with its source's ID and keeps it in step; the rest (capabilities, rules, decisions and the links between everything) is written in the map itself, and the business glossary fits too, each term a business object defined once by the business. Keeping a map true is a data integration job as well as a modelling one. Each element is kept once, with a stable ID that everything else points to, a name, a definition, an owner, its relationships named as ArchiMate names them, its evidence with a date, and whether its owner has confirmed it; decisions are kept too, each saying why. A small map could keep all this as text files in a repository, the simplest approach; with many sources and many people in their roles, a utility needs the tool, and the ideas are the same. Every version is kept; a change is proposed as a draft and approved by the owner of that part, and checks run on the map itself. Every view is generated from it, one way, down to dbt's YAML and Databricks' catalogue, so nobody edits a view. Then an agent can read it: given a request, a change or a column, it finds the decision, the owners or the rule, and cites IDs and evidence so a person can check. The agent is on the map too, with its rights written down: it reads and drafts, and never confirms; people confirm. The opening story carries the idea, and says so: Mau Piailug's star compass lived in his memory, few still held what he knew, and he chose to teach it beyond his own island; fifty years on, Hōkūleʻa is still sailing, navigated by people he taught and people they taught. The film names its test after him, "the navigator's test": could someone else sail by it? **A map stays alive while people use it, keep it true, and can pass it on.**

## The story in one paragraph

In May 1976, the double-hulled canoe Hōkūleʻa leaves Maui for Tahiti, more than four thousand kilometres south, with no compass and no charts. Its navigator, Mau Piailug, from Satawal in Micronesia, finds his way by the stars, the swells and the birds; his star compass is in his memory, the points on the horizon where the stars rise and set. After thirty-one days the crew sights Mataiva, and three days later, in Papeʻete, more than seventeen thousand people are waiting. Few still held that knowledge, and it passed only from navigator to apprentice; Mau chose to teach it beyond his own island, so it wouldn't be lost, and a young Hawaiian, Nainoa Thompson, learned it from him and taught others. Any map lives only while people use it, keep it true and teach it to the next navigator: could someone else sail by it? Today, Tomás's last note asks how to keep his map alive. It's used every week now, for the skipper's test, to give rules a home and to answer the minister, and that's when a map goes stale: it lives in slides and in a modelling tool only Tomás edits, its applications were typed in by hand from the IT service management tool and many have changed since, its copies drift in other people's decks, and the glossary and the catalogue keep their own words. A stale map is worse than none, because people trust it; if Tomás left tomorrow, nobody could sail by it. So he moves it into an enterprise architecture tool that everyone can reach, with a database and a graph. Much of what it holds is already kept somewhere else: strategy in documents, goals and initiatives in the project management tool, applications and servers in the IT service management tool. The tool brings each one in with its source's ID and keeps it in step, so nobody retypes it; the rest is written in the map itself, capabilities, rules, decisions and the links between everything, and even the business glossary fits, each term a business object defined once by the business. Keeping a map true is a data integration job, as well as a modelling one. Each element is kept once, with a stable ID, a name, a definition, an owner, its relationships, and his two rules from day one, its evidence with a date and whether its owner has confirmed it; decisions are kept too. A small map could keep all this as text files in a repository, the simplest approach; a utility, with many sources and many people in their roles, needs the tool, and the ideas are the same. Every version of the map is kept, with who changed what and when; a change is proposed as a draft, and the owner of each part it touches approves it, while checks run on the map itself and find, every time, that generating power still has no owner. Everything people read is generated from the map, one way: the ArchiMate views, the glossary, the conceptual model, the ontology, the tests and tracking tables, and the heat map, and, where data people work, each dbt model's owner, domain and rule IDs, and each Databricks table's owner, tags and description; a fix goes to the map, and every view follows. Then an agent can read it. Given a request, it applies the skipper's test; given a change, it walks the layers and lists the owners who must agree; given a column in the catalogue, it finds the rule behind it, with its owner and its reason; and every answer cites IDs and evidence, so a person can check it. The agent is on the map itself, software filling a role, with its rights written down: it may read everything and draft changes, it may not confirm one, and where two sources disagree it asks the owner; its owner is Tomás. When the regulator changes the retail code, the agent finds the one rule whose reason is that clause, retail's half of day one's rule, drafts its new words and lists what they touch, the billing team's tracking table and two views; the head of retail confirms it, and from the next morning the tracking table shows each bill not corrected within ten working days. Next spring, Zofia starts as a data engineer: a badge, a page of acronyms and a list of systems, as Tomás had, and this time a map. She asks the agent who owns the readings data and which rules it keeps, gets Grace and two rules, each with its reason and a link, and by the afternoon has drafted her first change, for Grace to confirm. In 2007, on Satawal, Mau made five Hawaiians master navigators, Nainoa among them; he died in 2010, and what he taught lives on: fifty years after that first voyage, Hōkūleʻa is still sailing. Grace confirms Zofia's change, the map passes the navigator's test, and Tomás's last note has its answer; the series' eleven films fill the map, layer by layer, and its meaning goes on to *From words to data*.

## Rigour to check before recording

- **Hōkūleʻa, 1976.** The canoe left Honolua Bay, on Maui, on 1 May 1976, made landfall at Mataiva, in the Tuamotus, on 1 June, after thirty-one days at sea, and reached Papeʻete Harbor on 4 June, where more than 17,000 people gathered to welcome it (the Polynesian Voyaging Society's announcement of the voyage's fiftieth anniversary, on hokulea.com; the Hawaii Tribune-Herald, 4 May 2026). Accounts that give thirty-three or thirty-four days count to Papeʻete. Mau Piailug navigated with no instruments; Kawika Kapahulehua was the captain, and fifteen sailed from Hawaiʻi, among them Ben Finney and David Lewis, so the picture's few silhouettes are a few of them. The voyage tested whether Polynesians could have settled the Pacific on purpose, by navigation, rather than by drifting. Honolua Bay to Papeʻete is about 4,350 km on a great circle, so the voice says "more than four thousand kilometres"; the canoe sailed further. Mau went home after Tahiti, and the film doesn't show the voyage back. Checked on 17 and 18 October 2026.
- **The canoe and the sea.** Hōkūleʻa, launched on 8 March 1975, is a double-hulled voyaging canoe about 19 metres long, with two masts and crab-claw sails. It's drawn from that description and the Society's photographs, as a silhouette at night or at dawn, so no colours are claimed, and nothing is drawn from Pacific art. Wayfinders read birds that fish at sea by day and fly home to land at dusk: on the thirtieth day, the crew saw white fairy terns, the trade-wind swell faltered, and they made landfall on Mataiva the next day (Christina Thompson's *Sea People: The Puzzle of Polynesia*, excerpted in Popular Science, 13 March 2019). The swells, the stars and the birds on screen are drawn, not a record of the voyage's nights.
- **The star compass.** Carolinian navigators hold a star compass in memory: points on the horizon named for where particular stars rise and set, with the pole star in the north and the Southern Cross upright in the south, thirty-two points named by fifteen stars, and unevenly spaced, because the stars are (Ward Goodenough, *Native Astronomy in the Central Carolines*, 1953; Thomas Gladwin, *East Is a Big Bird*, 1970). Mau learned it from his grandfather. The ring in chapter 1 puts its points where fifteen bright stars rise and set at Satawal's latitude, about 7° north, computed from their declinations (`D11_DEC` in `src/plan.js`); their names aren't shown, and accounts differ on which stars the compass uses. The ring in chapter 11 is the Hawaiian star compass, which Nainoa Thompson developed with Mau's help: thirty-two equal houses of 11.25°.
- **Knowledge passed on.** Navigators taught within families and schools of navigation, and were reluctant to share what they knew with outsiders. Mau was chosen as an apprentice by his grandfather when he was about five, and made a master navigator at a pwo ceremony, between about 1947 and 1952 by different accounts. He feared that navigation would die in his own culture, as it had in Hawaiʻi, and from April 1975 he worked with the Polynesian Voyaging Society. Nainoa Thompson learned from him, and from Will Kyselka at the Bishop Museum planetarium, and navigated Hōkūleʻa to Tahiti and back in 1980, with Mau aboard as an observer on the way out (the Wikipedia articles on Mau Piailug and Hōkūleʻa, which cite the Society's records). The six islands lit in the central Carolines (Satawal, Puluwat, Pulap, Pulusuk, Lamotrek and Woleai), their lights and the four passing to an apprentice are illustrative, not a count; the chart is sketched from longitude and latitude, with no coastlines.
- **2007 and 2010.** On Satawal, on 17 March 2007 (18 March in some accounts), Mau initiated sixteen of his students as pwo, master navigators, the first pwo for navigators there in 56 years: five Hawaiians (Nainoa Thompson, Shorty Bertelmann, Chad Paishon, Chad Baybayan and Bruce Blankenfeld) and eleven men of Satawal, among them his son, Sesario Sewralur. It was the first time the title was given to anyone from outside Micronesia. Mau died on Satawal on 12 July 2010, aged 78 (the Society's "Hōkūleʻa Moʻolelo: The Pwo Ceremony", 17 March 2017; the Honolulu Star-Bulletin, 17 and 18 March 2007).
- **Fifty years on.** Hōkūleʻa is on the Moananuiākea Voyage around the Pacific, launched in June 2023. In June 2025, Nainoa Thompson captained it from Hilo to Tahiti, while its sister canoe, Hikianalia, carried navigation training led by Bruce Blankenfeld (Spectrum News Hawaiʻi, 4 June 2025); the canoes reached Aotearoa in November 2025, and the plan of July 2026 sails them to Fiji and back between September and November 2026 (Kauai Now, 30 July 2026). "Navigated by people Mau taught, and by people they taught" sums up how the Society trains its navigators; the film names none of them, and its dawn shot is drawn, not a particular leg. Checked on 18 October 2026.
- **The navigator's test** is the film's own name for its test, as the skipper's test is *Now the questions appear*'s. "A map stays alive while people use it, keep it true, and can pass it on" is the film's own summary, built on the series' insights 9 (every claim traces to evidence, and every decision records why) and 12 (a model is true when the people who own it confirm it).
- **An architecture description.** ISO/IEC/IEEE 42010:2022 describes one as stakeholders and their concerns, viewpoints and the views that follow them, model kinds, and the correspondences between them. "Each one answers the questions of the people who read it" is a view addressing its readers' concerns; the film doesn't name the standard. TOGAF's architecture repository is the idea behind keeping it all in one place.
- **The map's tool, and its sources.** Enterprise architecture tools keep the map in a repository that many people reach, each in their role, and keep it in step with the systems that already hold its records, matched on each source's ID; examples checked on 20 October 2026: OrbusInfinity, from Orbus Software, whose ServiceNow integration imports applications and servers from the CMDB and keeps objects and relationships in sync; SAP LeanIX; Ardoq; Bizzdesign Horizzon; Sparx Systems Enterprise Architect; and Avolution ABACUS. The film names none of them, and its window is drawn as no product in particular.

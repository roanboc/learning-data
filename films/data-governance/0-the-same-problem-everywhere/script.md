# Data governance · The same problem, everywhere: script

*The script of the prologue to the first data governance series,* Governance principles*, which comes before* Off the shelf*, as made: 4:58, in eight chapters, in English, painted in watercolour, 23 October 2026. Made to read on a phone: on-screen text is at least 28 px in the frame, nothing sits under the captions, and no caption runs to three lines.*

## The promise

Every line passes two tests: a newcomer can follow it, and a data governance lead, a bank's risk officer and a privacy lawyer each find nothing wrong in it. The film tells the real story behind the series. Organisations that share data meet the same problems, decade after decade. The people who looked after data pooled what worked into a body of knowledge, the DMBOK, with governance at the centre, because the other areas only hold together when someone has the authority to decide. Leaders and regulators reached the same place. And the body of knowledge keeps growing, with ethics and with AI. The film says outright why it still matters: the question it answers never changed. **Who decides about this data, and who answers for it?**

## The story in one paragraph

Autumn 2008: a bank's board asks how much it stands to lose if another bank fails. The answer is in every business line, each with its own systems and its own names for the same customers, and by Monday nobody can add it up. Many banks couldn't. It wasn't new: from the 1970s, companies shared data between departments, which raised new questions about who owned it and who could see it. A new job grew up to look after it, and in 1980, in Los Angeles, the people doing that job started meeting to share what they'd learned. In 2002, a law in the United States made chief executives sign for their figures. In 2009, their association, DAMA, published the DMBOK, whose wheel puts governance at the centre. After the crisis, the banks' regulators made governance the first of their principles; ten years later, only two of thirty-one of the world's biggest banks met them all. In 2017 the DMBOK gave more room to ethics, and in 2018 a European law made organisations answer for personal data. In 2024 a revision added governance and ethics for AI, and Europe's AI law asked for governance of the data AI learns from; a third edition, a living body of knowledge, is planned for 2027. The book keeps growing, but governance stays at the centre. And at a university, a new data governance lead is about to meet the same question.

## The look

*Watercolour,* the series' look, as in *Off the shelf*.

- **The place and year in the corner** of every scene from the past, as *Off the shelf*'s operating room has its year: "2008 · a bank", "Los Angeles · 1980", "United States · 2002", "Basel · 2013".
- **Other organisations' rooms, on purpose,** because the point is that the problem is everywhere: a bank's risk floor at night, a 1970s office floor, a meeting room in Los Angeles, a boardroom, the regulators' table, an office in Europe, a team training a model. No company is named and no logo appears. The film ends in the university, where *Off the shelf* begins.
- **People invented, and dressed for their time:** wide collars, earth tones and big glasses in the 1970s and 1980; dark suits in 2002 and 2013; shirtsleeves and lanyards late at night in 2008; smart and casual from 2017 on.
- **The thread on a shelf.** An empty shelf takes the first edition in 2009; the second joins it in 2017 with a tab for ethics; the revision of 2024 with a tab for AI, and a draft marked "3.0" leans beside them. At the end, the newest copy is on Yusuf's desk.
- **Colour carries meaning,** as in the series: gold is governance's decisions, green what holds, red what nobody can trust.
- **The wheel painted our own way,** as in *Off the shelf*. DAMA shares its own images under a licence that allows no changes, so the film doesn't copy them.
- **Fewer diagrams, more situations.** The wheel and the list of principles are the only diagrams; everything else is people in places, asking questions.

### 1 · One question · 0:00–0:47

**Narration.** Autumn 2008. As the financial crisis spreads, a bank's board asks its risk team one question: if another bank fails, how much do we stand to lose? The answer is somewhere in the bank, in every business line, each with its own systems and its own names for the same customers. By Monday, nobody can add it up. It wasn't just one bank. The regulators later found that many banks couldn't add up their risks quickly or accurately. The numbers were all there. Nobody could put them together, or say which to trust.

Someone from the board's office crosses the floor with a card and hands it to the head of risk at her desk; her team gathers round. Close on the card: "From the board · How much do we stand to lose?". The camera pulls back over her desk as printouts arrive one by one from the business lines, each tagged, "treasury", "markets", "lending", "abroad", and on each the same customer under its own code, "client 0042", "CPTY-A17", "acct 9-310", "K-77/2"; a pencil rings each code and joins the four, with a question mark at each join, "the same customer?". On the whiteboard, "exposure, in millions", a figure for each line, "abroad" still a red "?", and the total, a red "?"; beside it, "same client?" and the four codes. On "By Monday", the desk calendar turns, Friday, Saturday, Sunday, Monday, and the total is still "?". On "It wasn't just one bank", the bank's tower at night, one floor lit, and the camera pulls back across the city: window after window lights late in the other towers. ### 2 · Not a new problem · 0:47–1:30

**Narration.** It wasn't a new problem, either. In the 1970s, companies began sharing data between departments. That raised new questions: who owns this data, and who may see it? A new job grew up to look after it: data administration, with a dictionary of what each piece of data meant. In 1980, in Los Angeles, the people doing that job started meeting, to share what they'd learned. Their association, DAMA, soon had chapters in city after city. Every organisation had been learning the same lessons alone. Now they could learn them together.

### 4 · Written down · 1:50–2:31

**Narration.** In 2009, DAMA published what its members had learned, as a body of knowledge for data management: the DMBOK. It draws the work as a wheel: architecture, modelling, security, quality, metadata, and more. At the centre, like a hub, is governance. The other areas only fit together, and stay in balance, when someone has the authority to decide. Other guides cover the same ground, or part of it. This series takes what they agree on, and shows it at work.

### 5 · Governance first · 2:31–3:09

## Rigour

What each chapter says, and what it rests on. Sources are numbered as in the list below.

- **1 · One question.** The regulators' own account of 2008: "Many banks lacked the ability to aggregate risk exposures and identify concentrations quickly and accurately at the bank group level, across business lines and between legal entities" (9, paragraph 1). "Its own names for the same customers" rests on the remedy they set: "single identifiers and/or unified naming conventions for data including legal entities, counterparties, customers and accounts" (9, paragraph 33). The board's question, the weekend and the four codes are a composite, as believable as the record allows: no bank is named, and "another bank" stays unnamed.
- **2 · Not a new problem.** In the 1970s, database software brought "the managerial challenges of creating a shared and consistent database to be used across different departments"; sharing "raises political problems: Who owns the data? What form should the data be stored in? Who should be allowed to access the data?"; and it brought "new roles such as the database administrator, and new concepts such as the data dictionary" (5). The two questions on screen are our plain wording of the first and the third. The job had a name by 1980: DAMA began as the Data Administration Management Association, in Los Angeles in 1980, with early chapters in other cities (1). "Learning the same lessons alone" is DAMA's own reason for its chapters: "Data management can be a lonely discipline… That is exactly why DAMA International chapters exist" (2). The dictionary entry and the flip chart are ours.
- **3 · Someone signs.** Section 302 of the Sarbanes–Oxley Act, 30 July 2002: the principal executive and financial officers certify each annual or quarterly report, and are "responsible for establishing and maintaining internal controls" (8). The certificate's sentence is our plain version of 302(a)(3). The scandals stay unnamed.
- **4 · Written down.** The DMBOK, first published in 2009 (1). Its wheel puts governance at the centre "since governance is required for consistency within and balance between the functions" (3), and it defines data governance as "the exercise of authority and control (planning, monitoring, and enforcement) over the management of data assets" (4): "the authority to decide" is our plain wording of both. The wheel's areas are DMBOK2's eleven, painted our own way; the shot carries no year, because the first edition's wheel was drawn differently. "Like a hub" is our image. DCAM, ISO 8000 and ISO/IEC 38505 are named only, as they stand (15): DCAM is the EDM Association's (named the EDM Council until 1 July 2025), now DCAM v3 (2025), for members; ISO 8000 is the series of standards on data quality (Part 1, Overview, 2022, current); ISO/IEC 38505-1:2026, the second edition (August 2026), replaced the 2017 edition.
- **5 · Governance first.** Principle 1 of the banks' principles is governance, and paragraph 26 calls a strong governance framework, with the risk data architecture and IT infrastructure, "preconditions to ensure compliance with the other Principles" (9). Ownership: "Roles and responsibilities should be established as they relate to the ownership and quality of risk data" (9, paragraph 34, under Principle 2, in the part the regulators call overarching governance and infrastructure). The progress report of November 2023: "Of the 31 banks assessed, only two banks are fully compliant with all the Principles", and the programmes "were often underfunded, limited in scope and lacking sufficient attention from boards of directors (boards) and senior management"; it also names fragmented IT, legacy systems and manual processes, which is why the film says "more than systems", not "not systems" (10). The 31 are global systemically important banks: "the world's biggest banks" is our plain wording. The principles listed on screen are the first six of the eleven for banks.
- **6 · Should we?** The second edition's "Expanded data ethics coverage" (2); the second edition came out in 2017 (1). "Not only whether we can, but whether we should" is our plain wording of what data ethics asks (6). The GDPR's accountability, "be able to demonstrate compliance" (11, Article 5(2)), applied from 25 May 2018 (Article 99(2)); the customer's letter is the right of access (Article 15). The proposal on the wall is ours.
- **7 · Machines that learn.** The 2024 revision: "AI Governance and Ethics: Ethical considerations and governance practices for AI have been integrated" (7). Europe's AI Act, in force since 1 August 2024: training, validation and testing data "shall be subject to data governance and management practices appropriate for the intended purpose of the high-risk AI system", including "data collection processes and the origin of data" (12, Article 10(2)); the team's two questions, said aloud and in the bubbles, are our plain wording of those. The film gives no date for when the rules for high-risk systems apply, which changed in 2026. The third edition: "Add and maintain sections on emerging topics such as AI governance, data ethics, sustainability, and data literacy", forming "a living body of knowledge" (13); publication "targeted for 2027" (2).
- **8 · Still at the centre.** The question is the series' own, and each era above asked it. Yusuf, the university, its 34 policies and binder 12 are *Off the shelf*'s.

**Checked since, and still to check.** The AI Act's Article 10(2), whose opening the page shows, was checked on 23 October 2026 against the European Commission's AI Act Service Desk (12). DMBOK2's lines on the wheel (3), the definition (4) and the ethics chapter (6) still come from secondary copies: check them against the book.

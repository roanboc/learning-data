## The promise

Every line passes two tests: someone new to data work can follow it, and a data manager, a data architect or an analyst who has taken a hundred requests finds nothing wrong in it. As the series' opening film, it says the series' promise once: these three films are about working with the people who ask for data. Its own idea: a request usually names someone's guess at a solution, and listening finds the decision behind it. Ask what they'll decide with it, by when, who else decides, what they already trust and what would change their mind; listen more than you talk; say back what you heard; then agree the question in writing, beside the request's own words. The team then answers the question the decision turns on, not only the thing first asked for.

**Takeaway:** "Ask what it's for, and agree it in writing."

## The story in one paragraph

Pisa, 1990: the marble bell tower, begun in 1173, has leaned since it was being built, because the ground under it is soft; its top hangs about four and a half metres out past its base, and the lean grows a little every year. In March 1989 a medieval tower in Pavia collapsed without warning and killed four people; in January 1990 Pisa's tower is closed to visitors, for fear it could fall too. Its stone is fragile, and any work is a risk: the slightest disturbance to the ground on its leaning side could bring it down. That spring, Italy's government appoints an international committee of engineers, architects and experts in old monuments. Before choosing how, they agree what saving the tower would mean: about half a degree back, too little for anyone to see, but enough to ease the strain on its stone and steady the ground beneath it. Their method is to draw a little soil from under the high, north side, so the tower settles back towards it; they try it first on a test footing, while lead weights on the north side hold the tower still. From February 1999 they do it under the tower itself, a little at a time, and every day the engineer in charge signs a note: how the tower moved, and exactly what to take out next. By the summer of 2001 its top has come back about 45 centimetres, to the lean of the early 1800s; it reopens in December 2001, still leaning, and its engineers expect at least two hundred years before it leans as far again. At the university, a year from now, on Thursday 11 February 2027, Graham Ellis, new as Director of Facilities, emails the data team: "Could I have a dashboard of room use, by Easter?" Helena Novak, the data manager, asks him for half an hour, with Noor, the team's data architect. On Tuesday 16 February they ask a few open questions, then mostly listen: he will decide whether to ask the University Council for a new teaching building, forty rooms, about A$140 million; his paper is due on 1 June and the University Council decides on 24 June, so Easter was a guess at a safe date; the academics say the rooms are always full, and he has seen empty corridors on Monday mornings and Friday afternoons; short at every hour, he'll ask to build, short only at some hours, perhaps not. Near the end, Helena says back what she heard, and Graham stops her at one word: by "used", he means people in the room. A booking isn't use, so the data is the timetable's bookings beside the head counts from each room's sensor; in Pisa, saving had meant half a degree, not a straight tower. Noor writes the question down with him on one page, "Do we have too few rooms, or too few at some hours?", with the decision, the dates, who decides, what would change his mind and what "used" means under it, and his own words at the top; that afternoon he replies, "Yes, that's it." The request waits its turn, like every other, until the team takes it up on Tuesday 30 March, from the same page; by 23 April it has an answer: the rooms are booked 38% of the whole week, but 93% from 10:00 to 14:00, Tuesday to Thursday, and in one booked hour in five, nobody was there. Not too few rooms, but too few at some hours: the academics were right about the peak and Graham about Monday mornings, and the week's average had hidden both.

The test: someone who has never heard of the tower can say, from the narration alone, why it was a problem (a tower that leaned further every year, closed for fear it would fall on the people near it, where even the work to save it could bring it down) and what the fix gave people (the tower safe and open again, still leaning, for at least two hundred years). ## The reference, and practice

The series' reference for the soft-skills films is the classic books on the practice (the module's plan, 5 November 2026). This film names two once, Edgar Schein's *Humble Inquiry* and Carl Rogers and Richard Farson's *Active Listening*, and the five whys once, with care, and teaches what they share: ask what you don't already know, listen for the whole meaning, and say back what you heard.

**Where the film adopts, extends or differs.** It adopts Schein's humble inquiry as the attitude behind the questions (asking, not telling; interest in the other person) and Rogers and Farson's active listening for its second half (hearing the whole meaning and saying it back, so the person can correct it, as Graham corrects "used"). It extends both from practice into a written agreement: the question goes on one page with the decision, the dates, who decides, what would change his mind and what the key word means, beside the request's own words, so anyone can see how one became the other; neither book asks for a page. It differs from the common use of the five whys: the film doesn't teach the technique, which was made to find the cause of a fault on a production line; it says that asked bluntly of a person, "why?" can sound like doubt, and that "what will you decide with it?" reaches the same place kindly.

**Practice, from the module's plan.** A practitioner will recognise the request that names a dashboard and a date and nothing else; the deadline that was a guess at a safe date; the person who decides, who isn't the person who asks; the word everyone uses and nobody has defined ("use", "active", "customer"); the team that delivers exactly what was asked, on time, and helps nobody; and the request that waits its turn, as every request does, so that the agreed page, not anyone's memory of a meeting, is what the work starts from. The advice is the briefs': ask what they'll decide with it, by when, who else decides, what would change their mind; listen more than you talk; say back what you heard; agree the question in writing. The team's front door (the data management module's form, from June 2027) is named once, as a form that asks; listening hears what the answers mean.

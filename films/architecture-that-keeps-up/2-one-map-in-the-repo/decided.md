| 10 November 2026 | *One map, in the repo* opens in Great Britain, in the four beats: in the late 1990s Ordnance Survey's maps are digital but cut into tiles, where a building is only lines, with no name or number to point to; councils, water companies and the emergency services each match their own records to the lines in their own way, so one school can be one thing in a council's records and another in a water company's, nobody can be sure two records mean the same place, and a new road must be found and changed by each of them on its own; in 2001 Ordnance Survey launches OS MasterMap, one seamless database of the whole country, every feature (about 440 million at first) with its own identifier, a TOID, a version and a date, kept up to date by surveyors on the ground and from the air, with users sent only what changed; now a council and a water company point to the same building by its identifier, every change is dated, and by 2016 it holds over 500 million features. Its data story: one picture of a platform, kept in copies that drift apart. It comes back when Noor says "copies drift; keep one source", and at the close. The users' "matched each its own way" is a general reading of the pre-2001 situation, logged as an assumption. |

Written from the module's brief (`briefs.md`, section 8) and shared numbers (`numbers.md`, *Small, every week*, as this film's thread set it on 5 November 2026), on assumptions, as the author asked for the night ("Work on the films with assumptions and merge them all", 5 November, 11:53): every question that would have gone to him is in the module's shared files, `8-small-every-week/assumptions.md`, with the assumption taken. It follows the four-beat rule for every opening (AGENTS.md, *Story and world*). Every claim is checked against its source on the day (*Rigour*, *Sources*). The university and its people are the module's invented ones; Mexico's wheat programme is real, told from the National Academy of Sciences' memoir of Norman Borlaug, the University of Minnesota's Borlaug pages, the Rockefeller Archive Center and FAO.*

## The promise

Every line passes two tests: someone who has never thought about data quality can follow it, and a data quality practitioner, a quality engineer who has run plan, do, check, act on a factory floor, an analytics engineer who keeps a dbt project, and a historian of Mexico's wheat each find nothing wrong in it. The film shows the step the module's brief gives it: data's quality gets better a little every week, not in one big clean-up. A clean-up fixes the data once, and the next load brings the same errors back; so the people who use the data and the people who make it meet for fifteen minutes a week, ask what the results said, make one small change, and say how they'll know it worked. Each change is one turn of plan, do, check, act, measured with what the team keeps already. The order matters: measure, simplify, standardise, and only then automate, or you only do the wrong work faster. And the last step of every change is a check that keeps it, so the results are kept night after night, not only last night's. Takeaway: **One small change a week, measured, then kept.**

## The story in one paragraph

Mexico, 1945: a programme run by Mexico's government with the Rockefeller Foundation is working to grow more food, and on wheat a small team of young Mexican agronomists and a plant scientist from the United States, Norman Borlaug, fights stem rust, a fungus that covers wheat's stems in rust-red spots, weakens them and shrivels the grain, and can take a whole field. It had ruined harvest after harvest; farmers lost their crops, and Mexico had to buy much of its wheat abroad. A wheat that resists the rust can be bred, but slowly: one harvest a year is one generation a year, and finding the right wheat takes many. So the team grew two generations a year, in winter in the Yaqui Valley in the north-west, near the sea, and in summer in the highlands near Mexico City, more than two thousand metres up and over a thousand kilometres away, carrying the seed between them after each harvest; each round they looked at every plant, saw which ones the rust left standing, and kept the best. Small steps, twice a year, halved the time to a new wheat: by 1948 they had four new wheats that resisted the rust; farmers came to see, more than 800 on one day in 1954, and sowed the new seed; and by 1956 Mexico grew all the wheat it needed. At the university, in December 2025, 41 degree certificates had to be printed again from a wrong list. In January the first idea was a big project, to clean every name on the list once, before March; but a clean-up fixes the names once, and the next list brings the same errors back. So Mei, who answers for the list, asked for something smaller: every Thursday at ten, fifteen minutes, standing at the bench, with Malia, who runs the ceremonies, Jun, who builds the list, and Sam, who runs it every night, and three questions: what did this week's results say, what one small change will we make, and how will we know it worked. One turn of plan, do, check, act, from Thursday 1 October 2026: since September names are fixed where they're born and each night the checks hold the doubtful ones, so they plan to check by hand only the names a check holds or a graduate reports, and to know by two measures they keep already; from Monday 19 October they check 78 names by hand, not 2,140; the round takes 26 hours, not 240, and on 6 November 97 of the hundred records are perfect; they keep it, and write it into the round's steps. They measured the hand work first, simplified it, standardised it, and only then automated it. The last step of every change is a check that keeps it: the hand checks are kept by the hundred records, every month, at a level agreed with Malia; and because the scorecard shows only last night's checks, a last step of each night's run in dbt now keeps every result, which a dashboard on Databricks reads on Thursdays. Fifteen minutes is enough because the people who use the list and the people who make it stand at the same table, as Mexico's farmers came to the table too, buying land for a research station in 1955 and taxing their own wheat to add more. On Wednesday 9 December 2026, Mei reads Andrés Peñaloza's name as he says it; 3 certificates are printed again, not 41, and on Thursday 17 December the four look at the three and pick the next small change. Borlaug's team didn't stop in 1956 either: round after round its wheats got better, and by 1963 Mexico was selling wheat abroad.

## The reference and the practice

- **Plan, do, check, act.** The loop Walter Shewhart drew and W. Edwards Deming taught, from Shewhart's cycle of specification, production and inspection (1939) and Deming's lectures in Japan in 1950, where it came to be called plan, do, check, act; Deming himself preferred *study* to *check* (the Deming Institute). The film calls it by the name quality engineers use most, and says *check*, the word this site uses for a rule that runs. One turn is planned with how its result will be known before anything changes: the film's two measures are chosen in the plan.
- **Kaizen and Lean's order.** Small changes, often, by the people who do the work, rather than one big project; and the order of improvement practitioners teach: measure first, simplify, standardise, and only then automate (Taiichi Ohno's "where there is no standard, there can be no kaizen"; Michael Hammer, "Don't automate, obliterate", 1990). Named for what they teach, not as a method to adopt.
- **Data quality as a cycle.** DAMA-DMBOK's data quality chapter builds its improvement cycle on Shewhart's and Deming's plan, do, check, act; Thomas Redman's practice gives the film its measure people take by hand, the hundred records (named in *A hundred records*, not again here).
- **What practitioners do.** A short weekly meeting at a board of results, where those who use the data and those who make it agree one change, is the habit the module's brief asks for (the author's advice in the module's proposal: engagement first, then small, measured steps). A change isn't done until something keeps it: a check, a level agreed with the people who use the data, and a measure read again. Results kept over time, not only last night's, so a trend shows before it fails: that is what the example project adds.
- **Tools show, they don't lead.** dbt keeps every night's results with a hook that runs at the end of each run; Databricks shows them on a dashboard. The film says what each does, and names no feature's status.
- **Don't repeat.** *Rules that run* made the scorecard, and this film keeps it night after night: it names the scorecard and shows it, and links the film. *Teachers of data* is about how people come to care about data at all; this film takes that engagement for granted at its Thursday table, and links it.

### 3 · Plan, do, check, act

**Narration.** Quality engineers call it plan, do, check, act: a loop drawn by Walter Shewhart and taught by W. Edwards Deming. Borlaug's team ran it twice a year: sow, look, keep the best, sow again. Here is one turn of it, from Thursday 1 October 2026, before December's round of hand checks. Last December, two people checked every name by hand: 240 hours. Since September, names are fixed where they're born, and each night the checks hold the doubtful ones. So Malia asks: do we still need to check every name by hand? Plan: check by hand only the names a check holds or a graduate reports, and let the hundred records look at the rest. They'll know by two measures they keep already: the round's hours, and the perfect records in the hundred. Do: from Monday 19 October, the team checks 78 names by hand, not 2,140. Check: the round takes 26 hours, not 240. And on Friday 6 November, 97 of the hundred records are perfect. Act: they keep it, and write it into the round's steps.

## Pause and think

Four questions, one at the end of each of four chapters, as `site/assets/small-every-week/think.en.js` holds them (keys unquoted). The right answers sit in different places (third, first, second, third); every option has the same length and detail; one near miss in each takes the right answer's words and changes the one thing the film turns on. The series has no labs yet, so no question has a lab to stop at.

**`thursdays`** (end of *Fifteen minutes, every Thursday*). In January, the first idea was a big project: clean every name on the list, once, before March. Why did Mei ask for something smaller?

1. Her office had no time for a big project before March, so a smaller one was all it could fit in.
2. A clean-up takes too long, so the team would clean a few names by hand each Thursday instead. *(near: cleaning a few names each Thursday still fixes names, not how the list is made; the film's Thursdays change how the list is made)*
3. A clean-up fixes the names once, and the next list brings the same errors back. **(right)**

*Why:* Cleaning every name once leaves the list's errors where they're born, so next year's list arrives with the same ones. Cleaning a few names each Thursday would do the same, only slower. Mei asked for fifteen minutes a week to change how the list is made, one small change at a time, each one measured. Which of your fixes would the next load undo?

**`cycle`** (end of *Plan, do, check, act*). In October's turn, the team checked 78 names by hand, not 2,140. How did they know the change worked?

1. By two measures they kept already: the round's hours, and the perfect records in the hundred. **(right)**
2. By the round's hours alone: 26, not 240, and time saved is what the change was for. *(near: fewer hours could mean more names missed; only the hundred records beside them say the list stayed right)*
3. By asking Malia after the ceremony whether any graduate had complained about a name.

*Why:* Fewer hours could mean the team simply missed more names. So they planned two measures before they began, both kept already: the round's hours, which fell from 240 to 26, and the hundred records drawn at random, 97 of them perfect on 6 November. Only both together said the change worked. Which two measures would tell you your next change worked?

**`order`** (end of *Measure, simplify, standardise, automate*). Before automating, the team measured the hand checks, cut them to the names a check holds, and settled each the same way. What would automating first have done?

1. Made the old work faster: all 2,140 names checked, so fewer errors would reach the printer. *(near: the same work, faster, catches no more errors; most names never needed a check)*
2. Made the old work faster: all 2,140 names checked, though most of them never needed it. **(right)**
3. Nothing different: the order of the four steps stops mattering once the tool is chosen.

*Why:* Automating the hand checks first would have checked every name, faster, when most names needed no check at all: the wrong work, done faster, and no more right than before. Measuring showed the 320 hours; simplifying left only the names a check holds; standardising gave each one a single way to be settled. Then there was something worth automating. What would you automate today that you haven't simplified yet?

**`kept`** (end of *Kept with a check*). The hand checks now cover only the names a check holds. What keeps that change from slipping back?

1. Nothing needs to: once a change has worked, it stays, so the team can move on to the next.
2. The hundred records, drawn once in November, when 97 of the 100 were perfect. *(near: a measure taken once says how it was then; the film's keeper is the hundred records every month, with a level that acts)*
3. The hundred records, every month: below 95 perfect, the next round checks more by hand. **(right)**

*Why:* A change lasts only until someone forgets it, unless a check keeps it. A measure taken once, however good, says how it was in November, not how it is now. So the hundred records are drawn every month, at a level agreed with Malia: below 95 perfect, the next round checks more by hand. What keeps your last change in place?

## What the example project has

Added to the example dbt project (`films/analytics-engineering/project/`) on 10 November 2026, appended or replaced in place so no cited line moves; the page's *See it in the project* (`site.json` `repo`) links each file.

- **The last step of every run:** `on-run-end` in `dbt_project.yml` calls `keep_check_results(results)` (`macros/governance/keep_check_results.sql`), which adds a row for each check that ran to one table, `check_results`, in the target's own schema: when the run started, the run's id, the check, its status (pass, warn, fail, error or skipped), how many rows it held or failed, and its owner and dimension from the check's `meta`. Nothing is replaced: each night adds to the nights before. The project's own checks on itself (`tests/governance/`) aren't kept, as the scorecard doesn't score them; a run with no checks adds nothing, and nor does `dbt compile`, which runs the same last step with no results to keep.

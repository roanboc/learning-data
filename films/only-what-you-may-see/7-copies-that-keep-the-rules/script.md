## The promise

Every line passes two tests: anyone who has ever copied a table to work on it can follow it, and a privacy officer or a platform engineer finds nothing wrong in it. The protection on people's data lives where the data is read: in the catalog, as each column's class and the policies that hide each class. A copy made inside, read through those rules, keeps them. A copy that leaves takes the records and none of the rules: a clone, a share, a download, a file. So each copy the work needs is made in a way that keeps the protection, or it isn't made: made-up learners to build with, a table of totals to share, a clean room where code both sides approved runs on both sides' rows, and no way out for a download. **Every copy keeps the protection, or isn't made.**

## The story in one paragraph

France, 1948. Lascaux, a cave in the south-west with horses, bulls and deer painted on its walls about 21,000 years ago, opens to visitors, and by the late 1950s more than a thousand people a day come to see them. Their breath fills the cave's air with warmth, water and carbon dioxide; in 1960, with the heat and the lights, green algae spread over the walls and white crystals form on them. The visits that let people see the paintings are destroying them. In 1963 the minister for culture, André Malraux, closes the cave; on the same hill its two best-known galleries are rebuilt in concrete, down to every bump in the rock, and painters copy each painting onto them with natural pigments. Lascaux II opens in 1983, true to the cave and none of it the cave; about ten million people have seen the paintings there since, and the cave rests, entered by a few scientists for about 200 hours a year. At the university, the learners' records are protected where they're kept: *Know what you hold* gave each column a class, *Tags that travel* made each class a tag, and *One policy for many tables* hides each class wherever the records are read. But the rules live in the catalog, not in the records, so a copy that leaves takes the records and none of the rules. This autumn four copies are asked for. On Monday 19 October, Jun Park wants a copy of the learner table in development; Azure Databricks refuses a clone to anyone its policies cover, and one made by someone they leave out would hold every real name and no column's class, so, wherever they can, development and test get made-up learners, 2,614 of them from a script: synthetic data, easy to write now, and of the kind written without reading a record, which gives no one away. The other kind, learned from the real records, is more likely to give a real person away the closer it fits them, so the university treats it as a copy of them; how closely made-up data should follow the real records is the harder question, and still open; dbt's unit tests run on rows written for each test. On Monday 2 November, the teaching hospital where the faculty's learners train asks how many leave after first year, by course; agreement 2026-21 is signed, but the university's policies don't travel with an OpenSharing share, so the share holds only *Show the least*'s totals, and a job ends it on 30 June 2027. The hospital's harder question, whether more weeks on its wards help learners do better, needs both sides' rows, so they meet in a clean room, where neither sees the other's rows, the university approves the hospital's code on 1 December, and the code hides any count under 10. On Tuesday 10 November, Elena Lindgren, Anil's new research assistant, would like to download the study's table; in the studies' workspace every way out is switched off, kept as code, and she cleans the data where it is. Every copy keeps the protection, or isn't made.

## The reference, and practice

The module's reference holds (its proposal, *The reference*): DAMA-DMBOK's data security area for what is protected and why, and zero trust (NIST SP 800-207) for how. For this film's question, how a copy of people's data keeps its protection, the thread took these, named here once; the film names none of them and teaches what they agree on.

| Body of knowledge | What it adds | Where the film uses it |
|---|---|---|
| NIST SP 800-53 Rev. 5, SA-3(2) *Use of live or operational data* | Live data in development and test is approved, documented and controlled, and the place it's copied to is protected at the same level as the data | *Made up, for building*: the film goes further, and development holds no live record at all |
| ISO/IEC 27001:2022, Annex A 8.31 *Separation of development, test and production environments* and 8.33 *Test information* | Development, test and production kept apart; test information chosen, protected and managed | The same chapter: made-up learners for development and test, and production's records stay in production |
| The UK Information Commissioner's guidance on privacy-enhancing technologies (2023): synthetic data, trusted execution environments | Synthetic data as data "generated from real data using a model trained to reproduce its characteristics and structure": the more it mimics the real data, the more useful it is and the more likely to reveal someone's personal information; computing on data the people running it can't see | *Made up, for building* (the kind learned from the real records, which can give a real person away; the film's own made-up learners are the other kind, written without reading a record) and *A room for both* (the clean room) |
| The Office for National Statistics, *ONS methodology working paper series number 16: Synthetic data pilot* (15 January 2019) | A scale of six levels, by how closely synthetic data resembles the original, what it's for and how much it can disclose: two built only from metadata, for testing code, with no or minimal risk; four built from the real dataset, their risk from non-negligible to extremely high | *Made up, for building*: the two kinds kept apart, and how close to the real records as the question between them |
| Stadler, Oprisanu and Troncoso, *Synthetic Data – Anonymisation Groundhog Day* (USENIX Security Symposium, 2022) | Synthetic data drawn from a model of the real data "either does not prevent inference attacks or does not retain data utility", and which of the two is hard to predict | The same lines: data learned from the records isn't safe because each row is new |
| The Financial Conduct Authority, the Information Commissioner's Office and the Alan Turing Institute, *Exploring Synthetic Data Validation: privacy, utility and fidelity* (2023) | Three things to check in synthetic data: utility (is it useful for the task), fidelity (how like the real data it is) and privacy (can anyone be found in it); the more like the real data, the higher the risk of finding someone; and what is enough depends on the use | *Made up, for building*: "How closely it should follow the real records, gaps and odd cases included, is the harder question, and still an open one." |
| The Five Safes (Desai, Ritchie and Welpton, 2016), *safe outputs* | What leaves a secure setting is checked first, and small counts that could single someone out are held back | *Protected before it leaves* (the share holds only totals, small counts hidden) and *A room for both* (the approved code hides any count under 10) |
| NIST SP 800-53 Rev. 5, AC-4 *Information flow enforcement* | Where data may flow is enforced by the system, not left to each person | *No other way out*: every way to download or export switched off, and a network that reaches only the catalog's storage and a short list |

**Where the film differs, on purpose.** The standards allow live data in a test environment under controls; the film doesn't, because a copy holds the values and none of the catalog's rules, and a masked copy still lets through a column nobody has classed yet. And what the privacy guidance calls synthetic data can still be personal data when it's learned too closely from the real records: the film's made-up learners are written by a script from the columns and their classes, so none of them is anyone. The film first left that difference to this page; since the author's yes of 12 November 2026 it says it, one kind after the other: "This kind is written without reading a record", and "The other kind is learned from the real records, and the closer it fits them, the more likely it is to give a real person away." What the university does with the second kind, "treats that kind as a copy of the real records", is the film's own, careful reading: whether such data is anonymous "depends on whether the personal information on which you model the synthetic data can be inferred from the synthetic data itself" (the Information Commissioner's guidance), so until that is shown it gets the real records' classes, and their policies cover it. The film names no way of measuring the risk and no tool that lowers it. It still leaves one question open, as the author did on 12 November 2026: written without the records, made-up data gives no one away but may miss how the real records behave, their gaps and odd cases; learned from the records, it behaves like them and may give them away. The film says only that the question is harder and open, and covers the cases that matter with rows written for each test. **Where the story stops fitting:** Lascaux II follows the cave as closely as its builders could, which is what a copy of paintings is for; a stand-in for people's records must not follow them that closely. So the chapter keeps the two kinds apart before it comes back to Lascaux II, and that line claims only what holds for both: they stand in, and not one is real. The film doesn't teach the classes (*Know what you hold*), how a tag travels (*Tags that travel*), the ways to hide a value (*Show the least*), how a policy is written (*One policy for many tables*) or how a share is found and asked for (*The marketplace*).

**Practice.** What practitioners recognise: a "temporary" copy of production in development that's still there three years later; a masked copy where a new free-text column went through unmasked; a partner sent a spreadsheet by email because the share "had the masks on"; a share nobody remembered to end; results downloaded "just this once" and found on a laptop; a photo of a screen. And what works: made-up data written from the schema, with unit tests on rows written for each test; sharing only what may leave, already in its final form; an end date that a job carries out; joint questions answered in a clean room, by code the data's owner has read; every way out switched off and kept as code; and each person's confidentiality agreement for what no switch can stop.

**The author's practice** (3 November 2026): every contributor signs a confidentiality agreement and takes responsibility for the data they access, as part of governance. The film says it where no switch reaches: "No switch stops a photo of a screen; her confidentiality agreement covers that." And on development and test data (12 November 2026, asked whether it is always made up or a masked copy is sometimes fine): "Made up as possible, especially now that synthetic data generation is so easy. We might need to define how synthetic data can reflect the reality of production data, which is a harder question." The film says so in *Made up, for building*: "So, wherever they can, development and test get made-up learners", "That's synthetic data, and it's easy to write now", and the harder question, left open. The same day a pushback was put to him: "Synthetic data. Data made up from rules is safe. Data synthesised from production can still leak real people when it fits the source closely. I'd have the film separate the two." He answered: "Yes, do it." The chapter now says the two kinds one after the other and shows them one over the other. In the film the first kind is "written without reading a record", not "made up from rules", because the rules, in this film, are the catalog's classes and policies.

**The opening.**
- **Lascaux** (French Wikipedia, *Grotte de Lascaux*, and English Wikipedia, *Lascaux*, read 10 November 2026; the French Ministry of Culture's Lascaux site, archeologie.culture.gouv.fr/lascaux, read 5 and 10 November): found in September 1940 and opened to the public on 14 July 1948. Painted "autour de 21 000 ans cal AP", in the early Magdalenian (the ministry's site: "Il y a 21 000 ans"); older sources give 17,000 years, and the film follows the current dating. "More than a thousand people a day" by the late 1950s: "plus de 1 000 touristes par jour, dégageant environ 2 500 litres de dioxyde de carbone et 50 kg de vapeur d'eau" (French Wikipedia; English Wikipedia, "1,200 visitors per day"). In 1960 the "maladie verte": the visitors' carbon dioxide, too much heat and the lights spread algae over the walls; the carbon dioxide also lays "un voile de calcite" on the walls and some paintings, the "maladie blanche" (French Wikipedia). On 17 April 1963 André Malraux, the minister for cultural affairs, closes the cave to the public (French Wikipedia; said "the minister for culture").
- **Lascaux II:** the Hall of the Bulls and the Painted Gallery, rebuilt about 200 metres from the cave (English Wikipedia) as a double shell of concrete shaped from the national mapping agency's surveys, "the reliefs ... reconstructed down to the finest" detail (the ministry's *Lascaux II and III*); the paintings reproduced "avec des pigments naturels" by a team led by the painter Monique Peytral (French Wikipedia). Opened in 1983; "Since its opening also 10 millions visitors have come to the cave" (the ministry's *Lascaux II and III*), said as "about ten million". "Seuls quelques scientifiques sont autorisés à pénétrer dans la grotte et la présence humaine est limitée à 200 heures par an" (French Wikipedia, citing the cave's curator). **The tools** (Azure Databricks docs on Microsoft Learn, 10 November 2026; Databricks' own docs, 5 November).
- **Clones** (*Requirements, quotas, and limitations for row filter and column mask policies*): "Deep and shallow clones aren't supported on tables with ABAC policies. To clone a table, use a service principal or group that is listed in the EXCEPT clause of every policy that applies to the table." "Exempted principals see unfiltered, unmasked data." *Clone a table*: "Metadata not cloned are the table description, user-defined commit metadata, Delta Lake table history, and Unity Catalog properties, such as tags." So a clone by someone a policy covers is refused, and one by someone exempt holds every value and no column's class, so no policy matches it. On screen, "Refused: its policies cover Jun" is the film's words, not the product's message.
- **Tag propagation** (*Tag propagation*): a governed tag follows a column into a table made by CREATE TABLE AS SELECT, INSERT INTO, views, and CLONE (table-level tags only), on serverless compute. *Tags that travel* teaches it; this film says only that "a copy made inside, read through those rules, keeps them". - **Its two kinds** (read 12 November 2026). The Information Commissioner's Office, *Privacy-enhancing technologies*, the chapter *Synthetic data* (ico.org.uk; the page says it is under review after the Data (Use and Access) Act): synthetic data "is generated from real data using a model trained to reproduce its characteristics and structure"; "The more that the synthetic data mimics real data, the greater the utility it has, but it is also more likely to reveal someone's personal information"; whether it is anonymous "depends on whether the personal information on which you model the synthetic data can be inferred from the synthetic data itself". The Office for National Statistics, *ONS methodology working paper series number 16: Synthetic data pilot* (ons.gov.uk, 15 January 2019; Bates, Špakulová, Dove and Mealor), proposes "a high-level scale to evaluate the synthetic data based on how closely they resemble the original data, their purpose and disclosure risk": its first two levels are "constructed based only on available metadata", for code testing, with "no disclosure risk" (structural) and "minimal disclosure risk" (valid: even "the range of plausible values" can sometimes disclose, so it is judged case by case); the four above are "constructed based on the real dataset", from "non-negligible" to "extremely high disclosure risk"; and "if an aim is to preserve fine multivariate distributions, then whole records are likely to appear in the synthetic dataset unchanged (even if generated from a model)". Stadler, Oprisanu and Troncoso, *Synthetic Data – Anonymisation Groundhog Day* (31st USENIX Security Symposium, 2022; the abstract on usenix.org): "synthetic data either does not prevent inference attacks or does not retain data utility", and the trade-off "is hard to predict".

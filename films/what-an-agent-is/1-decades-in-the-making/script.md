# What an agent is · Decades in the making: script

*The script of *Decades in the making*, the opening film of *What an agent is*, the first series of the module *AI agents in data work*, in eight chapters, in English, drawn in the series' white lab. Made on 3 November 2026 from the module's plan, which the author settled that day and asked to be made without a script review, and from the rule of 28 October 2026 for every opening (scenario, problem, solution and benefits, each said outright; *Opening story* quotes them). Its history is real, with its sources and the day they were checked under *Rigour*; Jun's team, the agent and the remedy copied in the opening are invented. As the author asked on 3 November 2026, it isn't rendered here: its pull request merges once its checks pass, and the release renders the video. It runs 6:25.*

## The promise

Every line passes two tests: someone who has never used an AI model can follow it, and a researcher who lived through these decades finds nothing wrong in it. This term an AI agent joins Jun Park's data team, and to some it seems to have come from nowhere: some fear it, some expect magic. The film walks the road that made it, a chapter for each step, each on its year of an oak rail along the lab's wall: a program that learns from examples (1958), and the limit of a single layer (1969); learning from mistakes in many layers (1986); a much bigger network, a million labelled photos and two graphics chips (2012); attention, and the transformer (2017); models trained on text from the web and books (2018 to 2020); training on the answers people preferred (2022); and models that work step by step and use tools (since 2024). Then it says why it seemed sudden: computing power, data and money arrived together. **It wasn't a sudden discovery; it was long work that finally had the power to run.** No company and no model is named.

## The story in one paragraph

Europe, in the 1440s: every book is copied by hand, letter by letter, so books are slow to make and rare (in 1424, the University of Cambridge's library held 122), and every copy can add new mistakes. The idea that will change it is already four hundred years old: in China in the 1040s, Bi Sheng made a piece of baked clay for each character, set them in an iron frame, printed a page and used them again; in Korea in 1377, monks printed a book with characters cast in metal, the oldest that survives; and in Mainz, around 1450, Johannes Gutenberg brought the other pieces together, letters cast in metal, an ink that clings to them, a press and paper, with the money of a lender, Johann Fust. By 1500, presses in more than two hundred towns had printed some thirty thousand different books, cheaper, for far more readers: printing seemed to arrive all at once, and had taken four hundred years. This term, an AI agent joins Jun Park's data team; to some it's come from nowhere, but its ideas are decades old. The film follows them: the perceptron, shown to reporters in July 1958, which told cards marked on the left from cards marked on the right after fifty tries, and a newspaper's hopes for it; the limit of a single layer (1969); learning from mistakes, passed back through many layers (1986), and twenty years of small networks; 2012, when the pieces came together on a million labelled photos and two graphics chips; 2017's transformer, which reads all the words at once; models trained on the written web, fluent but not trying to help; 2022, a model trained on the answers people preferred; and, since 2024, models that work step by step and use tools, in a loop: an agent. Computing power, data and money arrived together; printing waited four hundred years for its pieces, and these ideas decades for theirs. So nobody on Jun's team expects magic from the agent, or needs to fear it: they know where it came from.

Colours mean what they mean in every film of the series: blue tools and chips, violet the model thinking, orange data coming back, green a check that passes, amber a mistake.

### 2 · A machine that learns · 1:52–2:34

**Narration.** Our road starts in July 1958. The US Navy showed reporters a perceptron, a program by the psychologist Frank Rosenblatt that learns from examples. Shown cards marked on the left or the right, it guessed, and each wrong guess nudged its numbers. After fifty tries, it could tell them apart by itself. A newspaper said the Navy expected it to walk, talk, and be conscious of its existence. But a single layer can't learn some patterns at all, as a book showed in 1969, and money moved elsewhere.

"Try 1": a card marked on the left; the eye lights, the lamp "Right" comes on, a wrong guess, and a dial turns; six tries one by one, then the rest in a rush, to "Try 50", right: "Tells left from right". On "a single layer", four dots, two dark and two light at opposite corners, and a straight line that turns but never splits them: "No straight line splits them". The rail moves on to 1969, and a book comes in, *Perceptrons*, 1969; coins roll away, and the machine goes dark.

### 3 · Learning from mistakes · 2:34–3:05

**Narration.** In 1986, three researchers showed how a network with several layers can learn. It makes a guess, and measures how wrong it is. The error is passed back, layer by layer, and every connection is nudged to make it smaller. Over many examples, the layers in between find patterns no one told them to look for. But computers were slow and examples few, and for twenty years these networks stayed small.

**Picture.** The rail on to 1986. The paper pinned to the wall, *Learning representations by back-propagating errors*, 1986. A network on a panel: three inputs, two layers of five in between, and two outputs, "Left" and "Right". A card is shown; the signal climbs the layers and lights "Right", wrongly; "How wrong", an amber bar; the error passes back, layer by layer, each connection nudged, and the bar grows shorter. "Examples" slide in, each marked left or right, and the outputs come right; the layers in between show the patterns they found, small grids, under "Layers in between". On "computers were slow", an old computer on a desk, its progress bar crawling; the rail moves on, a bracket under it from 1986: "Twenty years".

### 5 · Attention · 3:39–4:12

**Narration.** In 2017, a paper called Attention Is All You Need changed how these networks read. Before, they read a sentence one word at a time. Its design, the transformer, takes in all the words at once, and learns which matter to each other. In "The team fixed the report because it was wrong", it learns that "it" is the report. Reading all at once splits the work across many chips, so models could grow far bigger.

### 6 · The written web · 4:12–4:45

**Narration.** From 2018 to 2020, researchers trained transformers on huge collections of text from the web and books. The task was simple: guess the next word, check it, and adjust. Billions of times over, a model picked up grammar, facts and ways of writing. The largest, in 2020, had 175 billion numbers to tune. It wrote fluently, but it wasn't trying to help: it only carried on the text.

### 7 · Helpful, with hands · 4:45–5:24

**Narration.** In 2022, researchers asked people to rank a model's answers, from best to worst. Then they trained it to give the kind of answers people preferred. People preferred its answers to those of a model a hundred times bigger. Since 2024, models have also been trained to work through a problem step by step before they answer. And they can use tools: search, run a query, read a file, and work with what comes back. A model that reasons, with hands for tools, in a loop: that's an agent.

### 8 · Why it seemed sudden · 5:24–6:25

**Narration.** So why did it seem so sudden? Because three things arrived together. Computing power: since 2010, the computing used to train the largest models has doubled about every six months. Data: the web, and millions of labelled examples. And money: the first transformer cost about nine hundred dollars to train; by 2023, the biggest cost tens of millions or more. It wasn't a sudden discovery. It was long work that finally had the power to run. Printing waited four hundred years for metal letters, paper, a press and a lender. These ideas waited decades for chips, data and money. So nobody on Jun's team expects magic from the agent, or needs to fear it: they know where it came from.

## Sound

A new palette ([`source/tools/score.py`](source/tools/score.py)), checked on 3 November 2026 against every `score.py` on main and on the open branches, and against the series' other films (the series' palettes, kept in its project files): none uses either instrument. **A cittern**, the small wire-strung instrument of the sixteenth century, plucked with a quill near the bridge, its pairs of strings tuned exactly together so they can't beat; and **a serpent**, the long wooden bass horn of the same years, bent like a snake, one held note at a time, played softly with no vibrato. Both come from Europe soon after printing, the opening's own place and time, and both are made in the score. A major all through, every chord tuned pure from A at 110 Hz, and F sharp minor for the opening's problem; B is never held with D or F sharp, which aren't pure in this tuning, so the chords are A, D, E, F sharp minor and C sharp minor.

- **The road has its notes.** The cittern plays each year on the oak rail with its own note, rising with time: 1958 is A, 1969 C sharp, 1986 E, 2012 the A above, 2017 C sharp, 2020 E, 2022 F sharp, and 2024 the A at the top. At each chapter's turn the rail leaves one year and reaches the next, and a chord rolls up to the new year's note. The year it leaves isn't plucked: with it, every turn played two figures a second apart.
- **The serpent is the long work:** one low note held under each long stretch of time, the four hundred years of printing, the twenty years of small networks, and "long work"; and under the opening's problem.
- **The mark:** pieces plucked one at a time, far apart, then struck together as one chord where they come together: printing's pieces in Mainz, the three pieces in 2012, all the words at once, the agent, the three things that arrived together, and the end card.
- **Under the opening,** music all through: the home chord for the scribe, slowly, the serpent's A under it, and the quill; F sharp minor as books are slow and rare, the serpent falling as night falls over days of copying; D for Cambridge's library, the books set out; a note copied three times that drifts down on the copies; "Something has to change", the serpent holding C sharp, a question. Bi Sheng's clay, a note for each piece, and A major as the page comes off; Korea, C sharp minor, softly; Mainz, the five pieces plucked as each comes and struck together as Fust puts up the money, the serpent under "years of trying". By 1500, a note for each town as its press opens, scattered, then more and more; all at once, the three notes struck together, then apart again over four hundred years. The agent arrives on 2024's A; fear is the serpent's low F sharp, magic a bright fourth; the rail's years sound as their tabs drop, and the title comes on the mark.
- **After the opening,** music comes only at a chapter's turn, under a moment on screen, or in a wordless pause, never as a bed under the voice (the module's rule, 3 November 2026, after the author's of 20 October): the check that passes, E to A, when the perceptron tells the cards apart; the network's guess, climbing to a wrong G sharp, left unresolved until the layers find their patterns and the line rises to A; the contest; the words read one at a time, then struck together, and "it" and "report" an octave apart; guess, check and adjust; "it only carried on the text", a phrase that stops unresolved; each of the steps, and "that's an agent" struck together; the late years' notes climbing at "sudden", and the whole road in one run. After the author's review (4 November 2026), the newspaper's "magic", the error coming back down, the words the web taught and the second check that passes go without music, so it comes in once every 10.5 seconds after the opening, not every 9.8. `tools/quiet.py` checks it: after the opening, no chapter has music for more than 47% of its length, or for more than 8.8 seconds without a break; it sounds 33% of the time, comes in at most four times in any 30 seconds, and plays no figure again soon after itself; no two held notes beat; and nothing pulses, since no chapter is urgent.

The author heard glitches in the first mix (4 November 2026): pages riffling, coins pouring and the contest's misses were made of many tiny sounds, up to 124 beginning in one second, which crackled. Each is now one soft texture that swells in, or a few sounds, and no more than 11 effects begin in any second.

## Changed from the plan

- **The opening** is the candidate the brief offered, movable type, with Cambridge's 122 books to give the problem a number, and a copied remedy, invented, to show the harm a mistake could do.
- **1969 is added** to the road: the limit of a single layer explains why the money moved away after 1958, and why the twenty years of small networks matter.
- **2012's story is told as pieces coming together,** so it echoes Mainz: the ideas old, the data and power new.
- **Since 2024** pairs reasoning step by step with tools, and closes on the agent, a model that reasons, with hands, in a loop, which the next film, *A model, reasoning, hands and a loop*, opens up.

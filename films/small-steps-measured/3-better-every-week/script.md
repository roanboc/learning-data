# Small steps, measured · Better every week: script

*The script of *Better every week*, film 3 of 4 of *Small steps, measured*, the fourth series of the module *Data management*, in six chapters, in English, drawn in the module's sunlit plaster. Written on 10 November 2026 from the module's brief and its shared numbers, with the leading data people module, which tells how the team reads its measures together and how a leader makes improving everyone's habit, and the data quality module, which keeps the same weekly rhythm for one list.*

## The promise

Every line passes two tests: someone new to data work can follow it, and a data manager, an agile coach or anyone who has run continuous improvement finds nothing wrong in it. Its idea: improve the team's own way of working a little every week, by the people who do the work. A fixed half-hour, kept like any meeting; one measure, to find where the work waits; one small change, tried for a week, then kept or dropped, out loud; and each kept change written that same day into the procedure it changed, so it lasts.

**Takeaway:** "One measure, one small change, every week."

## The story in one paragraph

In the United States in 1940, factories must suddenly make far more, with millions of new workers, many in a factory for the first time, and too few experienced supervisors to show them how. So work is done the long way: in one job, riveting two metal sheets into a part for a radio, the worker walks back and forth to the boxes, stamps every part and carries each full box fifty feet to a scale, and nobody has time to rethink it. Training Within Industry, a government service, trains the supervisors themselves, ten at a table; from 1942 its course Job Methods teaches them to improve the jobs they know best, in four steps that fit on a pocket card, the last of which says: use it until a better way is developed. In the course's own example, thirty details become fourteen, not by a speed-up but by dropping what wasn't needed; by 1945, of the plants that reported a rise in output, 86 in every 100 said it was a quarter or more; after the war it was taught in Japan, and became one root of kaizen. In September 2027, Helena Novak protects half an hour every Friday for how her team works: one measure, one small change, tried for a week, then kept or dropped, out loud. On the first Friday, Noor finds the longest wait in the tracker, six working days before a request's first question back; the team breaks the request's path into its steps and asks why it waits for Monday's planning; Ivy's change, the next free person reads each new request the day it lands, brings the wait to one day within a week. The second change, ten minutes every morning, is dropped out loud a week later. By 17 December, fifteen changes are tried, fourteen kept and one dropped, each kept change written into its procedure with the date and why; days from ask to first use are down to 14, from 24 in June, with July's fixes, and work sent back is down to 6 in 100. Each procedure's page is now what the pocket card said: the best way so far, until a Friday finds a better one.

Where the film differs: Job Methods taught supervisors to improve the jobs of the people they led, and its fourth step has the supervisor sell the proposal to the boss and win approval before the new method is used. The film's team improves its own way of working, together: the change is proposed by whoever sees it (Ivy, the team's graduate analyst), tried for a week, and kept or dropped by the team, out loud. TWI counted its results from plant reports; the film's team counts from its own tracker, the same measure before and after. Kaizen in Japanese firms grew into much more (suggestion systems, quality circles, the Toyota production system): the film keeps only its core, a little at a time, by everyone, every week. The film says "plan, do, check, act" once and names no one: the half-hour is that cycle, one change at a time.

Practice, from the module's brief and the data teams it draws on: protect the time, the same slot every week, or "real work" eats it; start from a measure of where the work waits, not from opinions; change one thing at a time, so you can tell what helped; try it for a fixed time, then decide; drop a change out loud, since a dropped change is a result and nothing should go on out of habit; write every kept change into its procedure the same day, with the date and why, so someone new learns it from the page and it holds while its author is away; pair the speed measure with a quality one, so faster doesn't mean worse; and show the small wins to the people the team serves. Every one of these is said in the film, the first four and the last in *On Monday*.

## Script

### 1 · A better way, on a card · 0:00–2:32
The United States, 1940: a factory hall under its saw-tooth roof; far more to make, and fast; millions of new workers, many in a factory for the first time. On the floor: too few supervisors with experience, the people who lead a group of workers and show them how. One job at the bench, done the long way: two metal sheets riveted into a part for a radio. The job from above: every trip to the boxes, the stamp on each part, the full box carried fifty feet to the scale; time the factory doesn't have, heavy work that wears people out, and no time to rethink the job. Something had to change. Training Within Industry trains the supervisors themselves, ten at a table; from 1942, Job Methods; its four steps on a pocket card, the last, "use it until a better way is developed". The course's own example, in green: the sheets at the bench, both hands at work, the stamp and the scale gone, neither needed. Not a speed-up: thirty details become fourteen. By 1945, 86 in every 100; after the war, Japan, and one root of kaizen. Then the university, 2027: a data team works the long way too; Helena's team, half an hour every Friday, fourteen small changes kept by December, and an answer within a day, not six. The title card.

### 2 · Half an hour on Fridays · 2:32–3:18
September 2027: since July the team has read its measures together every second Thursday, and fixed two waits already, as the leading data people module tells. From Friday 3 September, Helena protects half an hour a week for how the team works, Fridays from half past three to four, in blue on the calendar. The rules on one sheet: one measure; one small change, tried for a week; then kept or dropped, out loud; the ring of plan, do, check, act. The data quality module keeps the same rhythm for one list, fifteen minutes on Thursdays; here, it's for how the team works.

### 3 · Six days to one · 3:18–4:18
The first Friday: Noor looks in the tracker for where the work waits longest, and finds it at the very start: a request through the front door waits six working days for its first question back. They start where the pocket card does: the request's path broken down, four steps (it lands on the one list; it waits for Monday's planning; it goes to someone; who reads it, and asks). They question every detail: why wait for Monday? The form's five answers are enough to ask the first question that day. Ivy's change: the next free person reads each new request the day it lands, and asks within a working day; Monday's planning still sets the order of the work; only the first question moves. Tried for a week.

### 4 · Kept or dropped, out loud · 4:18–5:03
Friday 10 September: the tracker in green, every request's first question back within a working day; one day, not six; kept, and said so. A second change: ten minutes every morning, the whole team looking at the whole list; dropped out loud a week later, ten minutes a day from everyone for nothing new. Dropped is a result too: nobody goes on doing it out of habit. Every Friday, the same half-hour: fifteen chips laid down to 17 December, one grey cross; fifteen tried, fourteen kept, one dropped.

### 5 · Written into the procedure · 5:03–5:53
The six procedures' pages (ask, choose, build, release, fix, retire), each kept change written into one of them that same day. The ask procedure's page: its second step struck through and rewritten, with the date it changed, Friday 10 September, and why. So it lasts: someone new learns it from the page, and it holds while Ivy is away. Days from ask to first use: 14, down from 24 in June, the Friday changes part of it, with July's fixes. Work sent back: 6 in 100; faster hasn't meant worse. At each fortnightly review the people who asked hear what changed, and why: the small wins, shown.

### 6 · On Monday · 5:53–6:43
Back to the pocket card: its last step, use it until a better way is developed. Beside it, the ask procedure's page: the best way so far, until a Friday finds a better one. On Monday, four sheets: protect half an hour, at the same time every week; pick one measure, and look for where the work waits; change one thing for a week, then keep it or drop it, out loud; write what you keep into the procedure, and show the small wins. How a leader makes it everyone's habit is the leading data people module's. The end card: "One measure, one small change, every week."

## Pause and think

After *Six days to one* (break the request's path into its steps and question each one, not a target for each person, and not several changes at once), *Kept or dropped, out loud* (say a dropped change out loud so nobody goes on doing it out of habit, not only because it cost more than it found) and *Written into the procedure* (write a kept change in so it lasts, not only so the review can show it).

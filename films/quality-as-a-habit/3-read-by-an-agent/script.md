## The promise

Every line passes two tests: someone who has never thought about data quality can follow it, and a data quality practitioner, a data steward who writes descriptions for a living, an analyst who has set up an agent on governed data, and a linguist who knows the Cupertino effect each find nothing wrong in it. The film shows the step the module's brief gives it: when an agent reads data, the data's description is part of its quality. An agent reads what's written, answers with confidence, and can't walk over to ask what an empty column means. So describe data for a reader who can't ask (what it means, what empty means, its unit, who answers for it); where empty means two things, fix the data, not only the words; and read an agent's answer before acting on it. Agents help too: they profile, draft descriptions and propose checks, and people review them and set the levels. Takeaway: **Describe your data for a reader who can't ask.**

## The story in one paragraph

Around 2000, people writing for the United Nations, the European Union and NATO used spell checkers. Some checkers' word lists had *co-operation*, with a hyphen, but not *cooperation* without one, so a writer who typed *cooperation* was told it was wrong and offered *Cupertino*, a city in California, first. Writers in a hurry took it: a NATO report of 1999 named the "Organization for Security and Cupertino in Europe", and by 2006 hundreds of these could be found on such organisations' websites. A confident machine, a gap in what it read, and nobody reading after it. In 2000 the European Commission's English translators wrote about it in their magazine, and among the European Union's writers and translators the fault got a name, the Cupertino effect: a warning to read what the checker offers before you take it. The checkers' makers added to their word lists, and by 2007 checkers no longer routinely offered *Cupertino* for *cooperation*. At the university, on Thursday 22 October 2026, three weeks before December's list goes to the printer, Malia asks the graduations team's agent, a Genie Agent the data team set up on the list, which December graduates haven't confirmed their name. It answers 412 at once, with no doubt and no question back. Malia drafts an email to all 412, then reads the answer again: 412 is a fifth of the list, and in March 95 graduates in every 100 answered within the portal's two weeks. She asks Mei, who answers for the list. Empty means two things there: 38 graduates haven't confirmed yet, and 374 confirmed on the paper form, before the portal opened in February, and the paper form has no date in the list. The column's description said only "Date the graduate confirmed their name." Nobody had written down what empty means, and the agent can't walk over to Mei's desk: its 412 was a Cupertino. A description is part of the data's quality (ISO/IEC 25012's understandability), and empty meaning two things is a fault in the data. So the team fixes the data first: Noor, the data architect, finds where the paper form is recorded, in the student system, and from it Jun adds `name_confirmed_how` (portal or paper; empty, not yet). Jun writes the description in Mei's words, for a reader who can't ask: what it means, what empty means, who answers for it; dbt writes it into Unity Catalog on every run, where the agent reads it. Asked again, the agent answers 38 and shows what it counted; Malia writes to the 38. Jun's check from *Report, fix, tell*, which read empty the same way, holds 7 names instead of 76. Then Jun asks his own agent, Genie Code, to read the list and the checks it already runs: it profiles every column, drafts 9 descriptions, and proposes three new checks. Each draft is a start, so Jun reviews each one and Mei sets the levels: a name on the list that isn't the name its graduate gave in the portal stops the printer's file (it finds 3, changed names still waiting for a document); the two confirmation columns must agree, or it holds for Jun; and "every graduate has confirmed" isn't a check at all, since it's about what people do, so it becomes a count Malia follows each week and the agent's trusted answer to her question. What spared 374 graduates an email they didn't need was Malia reading the answer before she acted. The Cupertino's cure had two halves: complete what the machine reads, and read what it offers before you take it.

## Sound

The film's palette, as the module's `palettes.md` has it for this film: **lead, the pulse tones of an early stored-program computer**, as the first computers played tunes through their loudspeakers in 1950 and 1951: a machine playing exactly what its program says, square and plain, C4 to C5, one note at a time, with a rough start as the loudspeaker catches, low and filtered below 2.3 kHz, never a beep. It plays the checker and the agent: what they offer, confidently, as written. **Partner, the machine's own second voice**, chosen by this film: the same pulse tones an octave lower (C3 to C4), filtered round below 1.6 kHz, a soft swell to an even level; it plays only after the first voice has played and a person has read it, never with it: the reading. C major, A minor for the problem. Named for no machine or maker. No sound borrowed from the opening's world: no computer beeps beyond the lead's own plain tones, no keys typed, no office printer.

## Pause and think

Three questions, one at the end of each of three chapters, as `site/assets/read-by-an-agent/think.en.js` holds them (keys unquoted). The right answers sit in different places (third, second, first); every option has the same length and detail; one near miss in each takes the right answer's words and changes the one thing the film turns on.

**`empty`** (end of *Empty meant two things*). The agent counted 412 graduates whose confirmation date was empty. What made its answer wrong?

1. Its query read the wrong column, not the confirmation date.
2. Empty meant two things, and the agent didn't ask Mei which. *(near: the film's point is that the agent can't walk over to ask; the meaning had to be written)*
3. Empty meant two things, and nothing written said which. **(right)**

*Why:* The agent counted exactly what it was asked to, from what it could read. Empty meant both "not yet" and "confirmed on paper", and only Mei knew it. An agent can't walk over to her desk, so the meaning has to be in the data and its description. Which of your columns would an agent misread today?

**`proposes`** (end of *The agent proposes, people decide*). Genie Code proposed a check that fails whenever a graduate hasn't confirmed their name yet. Why did Jun turn it down?

1. An agent proposed it, and only people may write the checks.
2. It's about what people do, so it becomes a count Malia follows. **(right)**
3. It's about what people do, so it becomes a check that holds for Malia. *(near: the film makes it no check at all, but a count she follows each week)*

*Why:* A check says whether the data is right. Graduates who haven't answered yet aren't wrong data; they're work still to do, and Malia's. So it's a count she follows each week, kept in its own model, and the agent's trusted answer to her question. The agent's other two proposals became checks, so who proposed it wasn't the reason. What does your team fail as a check that's really a task?

**`before`** (end of *Read it before you act*). An agent answers your question at once, sure of itself, and shows its query. What should you do before acting on it?

1. Check it fits what you know, and ask the data's owner if it doesn't. **(right)**
2. Check it fits what you know, and ask the agent again if it doesn't. *(near: asked again, it reads the same silence; the person who answers for the data knows what isn't written)*
3. Trust it: the agent showed the query it ran, so the count is right.

*Why:* An answer can be confident, come with its query, and still count the wrong thing. Malia saw that a fifth of the list didn't fit a portal that 95 in 100 graduates answer within two weeks, and asked Mei, who knew what empty meant. Asking the agent again would have read the same silence. Whose answer would you check before acting on it this week?

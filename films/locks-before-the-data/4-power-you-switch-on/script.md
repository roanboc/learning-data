## The promise

Every line passes two tests: anyone who has ever held a key to something that matters can follow it, and a security engineer finds nothing wrong in it. In the 1870s, robbers who couldn't break a bank's vault went to the home of a man who knew its numbers, held his family, and made him open it. In 1874 James Sargent put clocks inside the vault's lock, so that until the hour set, no one could open it, even with the right numbers; one Friday night in May 1875, in Great Barrington, Massachusetts, a new time lock kept a vault shut while robbers held the man who knew them. At the university, Sam's and Jun's everyday accounts hold the platform's greatest power, on every hour of every day: the storage, where no mask or filter applies; the admin settings; restoring a table. Each data specialist now has a second account of their own, its power off until it's switched on for a task, with a reason, a time, another specialist's yes, an alert and a record; never for business users or analysts, and never for agents. **Great power is a separate account, switched on for a task.**

## The story in one paragraph

The United States, in the 1870s: a bank keeps its money in a vault behind an iron door, whose lock opens to a row of numbers turned on a dial, and only a few of the bank's men know them. Robbers find a way round the iron: at night they go to the home of a man who knows the numbers, hold his family, take him to the bank and make him open the vault. No lock can stop the right numbers from the right man, so every banker who knows them, and his family, is in danger. In Rochester, New York, the lock maker James Sargent builds clocks into a vault's lock: each evening the bank sets the hour it may open, and until then nobody can open it, even with the right numbers; the first is fitted to a bank's vault in Morrison, Illinois, in May 1874. One Friday night in May 1875, in Great Barrington, Massachusetts, eight robbers hold the Deland family at home and take Fred Deland to his bank; he turns the dial, but the bolts won't move, because the bank's new time lock, put on three days before, holds them. The robbers take a few bonds from a desk and flee, and the vault stays shut. Taking a banker from his home at night now opens nothing; Sargent's lock sells in the thousands, and banks soon expect one. At the university, Sam Okafor and Jun Park, the platform team, hold the platform's greatest power in their everyday accounts, the ones that open their email: every file in the storage, where no mask or filter applies; the admin settings; restoring any table. Whoever takes one of those accounts takes all of it. So each data specialist gets a second account of their own, an admin account, never shared and never used for email or the web, signed in only with a passkey, and its power off. To switch it on, Jun names the one power the task needs, gives a reason and a time (four hours at most), and another specialist approves; the platform team gets an alert, everything the account does is recorded under its own name, and the power switches off by itself when the time is up, or sooner when he's done. One morning the 02:00 load stops on a row it can't read. Jun's everyday account can't open last night's file, because the back door is shut; he switches on "read the storage" for an hour, Sam approves, Jun finds row 9,317, a date of birth that doesn't exist, tells the registrar's office which record to fix, and switches off after fourteen minutes. Then he asks whether the next switch-on could be avoided, and the load now sets a row it can't read aside, in a table the catalog guards. Business users and analysts never get an admin account: their work goes through the catalog, and they ask the data's owner for access, not for power. An agent never gets one: it may propose a task, and a person decides and does it. Like Great Barrington's vault, the platform's power doesn't open for the right account alone: it opens for a task, when someone else says yes.

## The reference, and practice

The module's reference holds (its proposal, *The reference*): DAMA-DMBOK's data security area for what is protected and why, and zero trust (NIST SP 800-207) for how. For this film's question, who holds great power over data and when, the thread took these, named once here; the film teaches what they agree on and names none of them on screen.

**Where the film differs, on purpose.** The guidance goes further than accounts: separate, hardened machines for admin work, and tiers of admin power. The film keeps to the account, which is the step every team can take first, and which the author's practice is; it doesn't teach admin workstations. It also gives the platform's power a name a newcomer can hold, "great power over data", where the guidance says "privileged": the narration never says "privileged", "elevate" or "just in time".

**Where the metaphor stops.** A time lock opens at its hour for whoever has the numbers; the platform's power opens only for a task, and only when someone else says yes, which the film says outright in *Switched on for a task*. A time lock barred the bank's own staff as well as robbers, which is the part that holds: the admin account keeps Sam and Jun out too, until a task. The time lock didn't stop the robbers coming to the Delands' house that night, and the film doesn't say it did: it made what they came for out of reach, so that, over time, taking a banker from his home opened nothing.

**Practice.** What practitioners recognise: the platform's first admins are its builders, and their everyday accounts stay admins of everything for years, the same accounts that read their email and click its links; a role on the storage given "just for this one task" and never taken back; a shared admin login nobody owns; an approval that's a formality because the person asking can approve their own; admin power handed to a power user or an analyst to "unblock" them; and an alert on every admin action that fires so often nobody reads it. And what works: one admin account per specialist, never shared, never used for email or the web; its power off, and switched on for one task, for the time the task needs, with a reason someone else can check; an alert to the team each time, so a switch-on nobody expected is noticed at once; a record of everything done; and after each switch-on, the question "could the next one be avoided?", so the platform needs less great power every month.

**The author's practice** (3 November 2026, the series' *Decided* table and his brief): "For security, Yes include considerations on storage access where there is no masking or policies at all, we usually have special admin accounts (each of the team members) to perform more delicate activities ... It is worth mentioning the additional permission elevation via admin accounts that would provide those additional risky access. That works for data specialists with a lot of power over data, not for standard business users or analysts." The film says it in its own words: "Some work can't go through it", "a second account, of their own: an admin account", "Never business users ... Never analysts".

## Sound

- **The diaphone is great power switched on.** The fog signal of lighthouses: Robert Hope-Jones's valve, made a fog signal in Toronto by John Northey in 1903, so its note is the air itself, rich and buzzing, with its horn's warmth near 400 Hz. A lighthouse switched it on only while there was fog. Here it is played softly and low (G2 and D3), one voice, at one pitch, with no vibrato, its buzz kept under 2.5 kHz, and never under the voice. One note swells in as the power comes on, is held a moment in a pause without words, and falls as the power goes off, in the diaphone's own short grunt, its air running down. It sounds four times: under the title, at the two switch-ons the film follows (in the beat after the other specialist's yes), and after the last line, as Jun's power is switched on for a task and off again.
- **The square piano is the everyday.** A parlour's piano of the 1870s, small and soft, in its middle and low range. It plays the opening: a house's tune at night, an original line in G, broken off when the robbers come (the Delands' house in May 1875 has the same tune); then the chapters' turns. It is dry and short, its dampers down, except while a power is switched on, when its pedal lets the notes ring; the same chord, rung and then dry, tells a power on and then off.

## Rigour to check before recording

Checked on 5 November 2026, and the page's guides on 10 November 2026; the sources are listed below.

- **Vaults and combination locks in the 1870s.** American banks kept their cash and securities in vaults behind iron or steel doors whose combination locks opened to a set of numbers dialled in turn; a few officers knew the combination. "A strong room behind an iron door" says what a vault is; the film draws no real numbers.
- **The robberies.** "Robbers in the late 1860's and early 1870's turned to what was called at the time the 'masked robbery' where the robber would kidnap after hours the person who had the combination and take him to the bank to open the safe" (*My Time Machines*, a collector's history of time locks); "a series of robberies in the late 1860s in which bank managers were kidnapped" made the time lock "not just accepted but expected" (Anne Day and David Erroll, "The Glory of American Locks", *Invention & Technology*, Fall 2006). "They hold his family" is how the Great Barrington robbery went, and the 1876 Northampton robbery too (its cashier was held and threatened at home); the film says "every banker who knows them, and his family, is in danger" and gives no count.
- **The time lock.** "A time lock secures a safe for a set period ... during which no one can open it, even with the key or combination" (*Invention & Technology*, 2006); "While the time lock is running, even the right combination would not be enough to open the door" (*My Time Machines*). The bank sets it at closing for the hours until opening; the film's "Each evening, the bank sets the hour it may open" and the pill "Opens at 9:00" are an example hour.
- **James Sargent and Morrison, Illinois.** "In 1873, using parts from locks and two, eight-day kitchen clocks, Mr. Sargent created the first time lock to be installed on a bank vault door. He personally connected the time lock to the vault door of the First National Bank in Morrison, Illinois, on May 26, 1874. This lock continued to secure the vault for nearly forty years" (Sargent & Greenleaf, *Company info*, read 5 November 2026). Sargent worked in Rochester, New York. Time locks had been tried before his (patents from the 1830s, and Holbrook's of 1858), but his was "the first commercially successful time lock" (*Invention & Technology*); the film says "the first is fitted to a bank's vault", after naming Sargent, so it means his first, and names neither the bank nor the company.
- **Great Barrington, May 1875.** Fred Deland's own letter, quoted by Bernard A. Drew in *The Berkshire Edge* (26 May 2020): "For years, as you know, we have felt that we were liable at any time to have a visit from masked bank robbers"; "Last Tuesday our new chronometer lock was put on ... But on Friday night they came"; eight robbers came to the Deland house (William N. Deland's, where Fred lived with his mother and sister), "handcuffed and roped the Delands", and their leader took Fred to the bank; "Deland opened the vault's lock, but the bolts wouldn't move because of the timer. The leader tried for himself, without avail"; the lock was a Sargent & Greenleaf double chronometer time lock; the robbers "took several bearer bonds from a desk and fled"; "The bank came out of it very nicely ... Our family were the only sufferers". Wikipedia (*Time lock*; *Frederick N. Deland*, read 5 November 2026) dates the robbery to the night of 28 to 29 May 1875; the lock was put on on Tuesday 25 May, three days before. The film says "hold the Deland family at home" and shows only lantern light and windows; it doesn't say the family came to no harm, because the family were held all night, and it names neither Fred's post at the bank (sources differ) nor the bank (the National Mahaiwe Bank).
- **"Now, taking a banker from his home at night opens nothing."** What the lock's mechanism does: with the time lock running, the man who knows the numbers can't open the vault before its hour, so taking him at night gains nothing from the vault. The film makes no claim about how many robberies stopped.
- **"Sargent's lock sells in the thousands, and banks soon expect one."** Sargent's Model 2 "sold thousands throughout the United States. It was the first commercially successful time lock"; "soon the time lock was not just accepted but expected"; and "bankers were at first reluctant to adopt a lock that barred friend as well as foe" (*Invention & Technology*, 2006), which the film's "Like the first bankers with a time lock, Sam and Jun find it slow at first: it keeps them out too" stands on.
- **The platform's great power.** "The storage: the files under every table. There, no mask and no filter applies" is the author's practice (3 November 2026) and *Walls, doors and keys*' words ("Storage keeps files. It knows nothing of who may see which column: no masks, no rules"); the row shown is *Show the least*'s invented learner, as the module's `numbers.md` gives him. "The admin settings: what the whole platform allows, such as who may sign in, and how" are account and workspace admin settings (who belongs, single sign-on, personal tokens: *People and groups* switches tokens off). "Restoring a table: putting it back as it was, over everyone's work since" is a table restored to an earlier version, which replaces what came after; *Who did what* shows one. The three powers are `numbers.md`'s: "read the storage, change admin settings, or restore a table".
- **"In data governance, the platform team are its keepers."** *Who decides* (data governance) gives the platform team the custodian's part: "Sam's team can change any table, but doesn't decide what an award means"; "keepers" is the film's plain word for custodians.
- **"The same accounts that open their email, and every link in it."** The university's people have one account each, used for email and the platform alike, through one sign-in (*Who is it, really?*, *No secret to lose*). CIS safeguard 5.4 and the Australian Signals Directorate both keep admin work away from email and the web because that's where most stolen sign-ins start: a link that harvests a sign-in, or an add-on that copies one (*Who did what* shows a copied session, of an everyday account).
- **"Some had gone to others too, each for one task, and never come back."** *Network, storage and keys* finds six people with a role on the storage, "each given for one task, and never taken back" (`numbers.md`); the ideas film shows two, "A contractor" and "A tester", and says it without a number.
- **The admin account.** "Each data specialist gets a second account, of their own": the author's practice, and `numbers.md` ("Sam Okafor (admin)", "Jun Park (admin)", the only two in this series). "Never shared": one account per person, so the record can say who. "Never used for email or the web": CIS 5.4; the ASD's guidance keeps privileged accounts from the internet, email and web services. "It signs in only with a passkey": *People and groups* (`numbers.md`: "signed in with a passkey"); *No secret to lose* ranks passkeys first. "It has no power either, until it's switched on": admin power lives in groups no one is in until a switch-on, and only admin accounts can be eligible (`numbers.md`, from *People and groups*).
- **The switch-on.** Which power (one of the three, "only the one the task needs"), a reason, a time ("four hours at most"), another specialist's approval ("Sam approves Jun's, Jun approves Sam's, and no one approves their own"), an alert to the platform team, a record under the account's own name, and the power off when the time is up or its owner switches off: all `numbers.md`'s, set with *People and groups*, which builds it with the directory's privileged identity management (eligible, never permanently active; the directory takes the account out of the group within seconds when the time is up). NIST SP 800-53 (release 5.2.0 of its catalogue, read 5 November 2026): AC-6, "Employ the principle of least privilege, allowing only authorized accesses for users (or processes acting on behalf of users) that are necessary to accomplish assigned organizational tasks"; AC-6(2), users with access to security functions "use non-privileged accounts or roles, when accessing nonsecurity functions" (the everyday account); AC-6(5), "Restrict privileged accounts on the system to" named personnel or roles (the data specialists only); AC-6(9), "Log the execution of privileged functions" (the record); AC-5's guidance, "Separation of duties addresses the potential for abuse of authorized privileges and helps to reduce the risk of malevolent activity without collusion" (no one approves their own). The UK NCSC's *Use privileged access management* (read 10 November 2026): "Instead of the credential being used to access an administration interface, it's used to Request access instead. If the request is approved, a Temporary credential is given"; multi-party approval, where a credential "is only released if a different, authorised individual(s) approve it"; "Just enough Administration is another way of describing the concept of least privilege"; and the process "forces an individual to request and justify their intended actions each time administration access is required", with the actions "all recorded". NIST SP 800-207's tenet 3 (access granted per session, with the least privilege the task needs) is paraphrased.
- **"It costs a minute, and a yes."** The film's estimate of a switch-on's cost to the person asking; the approver's time depends on who's there. With two specialists, each approves the other's; when one is away, the film says nothing, and the question is listed for the author.

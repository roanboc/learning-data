## The promise

Every line passes two tests: anyone who has seen a policy library gather dust can follow it, and an experienced data governance lead finds nothing wrong in it. The film opens the series and says what it's about. A policy that only lives in a binder is like the safety steps every surgical team knew, written in guidelines and often skipped, while patients paid for it. What made them happen, in a study of 2008, was all of it together: one agreed list, a copy in the room where the work happens, and someone who checks every step, with the authority to hold the team. Governance works the same way. Its job is to decide who decides, and to make sure each decision reaches the work; management does the work. The bodies of knowledge are named in the series' prologue; here the newest DMBOK only stands on Yusuf's desk, and the series takes what they agree on. Governance is people's work: it starts by listening, and tools carry out what people agree. Every line of the binder faces one question, *where does it run?*, and behind every line that stays there's something worth keeping, and someone to keep it: owners decide, stewards keep it up to date, the teams who do the work use it, and governance checks it's kept. **A policy that doesn't run anywhere is a wish.**

## The story in one paragraph

Surgical teams have long known the steps that keep patients safe: check it's the right patient, give the antibiotic before the first cut, count every sponge before closing. The steps were written in guidelines, and in a busy operating room they were often skipped. Patients paid for it: infections, mistakes, and deaths that could have been avoided. Something had to change. In 2008, a study in eight hospitals, in eight countries, tried one change: three times in every operation, the team paused, a nurse read a short checklist out loud, each step was confirmed, and the nurse could hold the team until it was done. Deaths after surgery nearly halved, from 1.5 to 0.8 in every hundred patients. What worked was all of it together: one agreed list, a copy in the room where the work happens, and someone who checks every step, every time. Today, Yusuf Demir starts as the university's data governance lead. He inherits a shelf of 34 policies, a maturity score of 2.1 out of 5, a chart of who's responsible for what with half the names "to be confirmed", and a council that meets once a month; his brief is to fix governance before the next submission to the government. The same week, Planning's census report and the draft submission disagree: 4,812 awards, or 4,867. The data team checks both, and both are right: on Monday the word *award* changed, so microcredentials of at least 6 credit points now count, and one report ran before the change. The government counts awards its own way, and no one agrees which number matches it, so the data team takes the question to Yusuf. Nobody has decided how the university's meaning maps to the government's: Mei owns the meaning, Jun builds it, Planning reports it, and the chart says "to be confirmed": like an operating room before the checklist, everyone knows their own step, and no one checks them all. Policy 12, "business terms are defined consistently", doesn't say who decides, or how a decision reaches the work. That's what governance is for. Yusuf doesn't write a 35th policy: he listens first, then asks of every line where it runs. Behind each line that stays is something worth keeping (rules with owners, one glossary, classes of personal data, a list of who owns what) and someone to keep it. Over nine films, he follows one symptom at a time to what's worth keeping, and to who decides and acts; three more draw it all as one model on the university's architecture map, and six build it with the tools.

## The look

*Watercolour*, the look of the series on master and reference data, chosen on 17 October 2026. It replaces toy town (chosen on 16 October), which showed homes and places that weren't the university's: now the two series look like one university, and this one shows only its own places.

- **Pencil and wash.** Every place is painted on paper: a pencil line, paint gathering in the paper's grain, soft light from a window. People are drawn the way the master data films draw them (`films/master-and-reference-data/shared/src/people.js`, bundled as it is), with a soft wash of colour behind each one.
- **Only the university's places.** The campus, Yusuf's office, the floor of offices behind glass where the work is done (Planning, the registrar's office, data governance, the data team, the platform team), each team's room, and close-ups of their boards, screens and papers. Each room has its own wall colour: governance warm cream, Planning sage, the registrar's office blue, the data team lilac, the platform team pale teal.
- **Colour carries meaning.** Gold is governance's decisions, and, behind a person, the business side; cyan is the technical side; green is what runs in the work, grey what can go, and red what nobody owns yet.
- **The past is painted the same way,** with the year in the corner, "Before 2008", then "2008": an operating room, a hospital office with its shelf of guidelines, a map of the world with the study's eight cities, and the checklist on its clipboard, close. The surgical team wears scrubs, caps, masks and gloves, with a warm umber wash behind them. The room is imagined, in no particular hospital, and the checklist is our own drawing, not WHO's page: no logo or emblem anywhere.
- **Fewer diagrams, more situations.** Ideas are shown as people doing things in places: reading a checklist aloud, ticking a box, asking round the offices, marking a policy line by line, pinning a poster. Words are on paper, screens, boards, tags and speech bubbles.

## Rigour to check before recording

Checked on 16 October 2026, and the opening's study on 27 October 2026; the sources are listed below.

- **The study.** Haynes and others, for the Safe Surgery Saves Lives Study Group, "A Surgical Safety Checklist to Reduce Morbidity and Mortality in a Global Population", *New England Journal of Medicine* 360 (2009): 491–99. Between October 2007 and September 2008, eight hospitals in eight cities (Toronto, New Delhi, Amman, Auckland, Manila, Ifakara, London and Seattle), "representing a variety of economic circumstances", took part in WHO's Safe Surgery Saves Lives programme: 3,733 patients before the checklist, 3,955 after. "The rate of death was 1.5% before the checklist was introduced and declined to 0.8% afterward (P = 0.003)"; complications fell from 11.0% to 7.0%. "Nearly halved": 0.8 is 47% less than 1.5. "In 2008" is the year the checklist was in use; the paper came out in January 2009.
- **Known, and often skipped.** The same paper: before the checklist, all six of the safety steps it measured were done for 34.2% of patients (56.7% after), and antibiotics and pulse oximetry "were available at all the sites, including the low-income sites, before the intervention, although their use was inconsistent". The three steps the film names (the right patient, the antibiotic before the first cut, every sponge counted before closing) are on the checklist and were in surgical practice before it.
- **What patients paid.** The WHO manual's introduction: the programme addresses "inadequate anaesthetic safety practices, avoidable surgical infection and poor communication among team members. These have proved to be common, deadly and preventable problems in all countries and settings." The study's paper: "Data suggest that at least half of all surgical complications are avoidable." The film's "mistakes" are the manual's operating "on the wrong patient or the wrong site", and sponges left behind, "uncommon but persistent and potentially calamitous errors". "Deaths that could have been avoided" is what the study then showed: deaths fell once the steps were done every time.
- **Three pauses, read aloud, and a nurse who can hold the team.** WHO, *Implementation Manual: WHO Surgical Safety Checklist 2009*: the checklist "divides the operation into three phases" (before anaesthesia, before the first cut, before the patient leaves the room; on the checklist, Sign in, Time out and Sign out); "a single person must be made responsible for performing the safety checks on the list. This designated Checklist coordinator will often be a circulating nurse"; "All steps should be checked verbally"; and "The Checklist coordinator can and should prevent the team from progressing to the next phase of the operation until each step is satisfactorily addressed". The film says "a nurse", as the manual says most often.
- **Simplified on purpose.** WHO's checklist has 19 items; the film's page shows three of each pause's, in short words of our own ("Patient confirmed", "Antibiotic given", "Sponges counted"), drawn by us, with no logo. The study compared the same hospitals before and after, so other changes, and teams knowing they were watched (the Hawthorne effect, which the paper names), may account for part of the drop. Across Ontario, where checklists were made mandatory, a later study found no significant drop in deaths (Urbach and others, 2014), and Leape's editorial beside it argues that a checklist works when teams adapt it, train for it and use it, not because it's required. That is the film's point: the list alone isn't the change; the list, a copy in the room and someone who checks, together, are.
- **The room and its people** are invented: no real hospital, team or patient. Scrubs, caps, masks and gloves as worn in 2008, and no emblem on anything.
- **The university's numbers** (4,812 and 4,867, 55 apart) are invented, and so is the change to the term: "...a graduate certificate, a master or a microcredential, for at least 6 credit points..." is the change *Written once* tested its glossary sync with (`films/analytics-engineering/project/skills/sync-the-glossary/`, run 6 October 2026 on a scratch copy). The glossary's own term today is in `films/analytics-engineering/project/skills/sync-the-glossary/example_glossary.json`, and its copy, with `source:`, in `films/analytics-engineering/project/models/core/course/_course__conceptual.yml` (lines 18 to 31).
- **The government's definition** is kept generic on purpose: every reporting regime counts completions by its own rules, and the film doesn't name one. The example project's course domain takes its reference model from TCSI (Australia's Tertiary Collection of Student Information); the film doesn't say which government or rule.
- **RACI** (responsible, accountable, consulted, informed) is a common form of the "chart of who's responsible for what"; "TBC" on it is invented, and common.
- **Governance and management.** ISO/IEC 38505-1:2026, Introduction: "Governance is distinct from management and concerns evaluating, directing and monitoring the use of data, rather than the mechanics of storing, retrieving or managing the data." DAMA-DMBOK2 (p. 67): data governance is "the exercise of authority and control (planning, monitoring, and enforcement) over the management of data assets." "Governance sets the rules and checks they're kept. Management does the work" keeps both: direction and monitoring, against doing. "Deciding who decides" is the proposal's wording of decision rights. Plan-build-run is COBIT's, not used here.
- **DAMA-DMBOK.** The book on Yusuf's desk is the current edition, the second, revised: "The second edition, DAMA-DMBOK® 2, was released in 2017, with a revised edition in 2024" (DAMA International, *Who we are*, checked 27 October 2026; DAMA calls the revision a "maintenance release" of DMBOK 2.0). It stands where the series' prologue sets it down, drawn the same way: our own picture of its cover, small, its words decoration, never DAMA's figures. The film doesn't name DAMA or draw its wheel; the prologue names the bodies of knowledge, with the sources in its script. If the third edition (the DAMA-DMBOK 3.0 project, planned for 2027) comes out before release, the book on the desk changes with the prologue's.
- **ISO/IEC 38505-1:2026** (second edition, August 2026) replaced the 2017 edition: anywhere that cites 2017 must change. iso.org can't be read automatically, so on 20 October 2026 the edition was checked in the standard's own preview: its foreword says "This second edition cancels and replaces the first edition (ISO/IEC 38505-1:2017)", and its introduction has the sentence quoted above, word for word.
- **Who decides and who acts.** "Owners decide. Stewards keep it all up to date." follows the types of data stewards on the GCcollab wiki's Data Steward page, which cites DAMA-DMBOK2's section 1.3.5, *Types of Data Stewards* (pp. 76 and 77): "Business Data Stewards are business professionals, most often recognized subject matter experts (SMEs), accountable for a subset of data. They work with stakeholders to define and control data." and "Data Owners are Business Data Stewards who have approval authority for decisions about data within their domain." Check both against the book before publishing. "Governance checks it's kept" is the monitoring in ISO/IEC 38505-1's evaluate, direct and monitor, quoted above. The four things worth keeping (rules with owners, one business glossary, classes of personal data, a list of who owns what) are what the first pack's films follow, one symptom at a time; the film names no file or tool for them, as the first pack doesn't. The three packs, the cards' titles and Noor's map follow the series' plan ([`../proposal.md`](../proposal.md)).

## The promise

Every line passes two tests: someone new to data work can follow it, and a data manager, a team lead or a platform engineer finds nothing wrong in it. Its idea is Ubuntu, "a person is a person through other people": a team's relationships make each member, and each member makes the team. So the team's collaboration and wellbeing come first, then each person, then the manager: nobody alone on what matters, nobody the only one who knows, a mistake said out loud thanked and not blamed, and leave protected. Leading weighs a little more than managing, every time, because the people are the team's first strength; and wellbeing isn't only comfort: sometimes one person needs more support, a better tool, encouragement or a push, so the team keeps working together and no big gap opens. Ubuntu is taught from Southern African writers; psychological safety, the research word for part of it, is named once.

**Takeaway:** "Nobody alone, nobody the only one who knows."

## The story in one paragraph

Johannesburg, in the 1930s: in crowded yards of rooms near the city live women who have come from the countryside, earning what they can, washing, sewing and selling, for low and irregular pay. Eight or ten who know and trust each other form a stokvel, a savings group: at every meeting each pays the same sum into one pot, and one takes it all, in turn. Few Black South Africans had a bank account then, or for decades after, under apartheid, and banks wouldn't lend to them; with no safe place to save and no one to borrow from, one funeral, a term's school fees or a lost wage could ruin a family. They didn't wait for a bank: each group agrees its rules together, every member answers to the others, the burial society carries a family through a funeral, and when one member falls on hard times, the group carries her. Behind it is Ubuntu: a person is a person through other people. Today, by the National Stokvel Association's count, more than 11 million South Africans belong to one, and together they put in over 50 billion rand a year. At the university, a year from now, Helena Novak starts as the data manager on Monday 1 February 2027. Before she judges anyone, she watches: half an hour a week with each person, about them, never a status report; then the team as a whole, against what this team really does. She finds the gap that matters most: every night alert goes to Sam Okafor, about sixty a week, and only he knows how to answer them. In April Ivy Reyes says at stand-up that she nearly let an agent's wrong model through; Jun thanks her, Helena asks what would make it easier to catch, and two more people say what they nearly missed. In June the team pairs on the platform, writing a runbook as it goes, until four can answer every night alert; from Monday 5 July they share the nights in a rota. On Sunday 1 August Sam's mother is taken into hospital. Helena tells him to go and switch his phone off; Jun and Erin take his week, the platform's upgrade waits two weeks, two night alerts are answered from the runbooks, and nobody calls him. In September Helena moves one of Jun's projects before the load can wear him down; in October she gives the team the credit, by name, and answers herself for what slipped. The team first, then each person, then herself.

**Where the film adopts, extends or differs.** It adopts Ubuntu as Southern African writers put it, relational personhood, without a dictionary definition of its own, and psychological safety as Edmondson defines it, named once as "the research word for part of it". It extends both into a data team's daily practice, as the brief asks: pairing until two can do each job that matters most, runbooks anyone can follow, a rota, leave nobody interrupts, one-to-ones about the person, and credit given by name while the manager answers for what slipped. It differs from two common readings. "Team first" isn't the team over the person: as Metz reads Ubuntu, sharing a life with others is held together with caring how their lives go, so Helena's first question to Sam is about his mother. And psychological safety isn't comfort or lowered standards, as Edmondson herself says: the mistake is thanked, and the fix is a better standard (what used means, in the glossary). The author's point 8 is in its own words: "Managing and leading are both her job, but for Helena, leading weighs a little more, every time." "Wellbeing isn't only comfort. Sometimes one person needs more support, a better tool, encouragement or a push."

**Practice.** A practitioner will recognise the one person every night alert goes to, who doesn't complain; the "bus factor" of one; runbooks written only after an outage; leave interrupted by "just one quick question"; the stand-up where nobody says what they nearly missed; and the new manager who judges a team against the last one. The advice is the brief's and the author's: pair until two can do each job that matters most, and write down how; protect leave; thank the person who says "I got it wrong"; ask how people are; and if you're new, watch first.

### 3 · Said out loud · 4:10–5:31

**Narration.** In April, Ivy Reyes, a new graduate analyst, reviews a model the team's agent drafted for her. It counts booked hours as used ones, when used means people actually in the room. She catches it, but only just. At stand-up the next morning, she says it out loud: I nearly let it through. Nobody blames her. Jun thanks her, and Helena asks what would make it easier to catch next time. Ivy's answer: what used means, written in the glossary, so the next draft starts from it. That week, two more people say what they nearly missed. The researcher Amy Edmondson calls this psychological safety. It means a shared belief that the team is safe for taking risks with each other, like admitting a mistake. In hospital teams she studied, some nurses talked openly about mistakes with medicines, and how to avoid them. In others, people kept them to themselves. In 51 work teams, those that felt safer learned more, and the teams that learned more did better. A mistake said out loud, and thanked, is one the whole team learns from.

## Rigour to check before recording

Checked on 10 November 2026.

- **Johannesburg's women and their stokvels, from the 1930s.** The module's source, Grietjie Verhoef, "Informal Financial Service Institutions for Survival: African Women and Stokvels in Urban South Africa, 1930–1998", *Enterprise & Society* 2(2), 2001, pp. 259–296: women who came to the cities from the countryside made their own earnings in informal work, and built stokvels and burial societies as a way to survive where banks didn't serve them. The yards of rooms near the city centre are the ones Ellen Hellmann studied for *Rooiyard: A Sociological Survey of an Urban Native Slum Yard* (1948), from fieldwork among slum dwellers near the centre of Johannesburg in the 1930s. The film names three kinds of work (washing, sewing, selling) and leaves out brewing, the commonest, which needs a sentence of its own (it was illegal for Black South Africans then). The yards are drawn from general knowledge, never a photograph.
- **Banks.** Few Black South Africans had bank accounts in the 1930s or for decades after, under segregation and then apartheid; the Natives Land Act (1913) and the Natives (Urban Areas) Act (1923) kept most from owning land or a house in town that a bank would take as security. Said as the law and the banks of the time, with no villain.
- **The burial society** is the most common kind of stokvel, as Verhoef (2001) and the National Stokvel Association of South Africa (NASASA) describe them.
- **Today's numbers.** "More than 11 million South Africans" and "over 50 billion rand a year": NASASA's count, about 810,000 active stokvels with more than 11 million members collecting about R50 billion a year, as reported by FA News (2022) and IOL (10 October 2023), and still cited in 2026. Said "by the National Stokvel Association's count", since it is the association's estimate. (Wikipedia's *Stokvel* gives another estimate, half of adults, so the page doesn't link it.)
- **Ubuntu and the saying.** *Umuntu ngumuntu ngabantu* (isiZulu), as Ramose (1999) and Tutu give it; Wikipedia's *Ubuntu philosophy* lists it beside the Xhosa, Sotho and Tswana forms.
- **Tutu.** *No Future Without Forgiveness* (1999): "A person with Ubuntu is open and available to others, affirming of others, does not feel threatened that others are able and good". The film quotes the clause it needs and dates the card 1999.
- **Ramose and Metz.** Ramose, *African Philosophy through Ubuntu* (1999); Metz, "Toward an African Moral Theory" (2007), who reads Ubuntu's ethic as harmony: sharing a way of life (identity) and caring for others' quality of life (solidarity). "Philosophers in South Africa": Ramose is South African and taught there; Metz has long worked at South African universities.
- **Edmondson.** The definition is the 1999 paper's ("a shared belief held by members of a team that the team is safe for interpersonal risk taking"), said in plain words with her example of admitting a mistake; the 51 work teams are that paper's (a manufacturing company; psychological safety went with learning behaviour, which went with performance); the hospital teams are her 1996 study of medication errors, where the better units talked about and reported more errors. The card dates the definition, 1999; the hospital study isn't dated on screen.
- **The data story's numbers** are the module's (`numbers.md`): Helena from Monday 1 February 2027; the team of seven, eight from 1 March; the agent's draft on Wednesday 7 April and the stand-up the next morning; the alerts from about 60 a week to about 12 by June; the rota from Monday 5 July (Sam, Noor, Jun, Erin a week each); Sam's two weeks away from Sunday 1 August, back on Monday 16 August; the quarterly review on 26 October.

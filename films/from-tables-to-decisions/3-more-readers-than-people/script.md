## The story in one paragraph

In Vienna, in November 1968, more people than ever drove from one country into the next, and the countries at a United Nations conference had spent a month on the rules of the road. But their signs still differed, and many gave their message in words, in the country's own language: a driver from abroad could pass a warning of a sharp bend and never know what it said, and a missed warning could end in a crash. So on 8 November, 31 countries signed the Convention on Road Signs and Signals, which defines each sign once, by its shape, its colour and its symbol. Today 75 countries keep it, and a driver reads the same signs from one country to the next; and the signs have readers nobody planned for in 1968: since July 2024, every new car in the European Union must read the speed limit off the signs it passes. At the university, casual teaching cost, defined once in September, has readers nobody planned for too. On pay day, Rohan gets A$1.84 million for the Faculty of Health from Genie One, but the morning's budget pack, a Power BI report built through the Fabric mirror, where the measure can't be seen, says A$1.56 million under the same name: it summed the pay paid. Read directly from Databricks, through Power BI's connector, each analyst as themselves, the report gives A$1.84 million. The faculties' planning app reads as the person using it, and a filter by faculty on the claims, set in Unity Catalog, shows Anil only Health's numbers. Finance's pay-day agent, which works alone, gets a name of its own and only what its job reads: it asks Genie One through its tool server, in the measure's own words, and Rohan checks the note and sends it. A group of universities outside reads a small table of totals by faculty, made from the measure and shared through OpenSharing, never a unit's, since a unit with one tutor would give away that tutor's pay. Every reader is written down in the data project. Like the signs agreed in Vienna, the definition was made once, for people, and machines read it too; but unlike a road sign, data isn't for anyone who passes: every reader shows who it is.

- **The conference and the convention.** The United Nations Conference on Road Traffic sat in Vienna from 7 October to 8 November 1968; the Convention on Road Signs and Signals was signed on its last day, by 31 countries ("Thirty-one countries signed the Convention on the final day of the conference"; the UN Treaty Collection lists 35 signatories in all and 75 parties). Its preamble: "international uniformity of road signs, signals and symbols and of road markings is necessary in order to facilitate international road traffic and to increase road safety". Article 8: the system of signs is based on "shapes and colours characteristic of each class of sign and, wherever possible, on the use of graphic symbols rather than inscriptions". It came into force on 6 June 1978, 12 months after the fifteenth instrument (article 39(1); UN Treaty Series vol. 1091, no. 16743). "A month on the rules of the road": the conference's five weeks, 7 October to 8 November.
- **The problem.** "Many give their message in words, in the country's own language": the convention's own reason, uniformity "to facilitate international road traffic and to increase road safety", and the 1949 Geneva Protocol had begun the work only in part. "A missed warning can end in a crash" is said as the harm a viewer can picture; no crash is dated or counted. The plates are invented, in French, German, Spanish and English, with no country named.
- **The three kinds.** Danger warning signs, a triangle edged in red (the film shows the triangle; the convention lets a country use a yellow diamond instead, and most parties use the triangle); regulatory signs, prohibitory or restrictive, a circle edged in red, with a speed limit among them; mandatory signs, a blue circle.

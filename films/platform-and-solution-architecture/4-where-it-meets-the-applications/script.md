## The promise

Every line passes two tests: someone new to data work can follow it, and an enterprise architect, an integration architect or a data engineer finds nothing wrong in it. Its idea: the integration platform and the data platform work better together, for operations and for governance. Between applications, the traffic is small messages, one at a time, and the integration platform carries them: its gateway when one application asks another for something, its events when news of a change reaches every application that wants it, every message checked and logged. Into a data platform come bulk loads and history, which its own connectors load natively; sent as one message each, they would be slower and costlier. So each kind of flow gets one rule: bulk loads and history native; a change in an application, which is already an event, subscribed to rather than read a second way; data served back to an application through the gateway. Each flow takes the route that's simplest, cheapest and easiest to govern, and every flow goes on one map both teams keep, so a flow nobody can see, the one that breaks when a system is upgraded, can't hide.

**Takeaway:** "Each flow on the route that suits it, and every flow on one map."

## The story in one paragraph

Japan, the 1600s: in Shinano, a province of high mountains, the rivers can't carry boats, so goods cross the passes on horseback. On the main roads they go by the stations' relay: a station's horses carry a load only to the next station, where it moves to another horse and the shipper pays a fare and the station's fee again. Every change costs time and money and can damage the goods, and when officials travel the horses go to them first, so loads wait; for the mountain villages everything they sell or buy costs more to carry, and takes longer. So farmers carry loads on their own horses straight through, with no change of horse and no station fee: the *chūma*, a driver with three or four horses. The stations lose loads and fees, and still have to keep horses for the shogunate, so from 1673 they take the farmers to court, again and again. In 1764 the shogunate rules: for each road, which goods the farmers' horses may carry and which go by the relay; it writes down every village that may run them, 679, and more than eighteen thousand horses, and the farmers help keep the relay going. That year 9,600 loads go from Iida to Matsumoto, and the farmers' horses spread across Shinano, joining its mountains to both coasts. At the utility, the integration platform joins the applications, billing, the customer system, outages, metering and work orders, and its team sees every message between them. This year Yasmin Saleh joins as integration lead, and her first review finds 34 flows in and out of the data platform that aren't on her map; one, a nightly read of the customer system, broke when that system was upgraded, and retail's reports were wrong for two days. She asks that every flow go through the integration platform. Ravi Menon agrees with half: a flow nobody can see is a flow nobody can govern, but each night forty million meter readings come into the data platform through its own connectors, and as one message each they would queue at the integration platform, slower and costlier. They sort the 34 flows by kind and give each kind one rule, and put every flow on one map both teams keep. When Grace asks, in a ticket, for each feeder's risk of a fault in the outage app, network's data product makes the score on the data platform every hour and it goes through the gateway, as an API the outage app already calls; Grace checks it in test before her ticket closes. Of the 34 flows, 25 stay native and are now on the map, six become the integration platform's events and three go through its gateway; nothing reads the customer system unseen any more.

| DAMA-DMBOK 2: data integration and interoperability (batch and event-driven integration; point-to-point, hub-and-spoke and publish and subscribe); metadata | The integration platform as a hub for applications; a nightly read straight from an application as a point-to-point flow nobody can see; bulk loads in batch; the map as the record of every flow, its route, owner and contract | *Two kinds of traffic*, *One map, both teams* |

Where the film differs: no standard says which route each flow takes; the plan's rule, the route that's simplest, cheapest and easiest to govern, is the author's. *Enterprise Integration Patterns* calls messaging often the best of its four styles, but it is about applications talking to each other: the film keeps messaging for messages, and loads a data platform's bulk and history through its own connectors. ArchiMate's flow relationship has no route of its own, so the film writes the route on it as a label.

Practice, from the author's brief: the data platform and the integration platform work better integrated, for operations and governance; each flow takes the route that's simplest, cheapest and easiest to govern, through the integration platform or native; every flow is on one map both teams govern. A new flow goes on the map in the pull request that builds it, reviewed by the team whose platform carries it. Grace asks in a ticket, the data team reviews the score's pull request, the integration team adds its route to the gateway, and Grace checks the scores in the outage app, in test, before the ticket closes (AGENTS.md, *Business users ask in tickets*). How each kind of flow is built, and how a team chooses one for each source, are left to *Data that moves* and *A path for every provider*, which the film names.

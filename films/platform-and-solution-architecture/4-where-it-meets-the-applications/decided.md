| 12 November 2026 | *Where it meets the applications* takes its four beats from Shinano's farmers' horses, the *chūma*, on the roads that joined the five: in the 1600s Shinano's rivers couldn't carry boats, so goods crossed the passes on horseback; on the main roads they went by the stations' relay, a station's horses carrying a load only to the next station, where it moved to another horse and the shipper paid a fare and the station's fee again; every change cost time and money and could damage the goods, and when officials travelled the horses went to them first; so farmers carried loads on their own horses straight through, cheaper and less damaged; the stations, losing loads and fees and still keeping horses for the shogunate, went to court from 1673, again and again; in 1764 the shogunate ruled which goods the farmers' horses could carry on each road and which went by the relay, wrote down every village that could run them, 679, and more than eighteen thousand horses, and had the farmers help keep the relay going; that year 9,600 loads went from Iida to Matsumoto, and the farmers' horses spread across Shinano, joining its mountains to both coasts. It comes back to the story in *Two kinds of traffic* (each reading sent as a message would be a load changing horses at every station), in *One map, both teams* (the ruling wrote down every village and its horses, as the map writes down every flow and its route) and at the end (both kinds of horse on the same roads, under one ruling; the quarrels didn't end, but there was a ruling to settle them by). The exact day of the ruling isn't said, since the sources give only the year, and the horses are "more than eighteen thousand", since the sources give 18,768 and 18,614. |
| 12 November 2026 | The film's data story: the integration platform joins the utility's applications (billing, the customer system, outages, metering and work orders) through its gateway and its events, and checks and logs every message, so the integration team sees every flow between applications. This year Yasmin Saleh joins as integration lead, new to the series, and her first review finds 34 flows in and out of the data platform that aren't on her map; one, a nightly read of the customer system, broke when that system was upgraded, and retail's reports were wrong for two days. She asks that every flow go through the integration platform. Ravi agrees with half: a flow nobody can see is a flow nobody can govern, but forty million meter readings each night, loaded by the platform's own connectors, would be slower and costlier as one message each. They sort the 34 flows into three kinds with one rule each, put every flow on one map both teams keep, and route Grace's new feeder-risk score through the gateway; 25 stay native and are now on the map, six become the integration platform's events, three go through its gateway. The numbers are invented. |
| 12 November 2026 | The film's positions, from the plan: the integration platform and the data platform work better together, for operations and for governance. Application integration is small messages, one at a time, through the integration platform: its gateway when one application asks another for something, its events when news of a change reaches every application that wants it. Data integration is bulk loads and history, native, through the data platform's own connectors (Lakeflow Connect, in Databricks). A change that is already an event is subscribed to, never read from the application a second way; data served back to an application goes through the gateway. Each flow takes the route that's simplest, cheapest and easiest to govern, and every flow is on one map (where it starts and ends, its route, its owner and its contract), kept by both teams: a new flow goes on the map in the pull request that builds it, reviewed by the team whose platform carries it. How each kind of flow is built, and how a team chooses one for each source, are left to *Data that moves* and *A path for every provider*. |

# Master and reference data · A hub at work: script

*The script of the eighth film of the series on master and reference data, The same one, everywhere: about 4½ minutes, in eleven chapters, in English. Scripted and built on 17 October 2026, from the approved plan ([proposal](../proposal.md#8-a-hub-at-work)); the times below are the built film's, and [Built](#built) lists what changed from the plan, and why. The title card follows chapter 1; the end card follows chapter 11. Made to read on a phone, like the rest of the series: on-screen text at least 28 px in the frame, and the camera moves in on whatever the narration is about.*

## The promise

Every line passes two tests: anyone who has filled in a form that asks "have you been here before?" can follow it, and an experienced master data manager finds nothing wrong in it. Earlier films write down the rules: when two records are one person (*Who is it?*), and which value wins (*Which value wins*). This one shows a hub putting them to work for a week. Rules act on the clear cases. An AI helper reads what rules can't, and suggests. A person decides the close calls, and her name and reason are kept. Every decision can be undone, every value shows where it came from, changes are published for each system to read, each office sees only what it needs, and a blind check on Friday keeps the rules honest. The hub is LakeFusion, on Databricks, as one example: the film shows nothing another hub couldn't, and ends with five questions to ask of any hub. **Rules act. AI suggests. People decide. And everything can be explained, and undone.**

## The story in one paragraph

On Monday at 9:00, Mei Tanaka, whose office decides who someone is, opens the hub. Earlier in the series, the university wrote down its rules; the hub runs them every day. Over the weekend, 1,240 changes came in from four systems: 820 from the student system, 190 from HR, 60 from the research office and 170 from the alumni office. The rules matched 1,180 to someone the university already knows and found 52 new people; 8 were too close to call, and wait for Mei. She starts with the one someone is waiting on. On Saturday, Rafael Souza, who graduated in 2019, applied to come back for a master's, like Linh last year. This time, his application searched for him first. It found a likely match, scored 74, but not a sure one, so instead of a new student number, it waits. The hub shows why: the same names add a lot; birth dates one digit apart, probably a typo, add a little; a new email takes a little away. 74 is in the band where a person decides; if the dates had agreed, it would be 93, and linked by the rules. An AI helper has read the case. On the form, he wrote "BSc here, 2016–19"; the old record says "Bachelor of Science, completed 2019". It reads meaning, not only spelling, and suggests they're the same person. Mei decides: she links them, and writes why. The hub keeps her name, the time and her reason, and her decision teaches the helper. Unlinking is one step, and every version is kept. The change is published, not pushed: his application moves on under his old number, with his degree on file, and the alumni office stops inviting him back, without ever seeing his birth date. His record shows where each value came from; the typo stays on the application, but doesn't win. On Friday, Leila Haddad, a steward in admissions, checks fifty of the week's automatic links without seeing what the rules decided. One was wrong, and Mei unlinks it. The office's rule: more than three wrong would stop automatic linking, until new rules were agreed. Whatever the hub, ask it five questions. It ends on Friday evening, with nothing waiting.

## Rigour, scene by scene

For each chapter: what's real, what the picture simplifies, and the sources, read on 17 October 2026 (listed below).

- **1 · Monday morning.** LakeFusion is a master data management product that runs inside a customer's own Databricks workspace (its Databricks Marketplace app, June 2026) and uses Unity Catalog for access and lineage. Its pages describe deterministic, probabilistic and AI-assisted matching, survivorship, golden records that keep their source lineage, and stewardship: reviewing uncertain matches, and accepting or rejecting merges. The hub's screens are drawn for the film, not taken from LakeFusion, and a tag says so. LakeFusion's own documentation site (docs.lakefusion.ai) couldn't be reached on 17 October 2026, so the film relies on its product pages, its blog and the Databricks partner blog, and shows only what they back, or what any hub on Databricks does with the platform. The rules "land" in the hub as configuration it runs; the film doesn't show how they're entered.
- **2 · Rules act.** Matching new and changed records against the people already known, with two thresholds and three outcomes (link, keep apart, review), is the usual probabilistic record linkage (Fellegi and Sunter, 1969), as *Who is it?* shows. About 120 dabs stand for the 1,240 changes. The counts are invented; 95% matched, 4% new and under 1% for review are plausible for a tuned matcher in application season. LakeFusion's stewardship pages describe routing cases to the right steward, not ranking them; Mei starts with the one someone is waiting on, as a steward would.
- **3 · Waiting, not a new number.** Search before create: LakeFusion's page lists it ("checks new records against existing golden records"), and *One person, many systems* adds it to the university's applications. When the search is unsure, the application waits for a person instead of getting a new number: a pending identity, as registries keep. Universities publish guidance on graduates who apply again and get a second number (the sources of *One person, many roles*). The picture shows the whole application waiting; a real one might go on with its identity pending.
- **4 · Why 74.** A waterfall of match weights is how probabilistic linkage tools explain a score: Splink draws one for any pair of records, adding each comparison's weight. The weights are log-2 Bayes factors: agreement adds, disagreement takes away, and a near agreement (a date one digit apart) adds less than an exact one. The score is 100 / (1 + 2^−(w − 5.7)): the weights, 7.2, with a starting weight of −5.7 (about one candidate pair in 53 being a match), give 74; the birth date at +3.1 instead of +0.9 gives 9.4, so 93. The bands' edges, 60 and 90, sit at weights 6.29 and 8.87. Real models compare more fields and weigh common names less; the film shows four. The new email's −0.4 is small because people change emails.
- **5 · AI suggests.** The Databricks partner blog (July 2025) says LakeFusion matches by "vector similarity + LLM-driven inference", flags low-confidence matches for review, and gives stewards "AI-generated match justifications". A language model can tell that "BSc here, 2016–19" and "Bachelor of Science, completed 2019" mean the same. The note's wording is the film's. It describes comparisons, not values; whether a hub's notes copy personal values, and where its language model runs, are questions for any hub (LakeFusion's trust page says the data stays in the customer's Databricks environment). The helper suggests; the rules' bands decide what's automatic, and a person decides the rest.
- **6 · People decide.** Stewards approve or reject matches "with full auditability" (LakeFusion, June 2026), and a decision should record who made it, what changed, when and why (LakeFusion, *What is data stewardship?*); the example project's decisions table has the same columns. Unlinking: LakeFusion's guide lists "unmatching records" among a steward's actions. Every version kept: the partner blog says each match decision is "explainable and governed through Unity Catalog lineage and Delta Lake time travel"; Delta Lake keeps a table's history, and time travel reads earlier versions. "Teaches the helper": the partner blog says LakeFusion "learns from feedback"; the film doesn't say how. v1 and v2 stand for the master record's versions: Saturday's, with the application waiting, and Monday's, linked.
- **7 · Published, not pushed.** The hub writes its changes to tables on the platform, and each system reads what concerns it. On Databricks, a table's change data feed gives row-level changes in order, to read in batches or as a stream; it's turned on per table, and records changes from then on. LakeFusion says its golden records "can feed" applications and pipelines without naming a mechanism, so the feed is shown as the platform's, not as a LakeFusion feature. Each system's own job applies what it reads: the student system moves his application under S-16352; the alumni office learns he's applying, and stops its invitation. Column masks in Unity Catalog have been generally available since August 2024: a function decides, for each reader, whether a column shows its value or a masked one, and policies can apply them by tag. The feed carries a status for the alumni office, never the application itself.
- **8 · Where it came from.** A golden record keeps, for each value, its source and the rule that chose it. LakeFusion's pages say its golden records "keep source lineage, show contributing records", and its implementation guide says teams should see where a value came from and which source supplied it. The rules are *Which value wins*'s: names and birth date from the system that checked them at enrolment; email, the newest one confirmed. The values that lost stay, linked as they arrived, so they can be shown, and chosen again if a rule changes. "Checked 2016" stands for an identity check against a document at enrolment.
- **9 · Checking the checkers.** Checking a sample of automatic links by hand is how linkage teams estimate and control false links (the Australian Bureau of Statistics sets and checks its cut-offs from clerically reviewed samples). Reviewing without the machine's answer avoids anchoring: a checker who sees the answer tends to agree with it. Sampling mostly near the threshold looks where errors are likeliest. The stop, more than 3 in 50, is the office's rule, agreed by its stewards, and the hub carries it out by sending every match to a person; LakeFusion's sources don't describe such a switch, so the film doesn't claim one. With fifty links drawn near the threshold, three wrong is an alarm, not a measured error rate. Leila finds the wrong link; Mei, who looks after the person, unlinks it.
- **10 · Five questions.** A synthesis of the film's features, not a standard's list: explain (each match and each value), undo (unlink, versions), review (people decide what's unsure and check what isn't), mask (each office sees what it needs), publish (systems read changes). DAMA-DMBOK's chapter on reference and master data covers matching, survivorship, stewardship and sharing; the questions fit a hospital's patient index or a bank's customer hub as well.
- **11 · Explained and undone.** The three parts in the film's colours: pencil for the rules, dashed cyan for the AI helper, gold for people.
- **Names and maturity, on the day.** LakeFusion, Databricks, Unity Catalog, Delta Lake: product names as their makers write them on 17 October 2026. Column masks and the change data feed are generally available; no feature the film shows is in preview. LakeFusion is named once, in words, with no logo ([NOTICE.md](../../../NOTICE.md)).

## Built

Built on 17 October 2026, from the plan, in watercolour: 4:23, eleven chapters, 121 words a minute, the voice speaking 65% of the time. What changed from the plan on the way, for the series' *Decided* table:

- **The case.** The plan's first case was "a new returning student with two numbers, like Linh last year". In the film, Rafael Souza, a 2019 graduate applying for a master's, never gets a second number: his application searched first, and waited when the search was unsure. Once *One person, many systems* adds the search, the second number is what the hub prevents, so the close call is the application's.
- **Starting with the one someone is waiting on.** The plan ranked Mei's inbox "by how many systems they touch". The film starts with the case someone is waiting on, as a steward would; the hub's sources describe routing cases, not ranking them.
- **Undo is unlinking, and versions.** The plan's undo tray (the decision waits a minute, then commits) became one-step unlinking and every version kept. The sources back unmatching and a table's history; they don't describe a countdown, and an unlink still works after the change is published.
- **One case, end to end.** "Three alike reviews decided together, after a sample checked one by one" was left out: one case told well fits the time, and the sources don't describe deciding cases in bulk.
- **The Friday rule is the office's.** The plan: if the blind reviewer disagrees too often, automatic linking stops "until the data owner restarts it". In the film, the stewards agreed the rule: Leila checks fifty automatic links blind, and more than three wrong would stop automatic linking until new rules are agreed. The sources don't describe such a switch in the product, so people own it, and the hub carries it out.
- **Every system reads one feed.** The plan named the student app and HR; the film shows all four systems reading what concerns them, HR only its own row and the research office nothing, and the alumni office stopping its invitation without seeing his birth date, behind a column mask.
- **The AI's note describes comparisons, not values,** instead of masking the values in it. Its reading of meaning is shown on the form's own words. What the plan also listed (proposing rule changes from the stewards' decisions, answering "why is this value here?") isn't shown: no source backs the first, and Rafael's record answers the second by itself, in chapter 8. Maker and checker, and rules tried out before they change anything, are left out for time.
- **The screens are the film's own.** LakeFusion is named once, in words, with "runs inside Databricks" and "another hub could take its place". No logo; a tag says the screens are drawn for the film.
- **Leila checks, Mei unlinks.** The blind check is done by another steward, Leila Haddad from admissions, so Mei doesn't check her own office's links.
- **The rules' cards** name their films as the series does, *Film 2 of 9* and *Film 3 of 9*.
- **Captions.** Spanish captions in Latin American Spanish, about as long as each English line; three were shortened to keep that.
- **Sound.** A new palette (in `source/tools/score.py`): a soft synthesiser holds the chords, the hub's own sound, one voice to a note, each chord blooming note by note from the lowest and changing with the narration, in B flat at the start and at the end; the rules are a felt marimba; the AI helper a vibraphone; people deciding a horn; the series' mark (1-8-5-1) on its clarinet; and the person's interval from *One person, many roles* on the marimba when Rafael stands as white paper. The effects are an office's: a muffled keyboard, a mouse's click, a card set down, a felt-tip pen. The music sits about 21 dB under the voice, and the only pulse, under Friday's wrong link, about 8 dB under the music.
- **A new chord sound** (20 October 2026). The first cut's chords were on a reed organ, as in *One person, many systems*, and its two reeds a hair apart on every note beat into a slow pulse on a phone's one speaker: held chords swung by up to 4.3 dB. The synthesiser has one voice to a note and its fifths tuned pure, 2 cents from equal temperament, so its chords swing by 0.6 dB at most. It sits at the same level in the mix as the organ did.

## The promise

Every line passes two tests: someone new to data work can follow it, and a data manager, an analytics engineer or a team lead finds nothing wrong in it. Its idea is learning on the job: skills grow fastest on the real work, a small step at a time, beside someone who knows; teaching what you learn makes it last, and spreads it; and learning needs time kept for it, because the tools change every month and their official docs change with them. A team where only one person knows how to run something is one absence away from trouble, so what one person learns, the team should learn too. Courses and docs still count: start from the official docs, then learn it on the work.

**Takeaway:** "Learn on the real work, beside someone, then teach it."

## The story in one paragraph

Angicos, a small town in the dry north-east of Brazil, in January 1963: about 380 adults, farm workers, builders and washerwomen among them, sign up for evening classes run by some twenty students from the university in Natal, trained by an educator, Paulo Freire. Three adults in four in Angicos can't read or write; in Brazil then, that means they can't vote; they sign papers they can't read, and others read their letters to them. Adult classes often use children's primers, with lines like *Eva saw the grape*, about nothing in their lives, and many give up. Freire's team starts from the town's own words: it listens to how people talk about their lives, writes down about four hundred words and chooses a handful. Each evening a culture circle meets, learners and a coordinator instead of a teacher; they talk about a picture from their own lives, take one word from it (*belota*, a hammock's tassels), break it into syllables and make new words from them: one small step each evening, by doing, together, and what each person already knows counts. At the fortieth hour, the last class, on 2 April 1963, the president of Brazil comes; the organisers count about 300 who can now read and write, a number still argued over. In January 1964 a national plan sets out to take the method across Brazil; that April a military government stops it, and Freire is jailed, then exiled; abroad, he writes *Pedagogy of the Oppressed*. At the university, a year from now, Ivy Reyes joins the data team on Monday 1 March 2027, straight from a degree in statistics. Jun Park doesn't hand her a course: he opens the team's real models, and for her first six weeks they pair for an hour a day, one small change a day, each reviewed by someone else; twenty-five in six weeks, and by the sixth she writes changes on her own. From Thursday 1 April the whole team keeps two hours every Thursday afternoon for learning, free of meetings, starting from the tools' own docs. Ivy picks the sensors' counts, and on Thursday 13 May she teaches the team what one count means; getting ready, she found two things she couldn't explain, and asked Sam: that's when she really learned them. In May the team lists the nine jobs that must keep running: five have only one name. They pair on those too, and write each one down, and by Wednesday 30 June every job has at least two names, Ivy's on two of them. Now anyone can take a holiday, or be ill, and the work goes on.

## The reference, and practice

The module's briefs name the references for this film (5 November 2026): Lave and Wenger, Ericsson, pairing from extreme programming, and the 70-20-10 rule of thumb, named as a rule of thumb, not as evidence. The film names each once, with one study on teaching what you learn, and teaches what they share: people learn fastest by taking part in real work, in steps just past what they can do, beside someone who knows, and they keep it by using it and passing it on.

**Where the film adopts, extends or differs.** It adopts Lave and Wenger's newcomer at the edge (Ivy's small changes are real tasks that matter, a check, a description, a column's name, before the 14 rooms her own page found), pairing as extreme programming has it (one types, one reads and thinks ahead, they swap), and teaching to learn. It extends pairing with the module's rule for review: because both of them wrote a change, someone else on the team reviews it before it goes in, so pairing never replaces review. It extends learning by teaching from one person to the team: what Ivy learns, the team learns, and a job that one person knows becomes a job two can run. It differs in two places, and says so. The 70-20-10 split is said as a rule of thumb, "not a measurement", since its numbers were never measured as a law; and courses and docs still count, against the reading of the rule that would drop them. Ericsson set deliberate practice apart from work itself, as practice designed for the purpose; the film takes its core (aim at what you can't do yet, with quick feedback from someone who can) into the real work, through pairing on small changes and a review of each, and shows Jun and Ivy's desk under his line, not a drill.

**Practice, from the module's plan.** The author wasn't asked for this film's reference or experience, as the module's plan is made on assumptions (see the working folder); the briefs' practical advice stands for it. A practitioner will recognise the newcomer handed a course and a sandbox instead of the team's project; learning time put off until a quiet week that never comes; the meeting booked over it unless someone keeps it free; the one person who knows how a job runs, and the week they're ill; the runbook written while two people do the job together; and the newcomer who understands a thing only when asked to explain it to the team. The advice is the briefs': learn on real work with a partner; take small steps and finish each; write down what you learn and teach it; put learning time in the calendar and protect it; start from the official docs, which change as the tools do.

### 3 · Two hours every Thursday · 3:56–4:58

From Thursday 1 April, the whole team keeps two hours every Thursday afternoon for learning, free of meetings. Each person picks one thing the work needs, and starts from the tools' own docs, dbt's and Databricks', open beside the work. They try it on the real project, and write down what they learned, in the team's notes beside the code. Ivy picks the sensors' counts: what one number in Sam's feed really means. ## Rigour to check before recording

Checked on 10 November 2026; the sources are listed below.

- **Angicos, and who signed up.** Angicos, in the sertão of Rio Grande do Norte, had about 9,500 people in 1960, most of them in the countryside, and about three adults in four couldn't read or write (Lobo, 1963, cited by Silva and Sampaio, 2015; the Tribuna do Norte's timeline; the Saiba Mais report of 2021): the film's "Three adults in four in Angicos can't read or write". About 380 enrolled, as several accounts give it; the learners' trades, from the coordinator Carlos Lyra's diary as Germano (1997) quotes it, include rural workers, builders (*pedreiros*) and washerwomen (*lavadeiras*), with domestic workers, factory hands, craftsmen, drivers and carpenters: the film's "farm workers, builders and washerwomen among them". Some accounts (the Instituto João Goulart's) say most were sugarcane cutters; the film doesn't say "most".
- **Who ran the classes.** Silva and Sampaio (2015), from interviews with thirteen of those who took part: one secondary school student and twenty students of several university courses (pharmacy, education, law, social work, dentistry, philosophy), most from the university in Natal (UFRN), recruited by the student leader Marcos Guerra and trained in ten classes at the law school by Freire and the University of Recife's extension service: the film's "Some twenty students from the university in Natal run them, trained by an educator, Paulo Freire". The film doesn't name the funders (the federal government, SUDENE, the state and USAID, under the Alliance for Progress, by an agreement of December 1962).
- **No vote.** Brazil's Constitution of 1946, article 132, barred those who couldn't read and write from registering to vote, until a constitutional amendment of 1985: the film's "In Brazil then, people who can't read can't vote". "They sign papers they can't read, and others read their letters to them" is said in general words for what not reading meant; the picture's thumbprint where a name should be signed is the film's own.
- **The primers.** *Eva viu a uva* (Eva saw the grape) is the primer line Freire used, in his writing and talks, for lessons about nothing in adults' lives; the film's "with lines like Eva saw the grape". "Many give up" is said in general words, for adult classes of the time; no number is given.
- **The town's own words.** The vocabulary was surveyed in December 1962, by students listening to how people talked about their lives (Germano, 1997; Silva and Sampaio, 2015); about 410 words were written down (the Instituto João Goulart, after Aventuras na História) and a dozen or more chosen (12 to 15 in Marcos Guerra's account; 16 in another), each holding the language's main sounds: the film's "write down about four hundred, and choose a handful". The six on screen (*belota, voto, povo, sapato, milho, feira*) are from a list of Angicos's words that begins "belota, voto, povo, sapato, chibanca, milho, feira" (citing Lyra, 1996). The opening's seed named *tijolo* (brick), Freire's best-known first word from other courses; the sources found name *belota* as the first word taught in Angicos, so the film uses it.
- **Belota.** The first literacy class, on 28 January 1963, four days after the first evening on culture, began with *belota* (the Tribuna do Norte's timeline; the Instituto João Goulart): "the name for the ornaments on hammocks and on cowherds' gear", the film's "a hammock's tassels". The slide's hammock is the film's own drawing.
- **The culture circle.** Freire's own account, *Educação como prática da liberdade* (1967): instead of a teacher, a coordinator of the discussion; instead of a lecture, dialogue; instead of pupils, members of a group; the film's "learners, and a coordinator instead of a teacher". The picture before the word, the word broken into syllables, the families of syllables (*ba be bi bo bu*) and new words made from them are the method's steps as the same book and the accounts of Angicos describe them; the three new words (*bola, lata, bota*) are the film's own examples, real Portuguese words made from *belota*'s families. "What each person already knows counts": Freire's "there is no knowing more or knowing less: there are different knowings", as the Instituto João Goulart quotes him.
- **The fortieth hour.** On 2 April 1963, President João Goulart came to Angicos for the closing of the circles' work, with the state's governor (Germano, 1997; the Tribuna do Norte's timeline, which calls it the 40th hour of class; *O Cruzeiro*, 1963, as quoted by Gazeta do Povo, 2024). Some accounts, from Lyra's diary, put the last regular class in mid-March and the tests in March, before the closing; the film's "The fortieth hour, the last class, is on 2 April 1963" follows the closing as the timeline and the press of the time tell it.
- **About 300, and the argument.** The organisers declared about 300 literate (the Tribuna do Norte's timeline: "300 pessoas são considerados alfabetizados", 70% in the literacy test and 87% in the test of political understanding; Silva and Sampaio, 2015, "cerca de trezentas"). Critics count fewer, and question how well they read: a report in the *New York Times* of 2 June 1963, as later quoted, gave 150 who finished and 135 judged literate; a critic's reading of Lyra's diary gives 122 who sat the final test, a third of whom didn't pass the reading test (Gazeta do Povo, 2024). The film says the organisers' count, and "people still argue over how many, and how well". Not "in forty hours", since the hours and the days are disputed too.
- **The national plan, and 1964.** Decree 53,465 of 21 January 1964 set up the National Literacy Programme on Freire's system; after the military coup of 31 March and 1 April 1964, Decree 53,886 of 14 April 1964 revoked it: the film's "in January 1964 a national plan sets out to take it across Brazil. That April, a military government stops the plan". Freire was jailed for some seventy days, then went into exile, in Bolivia and then Chile (Wikipedia's *Paulo Freire*; the Tribuna do Norte's timeline gives his arrest on 16 June 1964 and his exile from September): "jailed, then exiled".
- **The book.** *Pedagogy of the Oppressed* was written in exile in Chile and finished in 1968 (first published in Spanish and English in 1970, in Portuguese in 1974): on screen, "Written in exile, in Chile, 1968". "One of the most-cited books in the social sciences": Elliott Green's count on Google Scholar (LSE Impact Blog, 12 May 2016) puts it among the 25 most-cited books in the social sciences (counting its Spanish edition, which has more citations than the Portuguese original). The film gives no rank.
- **Freire's words.** *Pedagogia da autonomia* (1996): "Quem ensina aprende ao ensinar e quem aprende ensina ao aprender", the film's "whoever teaches learns in teaching, and whoever learns teaches in learning", "years after Angicos", on screen "Paulo Freire, 1996".
- **Fiorella and Mayer.** "The relative benefits of learning by teaching and teaching expectancy" (*Contemporary Educational Psychology* 38, 2013, 281 to 288): students who prepared to teach a lesson on the Doppler effect did better on a test straight after than those who prepared for a test, but not a week later; those who prepared and then taught it, as a short lecture to a camera, did better a week later than both. The film's "Students who prepared to teach a lesson, then taught it, remembered more of it later than those who only studied it" says the second finding in plain words ("only studied it" for preparing to be tested). The paper's record couldn't be fetched from here on 10 November 2026; the summary is from its published abstract (in the working folder's assumptions, to check).
- **Lave and Wenger.** *Situated Learning* (1991) draws on five studies of apprenticeship: Yucatec Maya midwives in Mexico, Vai and Gola tailors in Liberia, US Navy quartermasters, meat cutters, and members of Alcoholics Anonymous; its idea, legitimate peripheral participation, is the film's "first at its edge, with small tasks that matter, beside people who know".
- **Ericsson.** Ericsson, Krampe and Tesch-Römer (1993): deliberate practice is effortful activity designed to improve performance, with immediate, informative feedback, often under a teacher; the film's "practice aimed at what you can't do yet, with quick feedback from someone who can". Its numbers are a summary, not a measurement, as the film says; the module's briefs ask that it be named as a rule of thumb, not as evidence.
- **Extreme programming.** Kent Beck's *Extreme Programming Explained* (1999) made pair programming one of its practices: two people at one machine, one at the keyboard, the other reviewing and thinking ahead, changing roles often. "Two people, one keyboard" is the common shorthand.

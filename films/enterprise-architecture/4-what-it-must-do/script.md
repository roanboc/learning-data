## The promise

Every line passes two tests: anyone who has lived through a restructure can follow it, and an experienced enterprise architect finds nothing wrong in it. A capability is what an organisation must be able to do, whoever does it, however and with whatever. Teams, processes and systems change; capabilities stay, which makes them the spine of the map. A first capability map usually copies the org chart, and the test that catches it is simple: would this box survive a restructure, a new system and a new process? Three levels are enough. Each capability has one owner, a person. Coloured with evidence, the map shows where it hurts, so it shows where the money should go, and how deep to look: only where it hurts. Measures and data rules will hang from these boxes. **Know what it must be able to do, whoever does it.**

## Rigour to check before recording

- **The chasquis.** The Inca road system (Qhapaq Ñan) grew mainly in the 1400s, under Pachacuti and Túpac Inca Yupanqui, and was later recognised by UNESCO. Relay posts (*chaskiwasi*) stood along it at short intervals; sources give distances from about 1.5 to 9 km, so the narration says "every few kilometres". Chasquis carried messages spoken and memorised, and khipus. Runners served in rotation, as part of labour owed to the state (*mit'a*). The daily distance is an estimate: commonly about 240 km (150 miles), so the narration says "more than two hundred kilometres in a day". The film doesn't show the conch (*pututu*) runners blew to announce themselves, or the Andean textile patterns (*tocapu*); the runners and the cords are drawn in the series' own style, with plain tunics and natural colours, and the music has no borrowed Andean instruments (PLAYBOOK §2). Check against a Peruvian source, such as the Ministerio de Cultura's material on the Qhapaq Ñan, on the day.
- **"Khipu"** is the Quechua spelling; *quipu* is the Spanish one, which the Spanish captions use. The voice reads it "kee-poo", and "chasqui" "chahs-kee".
- **Capabilities** follow common practice across BIZBOK, TOGAF's capability-based planning and ArchiMate 3.2, where a capability is a strategy element: what an organisation is able to do, independent of who does it and how. Named as verb and noun ("manage outages"), as is common; some frameworks name them as nouns ("outage management"). Levels 1 to 3 are the usual depth for a capability map; BIZBOK allows more, and the film's "deeper, and nobody reads it" is the series' "just enough" insight, not a rule of any framework.
- **The traps.** The glyphs on the trap notes are ArchiMate's: a business role for the team, an application component for the system, a business process for the dispatch. A team strictly is an organisational unit, which ArchiMate draws as an actor; the role glyph is used because the series' glyph set already has it, and it reads as "who".
- **Level 1.** Eight core capabilities for a utility that runs a distribution network, owns some generation and sells energy, with support capabilities (people, money, technology) beneath them. A real map would name more support capabilities; they're folded into one line on purpose.
- **Ownership.** One accountable owner per capability is common practice; that the owner is a person, not a team, follows the series' governance insight ("a model is true when the people who own it confirm it").
- **Heat maps** are usually coloured against a stated scale (maturity, performance or importance against strategy). The film colours by how well each capability works today against the goals from *Why it moves*, with evidence named on each card.
- **Hosting capacity.** How much rooftop solar a part of the low-voltage network can take is usually called its hosting capacity; many distribution networks don't yet know it street by street, because the network was designed for one-way flow. The film doesn't use the term, and keeps the question plain.
- **The figures** (34 working days, of which 26 in assessment, 5 in approval and 3 for the meter; two poles in five uninspected for ten years; eight capabilities and seven owners) are fictional and plausible, not drawn from any one utility. The ten-day outcome is the one from *Why it moves*.

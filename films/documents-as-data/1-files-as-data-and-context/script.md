## The promise

Every line passes two tests: someone who has only met documents as PDF files and spreadsheets can follow it, and a data engineer who has built a document pipeline on Databricks, or someone who keeps an organisation's records, finds nothing wrong in it. The module so far moved data, ran the platform and served it to people and agents; this film reads documents, and closes the module. Documents come in three kinds, and each gets its own answer. What's written for people, from outside, such as a supplier's invoice, is read into data with the platform's functions where a use case needs it: the file kept, its path on every row, and what's read checked like any other data, with a person deciding. What's official, such as a signed contract, is kept as it was signed, with the metadata its application writes beside it, so a person or an agent finds the right version and cites it. What we write ourselves, such as a claim for hours, is written as data from the start, with what each field means in a contract, in an open standard, so nobody reads it back. **Read documents where a use case needs it; keep the file and its metadata; write what's yours as data.**

## The story in one paragraph

Washington, 1842. A small office of the United States Navy, the Depot of Charts and Instruments, keeps its charts and its ships' old logbooks: each a ship's daily record of where it was, the wind, the weather and the current, thousands of days at sea, each ship's kept in its own way, and never read again. So captains sail the routes they know, not the winds and currents that are really there, and passages take weeks longer than they need to: more weeks at sea for the crews, food and water running low, cargo and letters late. Something had to change. The office reads its old logs into tables, the winds and currents of each patch of ocean, month by month, and draws wind and current charts from them, free to any captain who keeps a log in one set form and sends it back; in 1853, at Brussels, ten nations, with the statistician Adolphe Quetelet in the chair, agree one log for ships of every flag. By the office's own count, ships sailing by the charts cut the average passage to California, round Cape Horn, from 183 days to 135, and nations begin to share their weather data, as they still do. Documents today are no different: written for people, each in its own way. What's written for people can be read into data where it's worth it, and what we write can be written as data from the start. At the university, three kinds of documents show three answers. In October, Finance received 3,860 supplier invoices, most of them PDF files, each in its supplier's own layout; Rohan Desai's team checks every one before it's paid, and for years typed them in by hand, and in September one invoice, sent twice, was paid twice. Now every file lands in a volume, governed in Unity Catalog as tables are; `ai_parse_document` reads each file, its words, its tables and where each sits on the page, and `ai_extract` takes out the fields Finance names, one row for each invoice, each keeping the path of its file. An AI model does the reading, so what it reads is checked like any other data: Jun Park's data project checks that every field is read and that the lines add up to the total, and Finance's own rules ask whether the number was sent before and whether Finance knows the supplier. Of 3,860, 3,734 pass every check; 126 go to a person, each with its reason, and nine of them had been sent twice. The function reads, the checks sort, and Rohan's team decides, as it still approves every payment. A signed contract is different: an official document, kept just as it was signed, never rewritten as a table, with its metadata beside it, which the HR system writes as it makes the document. Linh Nguyen's contract for this semester, T-26188, has two files: the offer HR sent on 9 July, ending on 13 November, and the contract she signed on 14 July, a week longer for marking. In a folder the two look alike; by their metadata, they don't. So when Linh asks when her contract ends, a person or an agent finds the signed version and cites its file: the 20th of November. Who may read a contract is Ingrid Holm's to decide, in HR. Best of all is a document nobody has to read back. Claims for hours came as spreadsheets by email, and someone typed each one into payroll; now the claims app writes each claim as data, the moment it's made, with who claimed, on which contract, whether it's approved and when, and what each field means is in its contract, a YAML file in an open standard, kept with the app's code: the log of 1853 again. Those logs were kept, and more than a century later climate scientists read them again, into over a million weather reports: keep the file and its metadata, and better readers can read it again. So Noor Rahman, the data architect, gives every document one of three answers: if we write it, we write it as data, in a standard structure, with its metadata; if it's official, we keep it as it is, its metadata beside it; if it's written for people, we read it, with the platform's functions or a scraper, only where a use case needs it, and keep the file. Every organisation's documents differ, and the standards are there already: start from the official docs, then investigate, adapt, implement, iterate. That closes the module: what once took whole teams, Databricks and dbt now do much of, and what's left for people is agreeing what the data means.

**Where the film adopts, extends or differs.** It adopts the docs' own path for invoices (files in a volume, parsed once and the result kept, fields extracted, the file's path on every row), and DAMA-DMBOK's view of a record: an official document isn't rewritten, it's kept with the metadata that lets it be found and trusted. **Naming the officer in charge.** The office's officer in charge was Lieutenant Matthew Fontaine Maury, who resigned from the US Navy in 1861 to serve the Confederacy, and in the 1850s argued for sending enslaved people to develop the Amazon; his statue in Richmond was taken down in 2020. The author can ask for his name to be said (the module's assumptions file).

## The promise

Every line passes two tests: anyone who has sat through a workshop about a new service can follow it, and an experienced enterprise or business architect finds nothing wrong in it. Its idea: a new design shouldn't start from a blank page. An agent drafts it from the industry's reference models and the organisation's own documents, every element with its source and the owner who must confirm it, and every element a draft until that owner does; where no source says something, the draft says it's a guess. People react to a proposal faster, and answer it better, than they describe everything from nothing, so the workshops start from the draft. But a tidy draft invites a nod, so the review asks what's missing and what's wrong, not whether it's right. Each owner confirms their own part; the agent drafts again what they correct, and never confirms; only what the owners confirm becomes the model.

**Takeaway:** "Start from a first draft, drawn from your industry's models and your own documents, with every source shown. Ask what's missing and what's wrong, and keep only what the owners confirm."

## The story in one paragraph

Babylon, about 620 BCE, over 2,600 years ago: King Nabopolassar's workmen clear what was left of the temple-tower, Etemenanki, down to its foundation. For Babylon's people, a god's house belonged where the gods had set it, on its old foundation, to its old measures; a mistake would be laid into millions of bricks, by hand, and couldn't be moved once the tower rose; so before the first brick they needed to know exactly where the old tower had stood, and how big it was. Well-trained specialists inspect the old foundation and measure the site. Nor is the tower designed from nothing: a stepped tower of brick with a temple on top had been built in the land for over a thousand years, and at Ur part of one built more than four thousand years ago still stands. Before anyone builds, the king asks the gods, through divination, to confirm the place and its measures, and only then sets its foundation, in his words, "on its original socle". The new tower rises on the exact spot of the old one, about ninety metres a side, and Babylon's god has his house where his people believed it belonged. At the energy utility today, the regional government that owns it asks for a plan: community batteries in three suburbs, in twelve weeks. So many roofs there have solar panels that at noon the streets can't take all their power; the panels turn themselves down, and on sunny days 2,400 homes lose power they could have used. The last new service, a night tariff for charging cars, began with workshops that asked every team "what do you do, and how do you work?"; they ran for eight months, and households waited a year. This time Tomás asks the map agent for a first draft. It reads the industry's reference models (CIM, the IEC's list of what a distribution utility does and Europe's market role model) and the utility's own documents (the government's request, the network's procedures, retail's product rules and the map itself), and in two days drafts 58 elements: capabilities, a value stream from sign-up to credit, the processes and the information each uses, each with its source and the owner who must confirm it, and one marked as the agent's own guess. Tomás asks the owners what's missing and what's wrong. Ama finds what's wrong: to invite households, the draft has retail use the network's list of homes with solar panels; each source was right on its own, but the step that joined them crossed the wall between the network and retail; so the network offers the battery to every retailer on the same terms, and each retailer invites its own customers. Grace finds what's missing: before a crew works on a line, every source that could feed it is switched off and locked, and a battery on the street is a new source the draft never switches off; no document said so, and Grace adds the step. Each owner confirms their part, with their name and the date; the agent drafts again what they correct, and never confirms. In five weeks the design is confirmed, 41 elements as drafted, 9 corrected, 8 struck out and 4 added, and the plan reaches the government in week eleven. Next summer the first battery's homes start storing their noon power, and not one household's details crossed the wall to get there.

## Rigour to check before recording

Checked on 12 November 2026:

- **The story.** *The Royal Inscriptions of Nabopolassar (625–605 BC) and Nebuchadnezzar II (604–562 BC), Kings of Babylon, Part 1* (Jamie Novotny and Frauke Weiershäuser, RINBE 1/1, 2024, [open access](https://epub.ub.uni-muenchen.de/118421/), read again on 12 November 2026). Its introduction: "Before construction could begin, the king's workmen had to remove the massive brick structure of the ziggurat, which was old and in need of repair. Once the demolition had been completed, well-trained specialists inspected the foundations, carefully measured the site, and after confirming the position of the (divinely-sanctioned) foundations through extispicy, Nabopolassar […] staged an elaborate ceremony to purify the building site and lay Etemenanki's new foundations"; and "The base of Etemenanki measured 91.5 × 91.5 m". Nabopolassar's cylinder for Etemenanki (Npl. 6, ii 24–43): "I made inquiries through divination to the gods Šamaš, Adad, and Marduk, and whenever (my) mind deliberated (matters) and took the dimensions into consideration, the great gods informed me through the outcomes of divination", then "firmly set its foundation(s) on (its) original socle". His cylinder for Babylon's wall, Imgur-Enlil (Npl. 3, iii 4–7), calls him "the one who examines the old foundation of Babylon, the one who discovers brick(s) of the past". The film says "divination", the inscription's word, and doesn't show how it was done (extispicy, reading a sacrificed animal's entrails); the diviner beside the king is the film's.
- **The film's readings.** "Cleared what was left … down to its foundation" is the introduction's removal of the old brick structure; "for Babylon's people, a god's house belonged where the gods had set it" is the film's reading of "(divinely-sanctioned) foundations" and the original socle; "to the same measures" and "on the exact spot" are its reading of the original socle and of the specialists' measuring; "a mistake would be laid into millions of bricks, by hand, and couldn't be moved" is its inference from a tower of mud brick in a baked-brick mantle, every brick made and carried by hand, as *Agents on the map* tells it from the same edition.

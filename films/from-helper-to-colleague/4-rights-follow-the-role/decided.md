| 5 November 2026 | *Rights follow the role* opens on American hospital wards of the late 1950s, in the four beats: medicines kept on the ward as stock, in big bottles for every patient, nurses pouring each dose from them by hand, and the pharmacist often seeing only a copy of the doctor's order, written out by a nurse or a ward clerk, so a slip went unnoticed and a patient got the wrong drug, the wrong amount, or an injection meant to be swallowed (*Mirror to Hospital Pharmacy*, quoted in Zellmer's history for ASHP); in the early 1960s, pharmacists at the University of Arkansas Medical Center, in Little Rock, tried another way: a pharmacist read each doctor's order, and the pharmacy sent single doses, labelled and ready to give, each as it fell due (Barker, 1969: "on a dose-by-dose basis at the time of administration"); in their study, mistakes other than timing fell from almost one dose in five to one in thirty (18.3% to 3.3%); as other hospitals took it up, each patient got a drawer, filled from the order, with no more than one day's doses (Zellmer: "mobile cabinets", and the rule of a 24-hour supply); by 1990, nine American hospitals in ten worked this way (ASHP's survey: 89% of those who answered). No patient and no hospital's mistake is named. The seed was kept over stories from outside the English-speaking world (the three-key chests of Spain's royal treasuries, China's tiger tallies), which show separation of duties but not rights made from the work. |

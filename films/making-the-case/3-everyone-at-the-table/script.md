## The promise

Every line passes two tests: someone new to data work can follow it, and a data manager, a product owner or a programme lead finds nothing wrong in it. Its idea: a plan for data has stakeholders, everyone it touches or who can shape it. List them all, not only the loudest; place each by how much they can shape the plan and how much it touches them, and keep the quiet ones on the list; ask each what they want from data, and write it down in their words, since it's what they'll judge the plan by; decide who speaks to each, and how often; send everyone the same page every month, bad news too; and when someone shows a real constraint, change the plan, and say why. It follows the series' thread: the case is funded, its risks are named, and now everyone it touches is at the table.

**Takeaway:** "Know what each person needs from data; tell everyone the same story."

## The story in one paragraph

London, 1850: a great exhibition of the work of every nation is planned for May 1851, in Hyde Park, and a royal commission under Prince Albert must put up its building. Its building committee turns down all 245 designs sent in and draws its own, a vast hall of brick under a dome, and when the papers print it, it's mocked. Everyone with a stake in it is against it, each for their own reason: the park's friends fear for its old elms, The Times and some members of parliament want no building in Hyde Park at all, and millions of bricks would be hard to take away from a building meant only for the exhibition. With less than a year to go, the exhibition is in doubt. Joseph Paxton, a gardener who built glasshouses, sketches a hall of iron and glass on blotting paper at a railway meeting in Derby on 11 June; made of the same few parts, it can go up fast and come down after; and he shows it to everyone at once, in the Illustrated London News of 6 July. Accepted on 15 July on one condition, that three old elms stay, inside, it rises into an arched roof over them, opens on 1 May 1851, as planned, has about six million visits by October, and its surplus buys the land in South Kensington where museums stand today. In 2027, the data council says yes to retiring the old warehouse, and Helena Novak, the data manager, lists the fourteen people and groups it touches, places them on a power and interest grid, asks each what they want from data in their own words, decides who speaks to each, and sends everyone the same page on the first Monday of every month. On 2 June Ingrid, in HR, shows Jun that July is the month payroll can least afford a surprise; HR's reports move to August, the data council agrees, and the July page tells everyone why.

## The reference, and practice

The module's spine holds (TOGAF's method, ArchiMate's words; `../../a-plan-in-ninety-days/proposal.md`). This film is about the people a plan touches, so it names, once:

| Body of knowledge | What the film takes | Where |
|---|---|---|
| Stakeholder mapping, by power and interest (Aubrey Mendelow, *Environmental scanning: the impact of the stakeholder concept*, ICIS 1981; textbooks' "Mendelow's matrix") | Two questions for everyone a plan touches: how much can they shape it (power), and how much does it touch them (interest) | *Everyone it touches*: "Planners call it a power and interest grid, after Aubrey Mendelow's work." |
| Stakeholder engagement, as project managers practise it (the Association for Project Management's ten key principles) | Listen before planning; communicate, consult; plan who speaks to whom and how often; keep it going | *In their own words* and *One page, every month* |
| *Teachers of data* (Governance principles, on main) | Getting people to take part comes first | *In their own words*, named once |

Where the film differs from the grid's usual reading: the grid's four corners are often taught as four treatments (manage closely, keep satisfied, keep informed, monitor), and "monitor" can read as "leave out". The film keeps the two questions and drops the treatments: everyone on the grid gets the same monthly page, and the quiet ones, low on it because they rarely ask, are asked too, through their representatives, as it's their data ("Low on the grid isn't off the list"). The year of Mendelow's work isn't said: his paper is in the proceedings of the second International Conference on Information Systems, 1981, which textbooks often cite as 1991.

Practice, from the module's brief and from how data managers and programme leads run a change: list everyone the plan touches, past the people who ask most often and most loudly (who uses the data, who makes it, who pays for it, who decides, and whose data it is); ask each the same question, what do you want from data, and keep their answer in their words, as what they'll judge the plan by; one person speaks for the team to each, so nobody hears two versions; one page, the same for everyone, every month, with bad news on it as soon as it's known; and a real constraint, a fact about the work, changes the plan, while a preference doesn't. Each is said in *Heard*, on Monday.

## Script

### 1 · The elms of Hyde Park · 0:00–3:05
Hyde Park in the summer of 1850, its three old elms; the royal commission's card; 245 designs turned down; the committee's hall of brick under a dome rises over the park, and the elms it would cut down are outlined in amber; the papers mock it. The worries come in round it, one card each: the elms, no building in the park, millions of bricks, less than a year to go; something had to change. A board room in Derby: Paxton, who built glasshouses; the sketch drawing itself on pink blotting paper, 11 June 1850; the same few parts, a column, a girder and a pane, again and again; the newspaper's page of 6 July, in many hands. Back in the park: accepted on 15 July, on one condition; the hall of iron and glass goes up, its middle rising into the arched roof over the three elms; the opening day, 1 May 1851; about six million visits; the surplus, and South Kensington's museums; each worry's card turns to its answer. Then a university, a year from now: fourteen stakeholders round the move, the loudest drawn larger; HR's reports and payroll's busiest weeks; the data manager asks each one, and the title card.

### 2 · Everyone it touches · 3:05–4:52
Tuesday 25 May 2027: the data council says yes; the 38 reports, the 21 people use moving to the platform, the 17 nobody opens retired. The next morning, Helena's list: the loudest first, then who uses the data, who makes it, who pays for it, who decides and whose data it is; fourteen. The grid's two questions; its name, after Mendelow's work; the fourteen placed, one by one: high on both, the deputy vice-chancellor and the director of digital services, Finance and HR; the deans and IT; the registrar's office, Planning, Facilities, the data partners and about 260 report users; the team itself; staff and students low, ringed, and asked too, through their representatives.

### 3 · In their own words · 4:52–6:15
The same question for each: what do you want from data? Their answers in their own words: the deputy vice-chancellor's, Rohan's, Ingrid's, the staff representatives', and the team's own; each answer beside each name on the map, what they'll judge the plan by. Who speaks to whom, and how often: Helena to the deputy vice-chancellor, each data partner to their dean, Noor to IT, Yusuf to the representatives every semester; one voice for each, so nobody hears two versions. Getting people to take part comes first, as *Teachers of data* showed.

### 4 · One page, every month · 6:15–6:59
The page, on the first Monday of every month: where the plan is, the measures, the top three risks, the decisions needed; a short line for each of the fourteen; the same page for everyone. The first on Monday 7 June, the day the one front door opens; from July, the measures page on the platform's screen; bad news on it too, as soon as it's known.

### 5 · A real constraint · 6:59–8:01
Wednesday 2 June, HR's office: Ingrid shows Jun payroll's year; the move's months, HR's reports in July; the financial year ends on 30 June, the last returns by mid-July; July in amber. A real constraint, a fact about the work, not a preference: HR's reports slide to August. The June page, under the decisions needed, with the reason; the data council agrees on 29 June; the July page tells everyone. Hyde Park again: the roof rose over the elms; here the plan moved round payroll's weeks.

### 6 · Heard · 8:01–9:26
Not everyone hears yes: Clara's forecast waits its turn on the one list, with a reason and a date. The July page: an old extract with personal fields nobody needed, left behind. Every month they see the plan move; how to explain, and how to say no, are the leading data people module's. The park, each worry answered and one picture for everyone; the university, the fourteen and the same page; three things to do on Monday, a card each; next, one front door and one list; the takeaway, which becomes the end card.

| 10 November 2026 | *Design it simple* opens in Korea, in the four beats: in the 1440s, in the kingdom of Joseon under King Sejong, people write in Chinese characters, a sign for each word, thousands of them, made for another language; learning them takes years, so most people can't write what they want to say, and in a court a person's words are written down in characters they can't read, where one wrong character could punish someone innocent, with no way for them to check (the harm the king's side argued, as Choe Manri's memorial of 1444 quotes it); something has to change. King Sejong starts from what people need, to write the way they speak: in 1443 he makes 28 letters, the consonants drawn from the mouth and throat, five basic shapes with a stroke added for a stronger sound, the vowels from three marks (heaven, the earth, a person), put together a syllable at a time; one of his scholars, Choe Manri, objects that they would turn people away from learning in characters, and the king argues for them and in 1446 publishes them, with a book eight of his scholars wrote. The benefit: its closing words (Jeong In-ji's postface, in the film's own translation) say a quick learner can know them before the morning is over, and anyone within ten days; today both Koreas write with them, and in the South about 98 adults in 100 can read and write (UNESCO's Institute for Statistics, 97.97% in 2008). It comes back to the story at "Five shapes made every letter; five principles can shape every choice", at "as the king argued for his letters", and at the end.

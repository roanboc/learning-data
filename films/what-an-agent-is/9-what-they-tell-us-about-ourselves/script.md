## The promise

Every line passes two tests: anyone who has talked to a chatbot can follow it, and a neuroscientist, or a philosopher of mind, finds nothing wrong in it. We build agents as rough copies of ourselves: networks loosely modelled on neurons, trained on what people wrote. Building them has taught us about our own brains, as building a rough copy of the inner ear did in Melbourne in 1978, when Rod Saunders's brain learned to understand speech from ten electrodes, after a pioneer of the field had doubted it could. The film asks five questions, each with the thinkers and studies behind it, and says plainly where science has no answer: what building them has taught us about the brain (much, and where the copy stops); whether creativity is more than mixing what we learned (not for science alone to settle); whether copying a brain would make a mind (nobody knows); why it feels like anything to be us, and whether a machine could feel (science doesn't know yet); and what makes us human, then (nobody has settled it). At the university, the agent's count is signed by a person, the five questions stay on the wall, and seeing where the copy is like us and where it isn't helps Jun's team decide what to hand the agent, and what to keep. **We don't have the answers yet. Keep wondering.**

## The story in one paragraph

Melbourne, Australia, 1978. Rod Saunders has lost his hearing in a car accident: he can read his wife Margaret's lips, but can't hear her voice, and a hearing aid can't help, because his inner ears no longer pass sound to his brain. For people deaf as adults, it means a life cut off from voices, and even a pioneer of the field had doubted, in 1966, that an adult brain could learn to understand speech from electric signals, "a foreign language". The surgeon Graeme Clark, who had watched his own father go deaf, worked for ten years with his team towards a rough copy of the inner ear: an implant, a fine wire with ten electrodes that carry sound to the nerve as pulses. On 1 August 1978 it goes in; when the team plays "Waltzing Matilda", Rod sings along; by December he understands words with no lips to read. The copy was rough, ten electrodes where a healthy ear has thousands of cells, and his brain did the rest: with practice, it learned the "foreign language". By 2019, more than 700,000 implants had been fitted worldwide. The agents we build for data are rough copies too, of neurons and of what people wrote. At the university, the agent counts this term's students, 18,412, and shows its query; Mei, in the registrar's office, signs; and over coffee the team asks five questions. What has building them taught us about the brain? Rosenblatt made the perceptron in 1958 to understand how a brain senses, remembers and acts; today's models descend from it and learn mostly by guessing the next word, and in 2022 the brains of nine people listening to a podcast were found guessing the next word too, as Friston and Clark had argued brains do; Rod's brain did something like it; but a brain predicts at many levels at once, one brain cell took a network five to eight layers deep to imitate, and how a brain learns from its errors, nobody knows yet. Is creativity more than mixing? We mix what we learned from others, and so do models; Lovelace in 1843 said the first computer design could only do what we order; Boden named three ways to be creative, and said whether a computer could ever really be creative isn't for science alone to settle; a person brings a life, an intention, a reason, as Clark brought his father. Would a copy of a brain be a mind? A worm's 302 neurons were mapped by 1986, and its computer copy still only wriggles; a fly's 139,255 were mapped in 2024, and a model from the map predicted which answer to sugar; we have about 86 billion; a copy can act like us without working like us, as Rod's implant, made for speech, still makes music sound poor; and whether a copy that worked exactly like us would be a mind, Searle and his critics disagree. Why does it feel like anything? The implant didn't hear; Rod did. Chalmers called it the hard problem, and a 2025 test of two leading theories challenged key parts of both. Could a machine feel? Llinás found the brain hums about forty times a second, awake and dreaming; Penrose and Hameroff look to quantum effects in microtubules, which critics doubt and tests go on; Llinás wrote in 2001 that he sincerely doubted the quantum idea, and in 2007 said he didn't know what in our cells produces feeling; in 2023, nineteen researchers found no current AI conscious, but no obvious barrier to building systems that show the signs. What makes us human? Descartes thought language was the line, in 1637; today machines answer whatever we say; nobody has settled it. Rod heard Margaret's voice again. Building copies keeps teaching us about ourselves, and seeing where they're like us and where they're not helps Jun's team decide what to hand the agent, and what to keep. We don't have the answers yet; keep wondering.

### 1 · A rough copy · 0:00–1:55

**Narration.** Melbourne, Australia, 1978. Rod Saunders had lost his hearing in a car accident. He could read his wife Margaret's lips, but he couldn't hear her voice. A hearing aid only makes sound louder, and his inner ears no longer passed sound to his brain at all. For people like Rod, deaf as adults, it meant a life cut off from voices. Even a pioneer of the field doubted, in 1966, that an adult brain could ever learn to understand speech from electric signals: a ‘foreign language’, he called it. Something had to change. The surgeon Graeme Clark had watched his own father go deaf. For ten years, he and his team worked on a rough copy of the inner ear: an implant, a fine wire with ten electrodes, to carry sound to the nerve as electric pulses. By 2019, more than seven hundred thousand implants had been fitted worldwide. It's the same with the agents we build for data. They're rough copies too, of neurons and of what people wrote. ### 3 · What the copy taught us · 2:37–3:50

### 5 · Wire by wire · 4:52–6:12

Nobody has shown that it is. |

## Labs (candidates)

The module's labs come later, as one set (briefs, *What the films say*). Candidates from this film, which gives no rules, so each lab asks for a judgement and has no single right answer:

1. **Like us, or not?** Ten things the team's agent does, from guessing the next word to saying it's sure: sort each into "like us", "not like us" or "nobody knows yet", and say what each means for what the team hands it.
2. **Acts like it, works like it.** Five copies (an implant, a flight simulator, a model of a fly's brain, a wiring map, a chatbot): for each, what it does as the original does, and where it works differently.
3. **What to hand it, and what to keep.** A week of the team's tasks: hand each to the agent, keep it, or hand it with a person's check, and say why; the sign-off always stays with a person.

## Rigour

Checked on 3 November 2026; the sources are listed below.

- **Rod Saunders, and why a hearing aid couldn't help.** IEEE Spectrum's history (2022) names him and his wife, Margaret ("Rod Saunders converses with his wife, Margaret, via his prototype cochlear implant", a photo caption), and says he "had lost his hearing in a car accident"; the *Medical Journal of Australia*'s history (Ho and others, 2018) describes the first patient as "a 46-year-old gentleman who had suddenly developed complete bilateral sensorineural hearing loss after a head injury 18 months before the operation" (IEEE says two years); so the film gives no interval. For the profoundly deaf, "there is an interruption of the sound signal to the auditory nerve ... Amplification will not help these individuals hear, and they rely on lipreading or sign language" (IEEE); the film's "his inner ears no longer passed sound to his brain at all" says this in plain words, and its reading of lips is what that sentence describes. Since the implant worked, his auditory nerve did; the loss was in the inner ear.
- **"For people like Rod, deaf as adults".** The film speaks of people who lost their hearing as adults and wanted it back, as Rod did. It does not say deafness is a tragedy for everyone: the MJA history notes that the implant "was initially met with criticism by some communities, and even regarded as an attack on the Deaf community", and many Deaf people live full lives in sign language. The page's text for the opening, if any, keeps the same care.
- **The 1966 doubt.** Ho and others (MJA, 2018): "Blair Simmons concluded in his important 1966 review that it was unlikely that electrical stimulation of the auditory nerve could provide a “uniquely useful means of communication” because the mature brain was unlikely to be sufficiently plastic to “learn” this new “foreign language.”" Simmons was himself a pioneer: with Robert White at Stanford he implanted a six-channel electrode in 1964, and Clark was inspired by his work (the same source). So the film says "a pioneer of the field", and "he called it" a foreign language, with the words the MJA quotes; Simmons's review itself (*Archives of Otolaryngology* 84, 1966) wasn't found, so the line is said as the MJA reports it. "Most experts thought nothing could help" was cut from the draft for length; the sources support it ("widespread scepticism", MJA; criticism "from all directions", IEEE).
- **Clark and his father.** Clark, born 1935, was "motivated by his father's sensorineural hearing loss" and decided at ten to become an ear, nose and throat doctor (MJA); his father Colin was a pharmacist in Camden, near Sydney, who asked customers to speak up, so "everyone else in the shop would hear their business", and Graeme "saw how tiring it was for his father and how he grew isolated" (IEEE). The pharmacy card draws that, without likenesses.
- **Ten years, a rough copy, ten electrodes.** "Clark spent 10 years investigating animal models of hearing", against "widespread scepticism", shaking tins for donations on Melbourne's streets until three telethons paid for the prototype (MJA). The implant had "ten active platinum electrodes", passed 25 mm into the cochlea through the round window, with the receiver under the skin and no wire through it (MJA). His team included Joe Tong, Bruce Millar, Jim Patrick and Ian Forster (IEEE). The film's "a rough copy of the inner ear" is its own phrase for an implant that does part of the cochlea's job, sending sound to the nerve as electric pulses; the corner's "1967–1977" is the ten years before the operation, said in the narration as "for ten years".
- **1 August 1978, and Waltzing Matilda.** IEEE: on 1 August 1978, "over the course of a 9-hour operation, Clark and surgeon Brian Pyman" placed the implant behind Rod's left ear. The first two tests found no hearing; at the third, "a loose connection" was fixed, "Saunders heard a sound emerge from the background tinnitus"; the team played "God Save the Queen", and he stood to attention and pulled out his leads; then "Waltzing Matilda", "and Saunders sang along" (IEEE; the MJA confirms the loose connection and that he recognised "Waltzing Matilda"). The draft's "At first, Rod heard nothing" was cut for length; the anthem is left out so the moment stays on the song the MJA's title names.
- **"By December, he understood words with no lips to read."** IEEE: "In mid-December, audiologist Angela Marshall gave Saunders an open-set test, during which she could say any word in his vocabulary to determine if he could decipher speech based not on context or lipreading but on electrical stimulation alone. Saunders did not get every word, but it was enough to show that the implant was working. Clark cried tears of joy." The film says "words", not every word. The card held in front of the audiologist's mouth is the film's picture of a test without lipreading, not a described detail.
- **"Ten electrodes, where a healthy ear has thousands of tiny cells that turn sound into signals."** About 3,500 inner hair cells and about 11,000 outer hair cells in each human cochlea (BioNumbers 117129, after Ashmore, 2008); the inner hair cells turn sound into signals for the nerve. "Thousands" is safe for either count.
- **"Rod's brain did the rest: with practice, it learned the ‘foreign language’."** Over the next two months he "logged hundreds of hours with audiologists to fine-tune the device" (IEEE); "Hearing through a cochlear implant is different from normal hearing and takes time to learn or relearn", and adults "learn to associate the signals from the implant with sounds they remember" (NIDCD, updated 13 June 2024). The film's "with practice" covers both the tuning and the learning; "the brain did the rest" is the film's plain summary of the NIDCD's account, and the point the MJA makes against Simmons's doubt.
- **"By 2019, more than seven hundred thousand implants had been fitted worldwide."** NIDCD: "As of December 2019, approximately 736,900 registered devices have been implanted worldwide." These are all cochlear implants, by every maker, not only the Melbourne design; and some people have two, so the film counts implants, not people. NIDCD: "An implant does not restore normal hearing."
- **Not the first in the world, and the film doesn't say so.** William House and John Doyle implanted a single-electrode device in 1961, Simmons and White a six-channel one in 1964 (MJA), and a team in France worked in parallel (IEEE); Melbourne's was the first modern multichannel implant of its kind in 1978 (MJA's words: "the first modern multichannel cochlear implant"). The film says "a rough copy", "an implant", never "the first".
- **The data story's claim.** "They're rough copies too, of neurons and of what people wrote": today's language models are artificial neural networks, which began as simplified models of neurons (Rosenblatt, below), trained mostly on text people wrote. "Seeing where they're like us, and where they're not, shows a team what to hand them, and what to keep" is the film's benefit for data, not a rule; the module's rules stay with the series' other films and series 2.
- **The census count and the signature.** 18,412 students is the series' number, from *A model, reasoning, hands and a loop*: the count on census day, 31 March 2026, always said as "students", never "enrolments", on the series' answer card with its evidence attached; the agent shows the query and a person signs (the module's rules: agents are never approvers; tools read with the fewest rights, here read only on the task card in chapter 8). Mei Tanaka is the governance films' Mei, in the registrar's office.
- **Rosenblatt, 1958.** F. Rosenblatt, "The perceptron: a probabilistic model for information storage and organization in the brain", *Psychological Review* 65 (1958), 386–408, from the Cornell Aeronautical Laboratory in Buffalo. It opens: "If we are eventually to understand the capability of higher organisms for perceptual recognition, generalization, recall, and thinking, we must first have answers to three fundamental questions: 1. How is information about the physical world sensed, or detected, by the biological system? 2. In what form is information stored, or remembered? 3. How does information contained in storage, or in memory, influence recognition and behavior?" The film's "to understand how a brain senses, remembers and acts" is those three questions in plain words. "Described", because in 1958 the perceptron ran as a simulation; the Mark I machine came later. The film draws no likeness and no copy of the machine. *Decades in the making* tells this history; this film only uses its purpose.
- **"Today's models descend from it, and learn mostly by guessing the next word."** Language models are deep neural networks, trained first to predict the next token of text and then further trained, as *Decades in the making* tells; "mostly" covers the later training.
- **The 2022 podcast study.** A. Goldstein and others, "Shared computational principles for language processing in humans and deep language models", *Nature Neuroscience* 25 (2022), 369–380: nine participants with electrodes placed for epilepsy treatment listened to a 30-minute podcast; their brains "spontaneously engaged in next-word prediction before word onset", and showed surprise after a word that didn't match. The film says "recorded the brains" and leaves the hospital out. Related: M. Schrimpf and others, *PNAS* 118 (2021), e2105646118, found that models' fit to brain activity tracks how well they predict the next word.
- **Friston and Clark.** K. Friston, "The free-energy principle: a unified brain theory?", *Nature Reviews Neuroscience* 11 (2010), 127–138; A. Clark, "Whatever next? Predictive brains, situated agents, and the future of cognitive science", *Behavioral and Brain Sciences* 36 (2013), 181–204 ("Brains, it has recently been argued, are essentially prediction machines"), and *Surfing Uncertainty* (Oxford University Press, 2016). The film's "had long argued that this is what brains do: predict, and learn from the errors" is their view in plain words, not a settled fact; the film says "argued". Llinás said much the same in 2001: "The capacity to predict the outcome of future events — critical to successful movement — is, most likely, the ultimate and most common of all global brain functions" (*I of the Vortex*, as quoted by Hawkins, *On Intelligence*, 2004, and others).
- **"Rod's brain did something like it, matching ten rough signals to the voices it remembered."** The NIDCD's "associate the signals from the implant with sounds they remember" (above); "something like it" because nobody has shown that his learning was prediction, only that the brain learned.
- **"A brain guesses at many levels at once, from the next word to where the story is going."** C. Caucheteux, A. Gramfort and J.-R. King, "Evidence of a predictive coding hierarchy in the human brain listening to speech", *Nature Human Behaviour* 7 (2023), 430–441: in 304 people, brain activity was best explained by adding long-range, high-level predictions to a model's; the brain "predicts a hierarchy of representations that spans multiple timescales", while models are trained to predict nearby words.
- **"To imitate one kind of brain cell, researchers needed a network five to eight layers deep."** D. Beniaguev, I. Segev and M. London, "Single cortical neurons as deep artificial neural networks", *Neuron* 109 (2021), 2727–2739: a temporal convolutional network of five to eight layers was needed to reproduce, at millisecond resolution, the input and output of a detailed model of a layer 5 pyramidal neuron of the cortex (with NMDA receptors). "One kind of brain cell", because it's one type of neuron, as modelled in detail, not every neuron.
- **"And how a brain learns from its errors, nobody knows yet."** T. P. Lillicrap and others, "Backpropagation and the brain", *Nature Reviews Neuroscience* 21 (2020), 335–346: the method deep networks learn by is widely thought biologically problematic, and how the brain assigns credit for its errors is an open question; the authors argue it may approximate it. Cut for length: the brain's power, about 20 watts (V. Balasubramanian, *PNAS* 118, 2021, e2107022118).
- **"We learn from others too, and mix what we learned in new ways."** The plan's own words for this question ("We learn from others and recombine in new ways; so do models"); human thinking's reliance on what others pass on, and add to, is the subject of M. Tomasello, *The Cultural Origins of Human Cognition* (Harvard University Press, 1999). The hands passing a pencil are the film's picture.
- **Lovelace, 1843.** Note G of her translation of Menabrea's *Sketch of the Analytical Engine invented by Charles Babbage* (1843): "The Analytical Engine has no pretensions whatever to originate anything. It can do whatever we know how to order it to perform." The film's "could do only" is "has no pretensions whatever to originate anything"; the quoted words are hers. "The first design for a computer": the Analytical Engine, designed by Babbage from 1834 and never built, is generally called the first design for a general-purpose computer. Corner: "England · 1843"; she is drawn as a woman of twenty-seven in a day dress of the 1840s, not as a likeness.
- **Boden.** M. A. Boden, *The Creative Mind: Myths and Mechanisms* (1990; second edition, Routledge, 2004): creativity is coming up with ideas that are new, surprising and valuable, in three ways, combinational, exploratory and transformational, the last changing the rules of a style (her "conceptual space") so that ideas impossible before become possible. Her four "Lovelace questions" ask whether computational ideas help us understand human creativity, whether computers could do things that at least appear creative, whether a computer could appear to recognise creativity, and whether computers themselves could ever really be creative; of the fourth she writes that "it is not a scientific question as the others are, but in part a philosophical worry about ‘meaning’ and in part a disguised request for a moral political decision" (as quoted by G. Franceschelli and M. Musolesi, *Creativity and Machine Learning: A Survey*, arXiv 2104.02726, citing the second edition). The film's "not for science alone to settle ... partly about what we mean, and partly a choice we'll have to make" is that sentence in plain words; the book itself wasn't read, so the line is kept as the survey quotes it. "A model can combine ideas and explore a style" is a description of what anyone can see a model do, not a claim that it can't change a style's rules: the third card is left as it was, neither lit nor struck out.
- **"A life, an intention, a reason to make it."** The plan's words for what a person brings that a mix may not; "may not", as the plan says. Clark's reason, his father, is from the MJA and IEEE (above).
- **The worm.** J. G. White, E. Southgate, J. N. Thomson and S. Brenner, "The structure of the nervous system of the nematode *Caenorhabditis elegans*", *Philosophical Transactions of the Royal Society B* 314 (1986), 1–340: the 302 neurons of the hermaphrodite and their connections, reconstructed from electron micrographs. Later maps filled its gaps, so the film says "the wiring ... had been mapped", not "every connection". The worm is about a millimetre long.
- **OpenWorm.** An open science project founded in January 2011 to build a simulation of *C. elegans* (S. Szigeti and others, *Frontiers in Computational Neuroscience* 8, 2014, 137; G. P. Sarma and others, *Phil. Trans. R. Soc. B* 373, 2018, 20170382). The *State of Brain Emulation Report 2025* (arXiv 2510.15745) says its current implementation can demonstrate basic locomotion, while matching behaviours such as chemotaxis, thermotaxis and learning remains a goal. The film's "It wriggles, but not much more" says that; "a map doesn't say how each connection works, or when" is from C. I. Bargmann and E. Marder, "From the connectome to brain function", *Nature Methods* 10 (2013), 483–490, which asks "what information is needed beyond connectivity diagrams" and stresses "neuronal dynamics and neuromodulation". The colon links the two as the film's plain reading, not as OpenWorm's own diagnosis.
- **The fly.** S. Dorkenwald and others, "Neuronal wiring diagram of an adult brain", *Nature* 634 (2024), 124–138: the whole brain of an adult female fruit fly, 139,255 neurons and about 50 million chemical synapses (FlyWire). P. K. Shiu and others, "A *Drosophila* computational brain model reveals sensorimotor processing", *Nature* 634 (2024), 210–219: a leaky integrate-and-fire model of the whole central brain, built from "only synapse-level connectivity and predicted neurotransmitter identity"; "activation of sugar-sensing or water-sensing gustatory neurons in the computational model accurately predicts neurons that respond to tastes and are required for feeding initiation", and its further predictions were tested and confirmed "by optogenetic activation and behavioural studies". The film's "predicted which ones answer to sugar" is the first of these; the green tick joins model and fly because the predictions matched known experiments.
- **"We have about 86 billion."** F. A. C. Azevedo and others, *Journal of Comparative Neurology* 513 (2009), 532–541: about 86 billion neurons in the adult human brain.
- **"It was made for speech, and to many people with implants, music still sounds poor."** C. J. Limb and A. T. Roy, "Technological, biological, and acoustical constraints to music perception in cochlear implant users", *Hearing Research* 308 (2014), 13–26: "the ability to perceive music remains limited for many cochlear implant users"; the devices "have been optimized for speech comprehension", and users have "fundamental disruptions in perception of pitch, melody, and harmony". Rod sang along to a tune he knew, which fits: a familiar song can be recognised from its rhythm and words; the film says "many", not all. "Did part of an ear's work, but not as an ear does": ten electrodes for thousands of cells, and pulses for sound.
- **Searle, 1980.** J. R. Searle, "Minds, brains, and programs", *Behavioral and Brain Sciences* 3 (1980), 417–457: "No one supposes that computer simulations of a five-alarm fire will burn the neighborhood down or that a computer simulation of a rainstorm will leave us all drenched. Why on earth would anyone suppose that a computer simulation of understanding actually understood anything?" The film quotes "will leave us all drenched" and says the rest plainly.
- **"Others say a mind is what a brain does, so a perfect copy would be one."** Computational functionalism, "the thesis that performing computations of the right kind is necessary and sufficient for consciousness", which Butlin and others (below) call "a mainstream—although disputed—position in philosophy of mind". One of the leading scientific theories disagrees with it: integrated information theory is "not compatible with computational functionalism" (Butlin and others), and holds that a computer that only simulates a brain need not be conscious (G. Tononi and C. Koch, *Phil. Trans. R. Soc. B* 370, 2015, 20140167). "Nobody knows who's right."
- **"Why does it feel like anything to be us?"** T. Nagel, "What is it like to be a bat?", *The Philosophical Review* 83 (1974), 435–450: an organism has conscious states "if and only if there is something that it is like to be that organism". The question is in his words; he isn't named, for length.
- **Chalmers, 1995.** D. J. Chalmers, "Facing up to the problem of consciousness", *Journal of Consciousness Studies* 2 (1995), 200–219: "The really hard problem of consciousness is the problem of experience"; "Why should physical processing give rise to a rich inner life at all? It seems objectively unreasonable that it should, and yet it does."
- **The two theories, in plain words.** Integrated information theory: consciousness corresponds to how much a system's causal structure is integrated, unified and irreducible to its parts (the film: "how tightly a system's parts work as one"). Global neuronal workspace theory: consciousness is information broadcast across the brain, through a "workspace" that makes it available to all its systems at once (the film: "news shared across the whole brain at once"). The film names neither, for plain words; both are named on the page.
- **The 2025 test.** The Cogitate Consortium and others, "Adversarial testing of global neuronal workspace and integrated information theories of consciousness", *Nature* 642 (2025), 133–142: a preregistered adversarial collaboration, its predictions agreed by the theories' proponents in advance, with 256 participants and three methods (fMRI, MEG and intracranial EEG); its results were "substantially challenging key tenets of both theories" (for integrated information theory, no sustained synchronization within the back of the brain; for the workspace theory, no "ignition" when a stimulus ended, and limited representation of some features in the front of the brain). The film's "challenged key parts of each" is that phrase. Left out for length: the 1998 bet between Christof Koch and David Chalmers, settled in Chalmers's favour at the meeting in New York in June 2023 where the test's first results were shown.
- **Llinás's rhythms.** R. Llinás and D. Paré, "Of dreaming and wakefulness", *Neuroscience* 44 (1991), 521–535: waking and dreaming sleep as "fundamentally equivalent brain states", consciousness as "fundamentally a closed-loop property"; R. Llinás and U. Ribary, "Coherent 40-Hz oscillation characterizes dream state in humans", *PNAS* 90 (1993), 2078–2081: measured in people, a coherent rhythm of about 40 cycles a second in waking and in dreaming, much reduced in deep sleep, which they proposed provides the "temporal binding" for "the unity of cognitive experience". In *I of the Vortex* (2001, p. 94, as quoted by Sporns, *Networks of the Brain*, 2011): "the waking state is a dreamlike state (in the same sense that dreaming is a wakelike state) guided and shaped by the senses". The film's "He thought this rhythm ties what we sense into one moment" is his proposal, said as his. Llinás was born in Bogotá in 1934 (the book's catalogue data); the film says "from Colombia" and gives no dates.
- **What Llinás said about quantum effects, exactly.** The plan asked for this. In *I of the Vortex: From Neurons to Self* (MIT Press, 2001), in the chapter on qualia, read through Open Library's search inside the scanned edition (archive.org item iofvortexfromneu0000llin), page not shown: "At this point there are a few possible scenarios that we can consider. One, as many people believe, is that qualia represent a very profound event in neuronal function dealing with quantum mechanical structures of neurons that include the detailed organization of microtubules and microfilaments. This of course opens up a new, previously unexplored area of neuroscience that I won't explore here, for I sincerely doubt it is a plausible scenario worth pursuing at any serious level. The reason for its dismissal is that the neuronal elements that support sensory activation seem to be quite similar to those that support motor activity." He doesn't name Penrose or Hameroff there; the book's other "quantum" is "a quantum of cognition", his name for the 40-a-second cycle as a unit of time. In 2007, asked by the Colombian magazine *Cambio* what his scientific obsession was: "Las bases físicas de la subjetividad. Yo sé cuáles son las bases físicas de muchos aspectos biológicos, pero no sé cuál es el efector físico que produce sentimientos. Tienen que ser unas células, pero ¿qué particularmente? ¿Filamentos? ¿Microtubos? ¿Mecánica cuántica? ... ¿cómo se generan el verde, el deseo o el amor?" (as reproduced at sindioses.org, read 3 November 2026). In English, the film's translation: "The physical basis of subjectivity. I know the physical basis of many biological things, but I don't know what physical effector produces feelings. It has to be some cells, but what in particular? Filaments? Microtubules? Quantum mechanics? ... How are green, desire or love made?" So the film says both: in 2001 he "sincerely doubted the quantum idea"; in 2007 "he said he didn't know what in our cells produces feeling", and quotes his three questions in English. The on-screen card quotes the 2001 sentence in full, so nothing is cut from it.
- **Penrose and Hameroff, and the debate.** S. Hameroff and R. Penrose, "Consciousness in the universe: a review of the 'Orch OR' theory", *Physics of Life Reviews* 11 (2014), 39–78, the theory first set out in the mid-1990s: consciousness arising from quantum computations in microtubules, ended by a quantum gravity "objective reduction". Penrose is a physicist (Nobel Prize in Physics, 2020, for black holes); Hameroff an anaesthesiologist. Critics: M. Tegmark, "Importance of quantum decoherence in brain processes", *Physical Review E* 61 (2000), 4194–4206, estimated decoherence in about 10⁻¹³ to 10⁻²⁰ seconds, against neural times of about 10⁻³ to 10⁻¹ seconds (the film: "far too fast to matter"). Replies: S. Hagan, S. R. Hameroff and J. A. Tuszyński, *Physical Review E* 65 (2002), 061901, recalculated much longer times for their own model. Tests since: an underground test at Gran Sasso ruled out the simplest, parameter-free version of the Diósi–Penrose collapse model (S. Donadi and others, *Nature Physics* 17, 2021, 74–78); rats given a drug that steadies microtubules took about a minute longer to lose consciousness under an anaesthetic (S. Khan and others, *eNeuro* 11, 2024, ENEURO.0291-24.2024); and networks of tryptophan in microtubules showed a quantum optical effect, superradiance (N. S. Babcock and others, *Journal of Physical Chemistry B* 128, 2024, 4035–4046). None shows that consciousness is quantum; the film says "the tests go on".
- **Butlin and others, 2023.** P. Butlin, R. Long and 17 others, *Consciousness in Artificial Intelligence: Insights from the Science of Consciousness* (arXiv 2308.08708, August 2023): nineteen authors, from neuroscience, philosophy and computer science; fourteen "indicator properties" derived from recurrent processing theory, global workspace theory, higher-order theories, attention schema theory and predictive processing, plus agency and embodiment (integrated information theory not considered, being incompatible with their assumption); "Our analysis suggests that no current AI systems are conscious, but also suggests that there are no obvious technical barriers to building AI systems which satisfy these indicators." Their method assumes computational functionalism "as a working hypothesis". The film's "None of the systems seemed conscious" is "suggests that no current AI systems are conscious"; "drawn from the leading theories", because the five are among the leading ones, not all of them. "AI" is said as "AI" (the module's say rule).
- **Descartes, 1637.** *Discourse on the Method*, Part V (Leiden, 1637; J. The film's "as even the dullest people can" is Veitch's "men of the lowest grade of intellect". He lived in the Dutch Republic when he published it; the clockwork figure is drawn because Part V speaks of automata.

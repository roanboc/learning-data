## The promise

Every line passes two tests: someone new to data work can follow it, and a data manager, a data architect or an analytics engineer finds nothing wrong in it. Its idea: when an agent writes the first draft, the work doesn't go away, it moves to either side of the draft, and the skills that grow are a person's. Before the draft, framing the work (the decision it serves, the words to use, what counts) and saying exactly what's wanted; after it, checking what comes back against something you know is true, deciding, and answering for it. A draft is cheap; judgement isn't. What an agent can't take from a person is the decision, and the responsibility for it.

**Takeaway:** "Let the agent draft; you frame, check and decide."

## The story in one paragraph

New York, May 1997: the world chess champion, Garry Kasparov, plays a six-game match against a computer built for chess, and loses, the first time a machine has beaten a world champion in a match. Many fear what that means for people who spend years learning the game, and many of whom earn their living by it: if a machine plays better than the best of them, what is their skill worth? A year later, in León, Kasparov tries another way: he and Veselin Topalov each play with a computer at hand, and with the machine checking every tactic, Kasparov finds he can give his thinking to the plan. In 2005, an online tournament goes further: anyone may enter, in teams, with any computers. Grandmasters enter with their computers, and a supercomputer built for chess enters on its own. Two amateur players from the United States win, with three computers: from their own experience they choose which moves are worth a look, compare what each program says, send the programs deeper where it matters, and then decide. In the final they beat a team led by a grandmaster; the supercomputer, alone, doesn't reach the last eight. Kasparov's lesson: weak players, with a machine and a better process, beat a strong computer alone, and, more remarkably, strong players whose process was worse. At the university, a year from now, on Wednesday 7 April 2027, Ivy Reyes, a graduate data analyst in her sixth week, asks the team's agent for a first model of room use, for Graham Ellis's question about a new teaching building. In a minute it's there, tidy, with four tests that pass and an answer that sounds sure. Before she opens a pull request she reads its notes to the end, and one line stops her: a booked hour counts as a used hour. In February, Graham had said used means people in the room. Against the sensors, one booked hour in five had nobody in it, and Graham would have weighed a new building on bookings. Nobody was careless: in timetabling, use often means booked, and the agent said what it assumed; it had been given the task, but not the decision or the words. So she frames the work: she writes room use into the glossary, Graham approves it there as its owner, and she asks again with the decision, the words and what counts. The new draft keeps booked and used apart; she checks it against one room's week, worked out by hand, and all fifty hours match. Jun reviews and approves her pull request, Graham is told what it changes, and at stand-up the next morning Ivy says what she nearly missed. Helena thanks her for saying it, and the team starts asking that way too.

**Where the film adopts, extends or differs.** It adopts Kasparov's lesson as the film's turn: a better process beats both a stronger machine and stronger players with a worse process. It extends it from the board to data work: the "process" is named as the skills that grow on either side of a draft (framing the work before it; checking, deciding and answering for it after), and the film shows each in Ivy's day. It differs from the common reading of advanced chess, that people and machines simply add up: the film says the players' skill still counted, and that what counted most was how they worked with the machines; and it never says the agent was wrong to assume, since it said what it assumed, as an agent should. The *Future of Jobs Report* is used for what employers say they seek, not as a forecast of jobs.

**Practice, from the module's plan.** A practitioner will recognise the draft that looks finished and sounds sure; tests that pass because they check the data's form, not what a word means; a word, such as "used", that means one thing to the person who asked and another in the field the data comes from; the definition that lives in one person's head until it's written into the glossary; and the reviewer who asks how the author checked, not only what the code does. The advice is the brief's: give the agent the decision and the definitions, not only the task; check the draft against something you know is true; read what it assumed; never approve your own work; keep the reasoning with the result.

### 3 · What it assumed · 3:48–4:52

**Narration.** At the end, the agent lists what it assumed. One line stops her: a booked hour counts as a used hour. In February, Graham had said what used means: people in the room, not a booking. The tests pass because they check the form of the data: no room counted twice, no hour left out. No test can know what Graham means.

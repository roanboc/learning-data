## The promise

Every line passes two tests: someone who has never started a job like this can follow it, and an experienced data manager or enterprise architect finds nothing wrong in it. It is the module's discovery film: before designing anything, find out what the organisation decides, what data it trusts and what slows it down. Listen to people, asking everyone the same few questions where they work; read what's already written; count from the systems themselves, not only from opinions. Then write the current state down, in the five layers architects use (strategy, business, information, application, technology), each on a page with its picture, and turn every finding into an opportunity, never into who failed. Send people back what you heard. **Ask people, count from the systems, and write down what is.**

## The story in one paragraph

Accra, in the early 1930s, on the Gold Coast, then a British colony, now Ghana. Cicely Williams, a doctor from Jamaica, cares for the Ga people's children, with their mothers. Children of one to four were falling sick: their hands and feet swelled, their skin peeled, and they wasted away; unless it was treated early, it nearly always killed them. Many doctors took it for pellagra, a disease of a missing vitamin, and treated it that way, and children kept dying; it wasn't in any medical book Williams had. So she listened, to mothers and grandmothers, to nurses and healers. For three years, nobody said its name in front of her, because the word was feared. Then the mothers told her: *kwashiorkor*, the disease of the displaced child, the one moved from the breast when the next baby comes, onto little but maize. She described it in 1933 and gave it the mothers' word in 1935; three children she saw early, fed milk, eggs and fruit, each went home apparently cured, and doctors everywhere still use the mothers' name. A year from now, at the university, a request for data takes 41 working days, on average, to be used, and nobody sees the whole picture. In February 2027 Helena Novak, the new data manager, spends six weeks listening: 31 conversations, the same three questions each time (what do you decide, what data do you trust, what slows you down), in the places people work, and their answers written down in their own words. She reads the strategy to 2030, the digital strategy and an earlier assessment's twelve decisions, and counts from the systems: 41 working days from the ask to first use; the old warehouse's 38 reports, 17 not opened in a year, 21 used by about 260 people, 310,000 Australian dollars a year, its licence ending on 31 December. Eleven ways to ask, three copies of the staff list, about 40 people doing data work in spreadsheets; and something that works, the graduation list's Thursday fifteen minutes, which took reprinted certificates from 41 to 3. She writes the current state in five layers, each a page with its picture, on Noor's map, and every finding as an opportunity: seven of them on Monday 15 March, with a summary sent back to all 31. The mothers of Accra knew; so did the people who live with data, and the current state, written in their words, is one they recognise.

## The reference, and practice

The module's reference is DAMA-DMBOK as the map of the job's areas, enterprise architecture's discover, define and plan as the spine (TOGAF's method and ArchiMate's words), and lean and agile delivery as the rhythm (the module's *Decided* table records why). This film is *discover*, and names two bodies of knowledge once each:

| Body of knowledge | What it adds | Where the film uses it |
|---|---|---|
| TOGAF's method (the TOGAF Standard, The Open Group) | The current state written down before the target, as the *baseline*: business, data, application and technology architectures, each described from evidence, so the gaps to the target can be found | *Five layers*: "the current state, or, as TOGAF's method calls it, the baseline." |
| The author's five layers (10 November 2026), on ArchiMate 4 (The Open Group, 2026) | Strategy, business, information, application and technology: TOGAF's four architectures with ArchiMate's strategy layer above them; ArchiMate has no information layer, and keeps what the data is about in its business layer (business objects) and the data itself in its application layer (data objects); "visualization everywhere", so each layer is a page with its picture | *Five layers*, each layer said and drawn; the line on ArchiMate, said once |
| Lean thinking, the Toyota Production System's *genchi genbutsu* | "Go and see": the facts gathered at the real place where the work happens, from the real thing | *Three questions*: "Lean thinking calls it go and see, genchi genbutsu: the real place, and the real thing." |

**Where the film adopts, extends or differs.** It adopts TOGAF's order (the baseline before the target, both before the gaps) and its point that the baseline is described from evidence. It extends it with the practice the module's plan asks for: three kinds of evidence (people, what's written, the systems), the same questions asked of everyone so answers can be compared, and a current state sent back to the people who gave it. It differs on weight and words: TOGAF's phases, deliverables and viewpoints aren't named; ArchiMate 4 calls its layers *domains*, and the films keep "layers", as *The map before the data* does (its *Decided* row of 17 October 2026), since "domain" already means a part of the organisation with its own meaning. The film keeps information as a layer of its own, as the author does: for a data manager, what the data is about and where it's born is the layer the job is about. Maturity models (DCAM, the Data Management Maturity model) aren't used for discovery here: the module names them only as checklists, never as the plan.

**Practice (the module's plan, the author's deck as input, and practitioners).** A new data manager is told everything at once; what works is a few questions asked the same way of everyone, in their own place, and the answers written in their words. Opinions show where it hurts, and the systems' own numbers show how much: a practitioner will recognise the tracker's days from ask to first use and the reports nobody opens, found in the warehouse's own logs. Something always already works, and a discovery that doesn't name it can break it. A finding written as blame closes doors, and the same finding written as an opportunity opens them. Sending the summary back is how people know they were heard, and how its mistakes get found. The author's own words (10 November 2026): design every layer, strategy to technology, and "visualization everywhere is key".

**What the film leaves to others.** Hearing the question behind one request is the leading data people module's (*The question behind the request*, named by title now that it is on main); designing the target is *Design it simple*'s, the next film; the current state drawn on the architecture map is *Today, and where it's going*'s, in *The map before the data*; the graduation list's Thursdays are the data quality module's (*Small, every week*, named only once it's on main).

- **The 1933 paper,** read page by page from its scan in PubMed Central (PMC1975318), 9 and 10 November 2026: Cicely D. Williams, "A nutritional disease of childhood associated with a maize diet", *Archives of Disease in Childhood* 1933, 8: 423 to 433, "From the Children's Hospital, Accra, Gold Coast Colony". Page 423: "About twenty cases have been seen in the last year"; "oedema, chiefly of the hands and feet, followed by wasting; diarrhoea; irritability; sores ...; and desquamation of areas of the skin"; "children of either sex, between one and four years old"; "uniformly fatal unless treated early"; "the only supplementary food consisted of preparations of maize, namely arkasa and kenki"; "They breast-feed one child until the next is born, and the deposed baby is still nursed sometimes." - **Listening, and three years.** Geoffrey Cannon, "Cicely Williams. The editorial "The significance of 'kwashiorkor'", *World Nutrition* March 2014, 5(3): 208 to 210 (read 9 November 2026): its conventional identification as a protein deficiency disease "clearly is not correct".

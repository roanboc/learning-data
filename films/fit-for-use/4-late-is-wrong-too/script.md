## The promise

Every line passes two tests: someone who has never measured data quality can follow it, and a data engineer or a data quality lead finds nothing wrong in it. It closes the series with the dimension *Rules that run*'s scorecard left in pencil: timeliness. Late data looks complete. Every row in it is right, and every check that reads the rows passes, so nothing says it's late. Whether it's too late depends on the use, so agree with the people who use the data how fresh each use needs it, and let the strictest use set the level. Say how old the data is wherever it's shown, for each source, counted from its records and never from when their file arrived. Every record has three dates: when it happened, when its source saved it, and when its file arrived. The first is the one that matters, and the hardest to read as data is loaded; the last is the easiest, and says the least; so the age goes by the second, the date every load already uses to find what's new. And check that age where it starts, at each source. Old records can't say why they're old, whether no file came or the file brought nothing new, so the file's arrival is still checked, as a second alarm for a source that goes silent: it measures no age, and it says why. Warn first, so the people who need to know hear at once, then stop the step that can't be undone, the send, never the data. **Agree how fresh it must be, and always say how old it is.**

## The story in one paragraph

November 1970: a cyclone comes ashore on the coast of East Pakistan, now Bangladesh, in the evening, with the high tide. Forecasters warn of danger, and as it nears, the radio broadcasts a great danger signal; but on the low islands it means little, and survivors said later they took signal number one to be the worst. Most people know a storm is coming, yet almost nobody takes shelter, and that night the storm surge sweeps over the islands: at least 300,000 people die, perhaps half a million. A warning people can't read, or don't act on, is no warning. In 1972 the Bangladesh Red Cross Society starts the Cyclone Preparedness Programme, run with the government from 1973: volunteers from the coast's own villages carry each warning the last mile, house to house with megaphones, and lead people to shelters, and their flags rise with the danger, one, then two, then three, a signal the whole village can read. In November 2007, Cyclone Sidr strikes the same coast with winds as strong as 1970's, though at low tide. Warnings have gone out for days, more than 40,000 volunteers carry them, a million and a half people are in shelters, and about 3,400 die: still too many, and about one for every hundred in 1970. Data has a last mile too. At the university, on Wednesday night, 17 June 2026, the student system's nightly file doesn't come: a full disk on its side holds it. At three, the night's update builds the graduation list from what was there, the file of the night before: 689 graduates, and every check green, even the check against the exam boards' own lists, which come in the same transfer and were a day old too. Missing: 23 graduates the boards approved on Wednesday. On Thursday at 8 am, with the list due at the printer at five, Sam Okafor, who runs the platform, sees in Databricks' monitoring that the student system's tables are late, and dbt's freshness check says thirty hours. The level there was, a warning after a day, was set by the builders, agreed with nobody and never run at night. Malia Fonoti, who sends the list, holds the send; the file arrives at 11:40, the list is built again at 12:10 with 712 graduates, her team checks the 23 new names, and it goes at 3:20. The December before, six graduates were left off, and nobody saw. On Monday, Malia and Sam meet Mei Tanaka, who owns the list: the certificates need yesterday's awards on the list when it goes to the printer, seating the Great Hall can use a list a few days old, and Planning's count for the government is fine weeks later. The strictest sets the level, a day old at most at the send, agreed by Malia, owned by Mei, and written beside the list's columns in dbt as the first timeliness rule. From then on the list says how old it is. Old, counted from when? Every record has three dates: when it happened (the board approved the award on Wednesday), when the student system saved it (that afternoon), and when its file arrived (Thursday, at 11:40). The first is the one that matters, but each table says it in its own way, and when no board meets there's none to read. The last is the easiest, and says the least: for December's list, a file came on time, full of the day before's records. So the list goes by the second, the date every load already uses to find what's new: every row carries its sources' age, the newest record each one had saved, shown first of all in the printer's file. Sam checks the same date at each source: older than 24 hours, the check fails. But old records can't say why: did no file come, or did the file bring nothing new? At three on that Thursday in June, and at three on Thursday 13 November 2025, the student system's newest record was 25 hours old; in June no file had come, and in November one had, on time. So the file's arrival is still checked, as a second alarm: no file tonight means the source has gone silent. Each night the update runs both checks first; if either fails, Sam and Malia hear at once, and the list is still built, saying its age. The send to the printer is a job too: it checks the list's own age, and waits if the check fails. The data isn't stopped; the send is. The volunteers can't stop a storm: they make sure the warning arrives in time, in words people understand. A list can't make a late file come: it can say how old it is, and the send can wait until it's fresh.

## The reference, and practice

The module's reference is DAMA-DMBOK's data quality chapter as the map, with Danette McGilvray's *Ten Steps* and Thomas Redman's practice as the spine, and the quality movement underneath (the module's *Decided* table records why). For this film, six bodies of knowledge, none taught:

| Body of knowledge | What it adds | Where the film uses it |
|---|---|---|
| DAMA's timeliness (DAMA UK's *The six primary dimensions for data quality assessment*, 2013; DAMA-DMBOK, second edition, 2017) | Timeliness as a dimension of its own: data that represents reality from the point in time it's needed. Its measure is a time difference, counted from "the time the real world event being recorded occurred" | *How fresh, for what*: the six dimensions as *Rules that run* left them, timeliness in pencil until its first rule. *Say how old it is*: the first of a record's three dates, the one that matters |
| ISO/IEC 25012:2008's currentness | Data "of the right age" in a specific context of use: how old is fine depends on the use | Not named; it's the film's "how fresh each use needs it" |
| Service levels, as site reliability engineering and data contracts write them | A level agreed with the people who depend on a service, measured, checked, with what happens when it's missed decided in advance | *How fresh, for what* and *Warn first, then stop the send*: one level, agreed, owned and checked |
| The Open Data Contract Standard (Bitol, a Linux Foundation project; the project pins v3.1.0) | A quality rule with the dimension `timeliness`, and a product's latency as `slaProperties` | *How fresh, for what*: the rule in the list's contract |
| dbt's source freshness, Databricks' data quality monitoring and Lakeflow Jobs (their docs, checked on the day) | How old each source is, from the date column a project names (`loaded_at_field`: when a row was loaded, or when its source last updated it); which tables are late; a data test, for what an age can't say: whether a file arrived tonight; a job's tasks, the condition each runs on, and whom a failed task tells | *Thirty hours* and *Warn first, then stop the send* |
| Incremental loads and change data capture, as loading tools write them (a watermark column, a sequencing column) | The date a source saved each record, by its own clock, is what a load goes by to find what's new: every source has it, in the same form | *Say how old it is*: the second of the three dates, the one the list's age is counted from |

**Where the film adopts, extends or differs.** It adopts DAMA's timeliness as a dimension of its own, and ISO's idea that the right age depends on the use: one list, three clocks. It extends them with the practice service levels teach: the level is agreed with the people who use the data, owned by the person who answers for it, checked by a machine every night, and what happens when it's missed is decided in advance, warn first and then stop the step that can't be undone. It differs from monitoring-first teaching: a platform can see that a table is late, as Databricks' monitoring does, but only its users can say when late is too late, so the agreed level comes first and the tools check it. On the date an age is counted from, it follows the author's answer (12 November 2026). DAMA's reference for timeliness is the time the event happened, the business date, and that's the date that matters; but it's hard to read as data is loaded, since each table says it in its own way and on a day when nothing happened there's none to read. So the age is counted from the next best date, the one the source saved the record on, which incremental loads and change data capture already go by, and never from the file's arrival alone: a file can come on time with old records. The arrival keeps one job, a point the author agreed the same day: old records look the same whether no file came or a file came with nothing new in it, so the film still checks that a file arrived tonight, as a second alarm beside the age, for a source that goes silent. It measures no age and sets no level; it says why. It's the signal *Thirty hours*' monitoring reads too, when each table was last updated, and there it was how Sam saw the late tables. The business date is measured afterwards, record by record (in the project, `analyses/profiling/profile_late_changes.sql`, and the list's `added_on` beside `conferred_on`). And it uses one level where dbt offers two: a dbt warning exits as a success, so it tells nobody; the warning is a failed task that tells two people, while the build goes on.

**Practice (the module's plan and brief, 5 November 2026; the author, 12 November 2026).** The author's own practice, in his words: "relevant dates as usual are business dates, so a file could have arrived with stale data, so the important thing are the records and ideally the business dates but that's hard to determine in ingestion", so "system transaction dates (generally chosen for CDC and incremental ingestions) would be the next best fit to check Timeliness in DAMA". And a point put to him on that answer, which he agreed the same day ("Agreed."): "Record dates are the right measure, but they can't tell "nothing arrived" from "nothing happened". I'd keep the arrival check as a second alarm for a source that goes silent, and use record dates for staleness." The brief asks for three habits a practitioner will recognise: agree how fresh each use needs the data, say "as of" wherever it's shown, and check freshness at the source, warning first, then stopping the send, not the data. A practitioner will recognise the night, too: a list built on time from yesterday's file, every check green; a freshness level set by the builders as a default, agreed with nobody who uses the data, and run by hand when someone thinks of it; a warning that goes to a log nobody reads; and a morning spent finding out why the data is old, whether the file never came or came with nothing new in it, before anyone knows whom to call. The film's numbers are believable ones: a nightly file a morning late, a level of 24 hours at the send that a nightly file meets, and a list that waits three hours for its file and still goes before five.

**What the film leaves to others.** How fresh data must be decides how it moves: that's *Hot, warm, cold*'s (*Modern data basics*), named once. A stale number labelled, and why stale and labelled beats fresh and wrong, is *Too good to be true*'s, named once. The scorecard and its dimensions are *Rules that run*'s; a data contract between systems, and its last good data, are *Silent change*'s; measuring and improving the list week after week is *Quality as a habit*'s, the next series.

### 1 · The last mile · 0:00–2:20

- **The 1970 cyclone.** Frank, N. L., and S. A. Husain, "The deadliest tropical cyclone in history?", *Bulletin of the American Meteorological Society* 52(6), June 1971: landfall on the night of 12 November 1970, the surge on a high tide; "over 90% of the people in the disaster area knew about the storm; yet, less than 1% sought refuge"; estimates near a million dead called unrealistic. The Associated Press, December 1970: survivors on the islands took signal number one, the lowest, to be the worst (the signals then ran from one to ten, and the radio broadcast the great danger signal). The film says "Forecasters warn of danger, and as it nears, the radio broadcasts a great danger signal", and never that no warning came.
- **The toll.** The World Meteorological Organization, "World's deadliest tropical cyclone was 50 years ago" (12 November 2020): 300,000 to 500,000 people died. The film says "at least three hundred thousand, perhaps half a million". The CPP's own history says about a million; Frank and Husain's estimate is lower.
- **The programme.** The Cyclone Preparedness Programme's own pages (cpp.gov.bd, its history updated 30 December 2024) and the Bangladesh Red Crescent Society's (bdrcs.org, *History* and *Cyclone Preparedness Programme*), read 5 November 2026: started in 1972 by the Bangladesh Red Cross Society, its name until 1988, with the League of Red Cross Societies (19,270 volunteers by August 1972); the government made it a joint programme in 1973 (approved 28 July 1973). Volunteers from the coast's own villages, in units, carry the warnings house to house with megaphones and hand sirens, raise flags, and lead people to shelters. The sources differ on the flags for the lower signals and agree on three for great danger, so the film says "one, then two, then three" and no signal numbers. Today: 77,360 volunteers, half of them women (the CPP's table of 1 September 2026; the Bangladesh Red Crescent says 76,020); not said in the film.
- **Sidr.** The Government of Bangladesh's *Cyclone Sidr in Bangladesh: Damage, Loss and Needs Assessment for Disaster Recovery and Reconstruction* (February and April 2008, pp. xvi and 5) and the Ministry of Food and Disaster Management's *Super Cyclone Sidr 2007* (February 2008, p. 17, its table dated 21 January 2008): landfall on the evening of 15 November 2007; 3,406 dead and 1,001 missing; about 3 million evacuated, 1.5 million in shelters. B. K. Paul, *Human Response to Cyclone Sidr* (Natural Hazards Center, quick response report, 20 August 2008): warnings five days ahead, the highest signal almost 27 hours before landfall. The IFRC's information bulletin no. 1 (15 November 2007) and Paul: about 42,700 to 44,000 volunteers, a third of them women; the film says "more than 40,000". The Bangladesh Meteorological Department lists Sidr's winds at 223 km/h and 1970's at 224 km/h ("winds as strong as 1970's"); Sidr struck at low tide. The government credits the warnings and shelters, the Sundarbans forest and the low tide, so the film credits no single cause. "About one for every hundred in 1970": 3,406 against at least 300,000.
- **The look of the coast.** Low islands where the Ganges, the Brahmaputra and the Meghna meet the Bay of Bengal; homesteads on raised earth; cyclone shelters of two storeys on pillars. Drawn for the film, with no person's likeness, no emblem, and nothing from Bengali art.
- **dbt.** dbt-core 1.12.5, the version the project pins, run on the project's receipts on 10 November 2026, and again on 12 November with their newest records: `dbt source freshness` reads `loaded_at_field`, writes each source's age to `target/sources.json` (`max_loaded_at_time_ago_in_s`), and exits 1 on `ERROR STALE` and 0 on PASS or WARN; a table's `freshness` is merged over its source's, and `warn_after: null` unsets the warning the table would take from it; freshness isn't run by `dbt build`. The docs, read 10 November 2026: *freshness* (docs.getdbt.com, last updated 12 October 2026): `warn_after` and `error_after`, each a `count` and a `period` (minute, hour or day); `loaded_at_field`; a source's freshness applies to its tables, and a table's own overrides it; `freshness: null` turns it off. Read again 12 November 2026 for the date it reads: `loaded_at_field` is the "column dbt queries to determine the most recent loaded timestamp", and its examples name both a load date (`_etl_loaded_at`) and a source's own date (`updated_at`); the page doesn't say which to prefer, so the choice is the project's, and the film's reads the newest record each source saved. *About dbt source command* (last updated 16 September 2026), written for dbt v2.0 and later, calls `dbt source freshness` a legacy subcommand beside a newer `dbt freshness`; the project runs dbt Core 1.12, where `dbt source freshness` is the command, and the film shows that. Neither page marks what the film uses with a release status. The second alarm is a singular data test on the receipts, tried on 12 November 2026 on the sample files: with the clock at 03:00 on 18 June 2026 it fails with one row, the student system, whose last file came at 02:00 on the 17th; at 03:00 on 13 November 2025 it passes; at 12:10 on 18 June 2026, after the late file, it passes. It's off unless a var turns it on (`enabled` in the tests' YAML), so `dbt build` never runs it, and `dbt parse --warn-error` stays clean.
- **Databricks' data quality monitoring.** *Data quality monitoring* (docs.databricks.com, last updated 11 September 2026) and *Anomaly detection* (last updated 2 November 2026), read 10 November 2026: anomaly detection checks each table's freshness (how recently it was updated) and completeness (the rows expected in the last 24 hours), keeps its results in `system.data_quality_monitoring.table_results`, and can alert. Read again 12 November 2026: "Freshness refers to how recently a table has been updated", from the table's history, and "event freshness", the age of the records' own dates, isn't supported in the current version; so the film's window shows when each table was last updated, and the age counted from the records is the list's and dbt's to say. Neither page gives anomaly detection or freshness a release status; the anomaly detection page marks four completeness features (percent null, completeness slicing and its setup, recently resolved incidents) as Beta, none of which the film uses or shows. The film's window is drawn at the level of what the product shows (each table, when it was last updated, late or on time), with Databricks' official logo; no screen is captured.
- **Lakeflow Jobs.** *Configure task dependencies* (docs.databricks.com, last updated 2 November 2026), read 10 November 2026: a task's *Run if* condition, *All succeeded* by default, and *All done*, which runs a task once its dependencies have run, whatever their outcome. *Add notifications on a job* (last updated 6 November 2026): notifications on a job's runs and on its individual tasks, for a failure among other events; job-level notifications aren't sent when failed tasks are retried, so the docs advise task notifications for a notice after every failed task; tasks are retried before failing fully, and a task's notices can be muted until the last retry, which the film doesn't do. *dbt task for jobs* (last updated 11 September 2026, read 5 November 2026): a job runs a dbt project as a task. None of the three pages marks what the film uses with a release status. The film's two jobs are drawn for it. The night's two checks are two tasks, neither depending on the other, and the build depends on both (*All done*), so each check runs whatever the other says and its notice names the one that failed; whether one dbt task with two commands goes on to the second after the first fails wasn't read in the docs, so the film doesn't lean on it.
- **The Open Data Contract Standard.** Its *Data Quality* page and its *Service-Level Agreement* page, latest, read 10 November 2026: a quality rule's `dimension` takes `timeliness`; `slaProperties` each have a `property` and a `value`, with `unit`, `element`, `driver` (regulatory, analytics or operational) and `description`, and latency is the property preferred to freshness. The project pins v3.1.0. A published open standard; no status to date.
- **A record's three dates,** read 12 November 2026, after the author's answer of that day. DAMA UK Working Group, *The Six Primary Dimensions for Data Quality Assessment* (October 2013, p. 10), on timeliness: its reference is "The time the real world event being recorded occurred", its measure a "Time difference"; its example is a change given on 1 June and entered in the student database on 4 June, a delay of 3 days against an agreed 2. The UK Government Data Quality Hub, *Meet the data quality dimensions* (24 June 2021): "Timeliness means different things for different uses." Microsoft Learn's overview of incremental copy in Azure Data Factory: "A watermark is a column that has the last updated time stamp or an incrementing key." Databricks' *AUTO CDC* pages (last updated 10 November 2026): changes are put in order by a sequencing column (`SEQUENCE BY`). The Open Data Contract Standard's own latency example counts from a transaction date (`element: tab1.txn_ref_dt`). The film's names for the three, business date, system date and load date, are the common ones; it says "when it happened", "when the student system saved it" and "when its file arrived", and shows the names once. That each source writes a heartbeat record as it makes its file, so its newest record is that night's even when nothing else changed, is the project's assumption; the film doesn't say it.

# Master and reference data · Kept, not cleaned: script

*The script of the ninth and last film of the series on master and reference data, working title The same one, everywhere: about 5½ minutes, in eleven chapters, in English. Written and built on 17 October 2026, from the plan the author approved that day ([the plan for films 2 to 9](https://claude.ai/code/artifact/b5341da1-808c-4b08-8441-42ab596feb1c), and film 9 in [the series' proposal](../proposal.md#9-kept-not-cleaned)); the times below are the built film's, and [Built](#built) lists what changed on the way. The eleventh chapter, a summary of the series and a bridge to data governance, was added on 20 October 2026 at the author's request. The title card follows chapter 1; the end card follows chapter 11. Made to read on a phone, like the other films: on-screen text at least 28 px in the frame, and the camera moves in on whatever the narration is about.*

## The promise

Every line passes two tests: anyone who has ever tidied a shared folder or a contact list, and watched it fill up again, can follow it; and an experienced master data manager finds nothing wrong in it. The film closes the series. A clean-up fixes the duplicates there are today, but not the way new ones are made, so they come back with every intake. A check where records are made, the search before create of film 7, stops most of them; the steward's queue catches the rest. A few measures, each with a limit agreed with its owner, show whether it's working, and an alert says when one passes its limit, pointing at a case, not at a person. An agent can draft a change, with its evidence; the owner decides. And the measures sit on the governance scorecard, beside every other domain's. Master data is a process, not a project. **A clean-up decays; prevention lasts.** Then, as the last film, it sums up the series in its four parts, each film's lesson in a few words, and shows master data as one of the things data governance looks after, which the next series takes up.

## The story in one paragraph

Around 256 BC, the governor Li Bing split the Min River in two, without a dam, to water the Chengdu plain. Its shape sends most of the river's gravel down the outer channel, and every dry season the inner channel is closed off and dug out, down to a mark buried in its bed: stone horses, it's said, at first, and later iron bars. Its rule is six characters long: dig the riverbed deep, keep the weir low. More than two thousand years later, it still waters the plain, because it was never cleaned out once and left: it's kept, every year. At the university, a clean-up project merged two thousand duplicate records of people in 2024. A year later there were eleven hundred new ones, because every intake made more: graduates came back to study, and people applied through two routes, each giving them a new number. Linh's second student number, in July 2025, was one of them. In 2026 the application starts searching before it creates a person, and new duplicates fall under fifty an intake, and stay there: like the river's shape, the search keeps most of them out, and Mei's queue digs out the rest. Mei's office has its own iron bars: five measures, each with a limit. One Monday an alert says the queue's oldest case has waited twelve days; it was waiting for HR, Mei rings them, and it's decided that morning. When the government's list of visa types adds one, an agent drafts its mapping, and the admissions office, the list's owner, approves it. Yusuf, who leads data governance, reads the same measures on his scorecard. A bank that cleans up every three years sees its duplicates come back every time, until one check at entry ends the cycle. In February 2027, Linh starts her PhD: a new role, on the person she already is, and not one of the intake's thirty-eight new duplicates. And at Dujiangyan, every spring, when the repairs are done, the water is let back in. To close the series, its nine films line up in their four parts, each with its lesson, and the whole line becomes one card on data governance's wheel, beside the four things master data needs from the rest of governance: someone who decides, one meaning for each word, access for a purpose, and rules that run. The next series follows Yusuf as he puts each of them in place, for all of the university's data.

## The look

Watercolour, as in every film of the series ([the script of *One person, many roles*](../1-one-person-many-roles/script.md#the-look)): daylight in soft washes on warm paper, pencil lines, and colours that mean something. Each role keeps its colour (student teal, applicant amber, researcher coral, tutor violet, alumna green); the person, as the master record, is the white of the paper in a pencil line, banded in her roles' colours. Crosses are red ink. The government's list wears the public sector's green-blue, and the bank its blue.

- **Dujiangyan** is painted in the series' own watercolour, not in a borrowed style: mountains in layered washes, the river in blue, the levee and its trees in pencil and wash, the plain as strips of field. The past is the same watercolour, with the year in the corner. The people who dig in the dry season are small figures in plain working clothes of hemp, belted at the waist, with cloths tied round their heads, and wooden spades and baskets: dressed for the work and the time, with no costume clichés. One language on screen: the rule is shown in English only, as a strip of paper, and its six characters are said, not drawn.
- **The measures** are gauges on paper, like the iron bars: each has a level and a mark at its limit, so "past its limit" reads without colour. A tick in green ink means within the limit, a red-ink mark past it, and a word says which.
- **No tool is named.** Two small cards on the dashboard say what happens: each measure has a test for its limit, and the platform works them out every night, keeps them and shows them (the series' rule of 20 October 2026: no film names a tool as the home of its rules).
- **People.** Mei (gold, the business side) in the registrar's office; Leila of the admissions office (gold) approving the list's change; Yusuf, the data governance lead (gold), at his scorecard, and again in the last chapter, beside data governance's wheel; Sam, Ben and Rosa (cyan, the technical side) with Mei in the clean-up's team of four; and Linh, at the end, as the person.

## Rigour to check before recording

Sources checked on 17 October 2026.

- **Dujiangyan's date and builder.** Built from about 256 BC under the Qin, by Li Bing, governor of Shu, and still in use; World Heritage since 2000 ([Wikipedia, Dujiangyan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dujiangyan); Sichuan Museum, [China's 2000-year-old irrigation system that's still in use today](https://artsandculture.google.com/story/8AVRiJGumXdeJg), "built late in the Qin Zhaowang (~256 BC - ~251 BC)"). Li Bing's appointment as governor is dated earlier (277 or 272 BC in the classical sources), so "the governor Li Bing" holds in 256 BC. "Without a dam": the system divides the river rather than damming it.
- **Its shape keeps most of the gravel out.** The levee's head splits the river into a deep, narrow inner channel and a wide, shallow outer one, which carries most of the silt and gravel; the Flying Sand spillway sends more back out (Wikipedia, above). A hydraulic study (*Hydraulic Principles of the 2,268-Year-Old Dujiangyan Project in China*) reports about 26 to 29% of the river's bed load entering the inner channel. The film says "most", without a figure, since popular sources give 80% and studies differ.
- **The yearly repair.** "Dujiangyan's annual repairs included systematic maintenance in the dry season – every winter and spring" (Sichuan Museum, above). It still happens: the irrigation district's yearly repair began on 14 November in 2022, with the rivers cut off in turns (found 17 October 2026 in Chinese reporting); the water-releasing ceremony opened the spring irrigation on 3 April 2026 ([Xinhua](https://www.news.cn/local/20260403/fe15843bb1af408e8c16019efd3b9ee6/c.html)). Wooden tripods (*macha*) and bamboo baskets of stones held the river back for the repairs, and were pulled away in spring ([National Geographic](https://www.nationalgeographic.com/travel/article/water-releasing-festival-china-travel-spd)); the film shows them as the old way, and doesn't claim they're used today.
- **The mark in the riverbed.** "Stone horses were buried in the middle of the inner stream to mark the proper depth of the riverbed … later replaced by iron rods or *wotie*" ([ADB](https://rksi.adb.org/publications/applying-ancient-wisdom-to-disaster-risk-mitigation/)). Chinese sources call the stone horses a tradition (传说), recorded in a Song-dynasty text, and list four iron bars at Fengqiwo in the inner channel, cast in 1576, 1864, 1927 and 1994; they're about 4 m long, their tops about 1.8 m under the riverbed, and the dredging stops when they show, because digging deeper would unsettle the bed ([卧铁, Baidu Baike](https://baike.baidu.com/item/%E5%8D%A7%E9%93%81/9173742); [Tencent News, 14 February 2025](https://news.qq.com/rain/a/20250214A07VP000), which dates the third bar to 1925–29). Hence "it's said", and on screen only the oldest bar's date, which the sources agree on.

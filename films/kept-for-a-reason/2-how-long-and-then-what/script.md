## The promise

Every line passes two tests: anyone who has ever cleared out a drawer can follow it, and a records manager or a privacy officer finds nothing wrong in it. In 1943, Washington's war offices were drowning in paper, kept because no one had the time or the job of deciding, and lost, the valuable with the useless, when an office closed. The answer was a schedule, written once for each kind of record: how long it's kept, and what's done with it then, decided before the papers pile up, with papers about an open claim kept until it's settled, and nothing destroyed any other way. Data needs the same: a record goes through three stages, live, then archived, then deleted, or, for the few, is kept for good; one schedule, by kind of record, written by each record's owner, says how long and then what, counting from an event; a hold stops the clock for a claim, an audit or a case; and the date holds for every copy. **Every record has an end date, decided before it's needed.**

## The story in one paragraph

Washington, 1943. The country is at war, and its new offices make papers faster than anyone can read them: orders, contracts, payrolls, letters, in steel cabinets that fill rooms, then floors. The head of the National Archives calls it a flood: every year, enough to fill a building the size of the Archives, and eighty percent of it of no lasting value. But a paper may be destroyed only if it's on a list that Congress approves, and each list covers only the papers already there, so most are simply kept, at a cost of millions in rent, staff and cabinets; and when the war's offices close, their papers will be shipped away or lost, the valuable with the useless, including papers someone will need to prove a claim or their service. On 7 July 1943 the President signs a law the National Archives drafted with a committee of Congress: each office writes a schedule, once, saying for each kind of record how long it's kept and what's done with it then; the Archivist and Congress approve it, and it covers papers not yet written. Papers about money claimed stay until the claim is settled, and no paper may be destroyed any other way. In 1944, in the Army Air Forces alone, the records program saves $927,000, about as much as the Archives' whole budget; today only 1 to 3 percent of the government's papers are kept for good, where families find their history and veterans prove their service. At the university, in October 2026, Ingrid Holm, who owns HR's records, finds the platform keeping everything: pay records back to 2016, applications from people never hired back to 2017, nothing ever deleted. A record goes through three stages (live, archived, deleted, or for the few, kept for good), and keeping everything is no safer than deleting without a plan. So the university writes one schedule, by kind of record: each owner decides their rows (Ingrid for HR's, Mei Tanaka for the registrar's), each with its reason from *A reason to keep it*, and Yusuf Demir, in data governance, keeps it. Pay records are kept 7 years after each contract ends, academic records for good, the tutors' mailing list until a person says no or 2 years without contact, applications from people not hired 12 months after the decision. The owners sign it on Wednesday 14 October 2026, and the first clean-up deletes the pay records of 2016 to 2018 and old applications. The clock starts at an event: Linh Nguyen's eight contracts give her pay records eight end dates, from 23 June 2030 to 20 November 2033, and when her last contract ends on Friday 20 November, nobody has to decide anything. A former tutor's claim about her pay in 2019 isn't settled, so the legal office places a hold on her 2019 records, due to go in January: the clean-up skips them until the claim is settled. Deleted too soon, a record breaks a promise; kept too long, it's a risk with nothing in return; so nothing is deleted outside the schedule, nothing stays past its date except under a hold, and the date holds for every copy. Like Washington in 1943, the university decided once, before the records piled up.

## The reference, and practice

The module's reference holds (its proposal, *The reference*): for privacy, the principles most privacy laws share, with Europe's GDPR named once as the best known, and ISO/IEC 27701. For this film's question, how long each record is kept and what happens then, the thread took these, named once here; the film teaches what they agree on and names none of them on screen.

| Body of knowledge | What it adds | Where the film uses it |
|---|---|---|
| ISO 15489-1:2016, *Records management: concepts and principles* | Records are appraised and kept under disposition authorities (in the US, records schedules): for each class of record, how long it's kept and what happens then, applied by rules, not case by case; disposition is carried out only under an authority, and documented | *One schedule, by kind of record* |
| ARMA International, *Generally Accepted Recordkeeping Principles* (2009) | Retention: "maintain its records and information for an appropriate time, taking into account legal, regulatory, fiscal, operational, and historical requirements"; Disposition: "secure and appropriate disposition for records that are no longer required to be maintained by applicable laws and the organization's policies" | *Three stages of a record*, *An end date for every record* |
| DAMA-DMBOK (2nd edition), *Document and content management* | The records lifecycle, retention schedules and disposition, and legal holds for e-discovery, as part of data management, not only of paper | *Three stages of a record*, *A hold stops the clock* |
| The US National Archives' records scheduling (today's guide, and the 1943 law in its own report) | Schedules, by kind of record, approved once and applied to records not yet made; most records temporary, a few permanent; a general schedule for records every office keeps, such as pay | The opening; *One schedule, by kind of record* |
| Privacy principles: GDPR Article 5(1)(e), *storage limitation*, and Article 17(3); ISO/IEC 27701's controls on retaining and disposing of personal data | Personal data kept "for no longer than is necessary for the purposes for which the personal data are processed"; erasure doesn't apply where a law requires the data, or for legal claims | *An end date for every record*; *A hold stops the clock* |

**Where the film differs, on purpose.** Records managers say "disposition", "temporary" and "permanent", and "archives" for the records kept for good. The film says "then what", "deleted" and "kept for good", and uses "archived" as data teams do, for a record kept cold, rarely read, before its end date (*Hot, warm, cold* uses the word the same way); "kept for good" is the archive in the records manager's sense. It doesn't teach appraisal methods, file plans or one country's retention law: the university's schedule is invented and believable (the module's `numbers.md`), and the law that asks for pay records is said only as "the law".

**Practice.** What practitioners recognise: retention schedules written for documents and paper that never reach the data platform, where every table keeps everything by default; "archive" used as a polite word for "forever"; retention counted from the day a row was written, when the schedule counts from an event (a contract's end, a decision); deletion done by hand, by whoever needs the space, with no record of why; holds sent by email from the lawyers and never reaching the jobs that delete; and copies, old versions and logs that nobody counted. And what works: a schedule in big buckets, by kind of record, that a business owner can read and sign; the event recorded as data, so the end date follows by itself; holds recorded where the clean-up reads them, each with its records, reason and who lifts it; deletion only by the schedule's job, logged; and the same date on every copy.

**The author's practice** is an open question while he's away (in *assumptions.md*): the film takes the practice above as the team's.

### 3 · Three stages of a record · about 2:45–3:55

**Narration.** October 2026. Ingrid Holm, who owns HR's records, looks at what the platform holds, with Yusuf Demir from data governance. ## Rigour to check before recording

Checked on 5 November 2026; the sources are listed below.

- **The flood.** "And in the war agencies and the armed services the volume of records being accumulated rose to a flood that would fill annually a building the size of the present National Archives Building" (the Archivist of the United States, *Tenth annual report*, for the year ending 30 June 1944, its introduction, read on the scanned page). The film's "The head of the National Archives calls it a flood" is the Archivist, Solon J. Buck, who isn't named or drawn. "Washington, 1943" sets the scene in the year of the law; the report describes the war years up to mid-1944.
- **Steel cabinets, four drawers each.** The report measures records in four-drawer file cabinets ("111,612 cubic feet of records—the equivalent of 15,945 four-drawer file cabinets", taken in that year, in its introduction). Steel was scarce: "When steel went to war and it was no longer possible to obtain further supplies of the steel equipment" (its section on the Archives' own storage); the film says only that keeping costs money in steel cabinets.
- **Eighty percent.** "It is estimated that of the records in existence and being created today in any typical agency of the Federal Government 80 percent have no lasting value. These, of course, should be destroyed as soon as they have served their purpose, but their destruction cannot be left to chance, expediency, or the whim of a file clerk" (its section on records administration).
- **Lists, pile by pile.** "Formerly records could be reported for disposal only on lists, which could not be made applicable to future accumulations of records" (records administration); the 1943 law's schedules end the "wasteful, repetitious reporting of successive accumulations of records of the same type" (its section on the disposal of records). The lists went to Congress, whose joint committee examined them, under the Act of 5 August 1939 (53 Stat. 1219), which the 1943 law repealed (section 16). "A list that Congress approves" says no more.
- **Simply kept, and the cost.** "Untold millions in rentals, salaries, and equipment costs have been spent by the Government in the maintenance of records that were kept because nobody had time to examine the ever-growing, ever-encroaching avalanche of them, because no official was charged with responsibility for their disposition, or because clerks felt that they had a vested interest in them" (records administration). The film's "millions in rent, staff and steel cabinets" is that sentence.
- **The valuable with the useless.** "Thousands of field offices will be closed very shortly after the war ends and unless disposal schedules are worked out now masses of unselected records will be shipped to Washington or lost, the valuable with the useless" (records administration, on the field offices). The film's two examples of the valuable are the law's own (claims, section 9) and the Archives' own today ("need to prove a veteran's military service", archives.gov, *About*).
- **The law.** "An Act to provide for the disposal of certain records of the United States Government", approved 7 July 1943 (57 Stat. 380; the report calls it the Federal Disposal Act, and it's often called the Records Disposal Act of 1943). "Drafted by National Archives officials in cooperation with the Bureau of the Budget and the House Committee on the Disposition of Executive Papers, it was signed by the President on July 7, 1943" (the disposal of records). The film says "with a committee of Congress" and leaves out the budget bureau, named here.
- **The schedule.** Section 3 lets each agency submit "schedules proposing the disposal after the lapse of specified periods of time of records of a specified form or character that either have accumulated in the custody of the agency or that may accumulate therein at any time after the submission of such schedules"; the Archivist submits them to Congress, whose joint committee reports (sections 4 to 7). The report: "the comprehensive, or retention and disposal, schedule, which covers all records of an agency and lists, series by series, how long records are to be kept and what is to be done with them at the end of that time" (records administration), and "the principle that the disposition of records should be determined before they become noncurrent" (the disposal of records). "Each office now writes a schedule, once" is the law's aim; the report is frank that by June 1944 "few agencies had taken full advantage of its provisions", with "tables and schedules" approved for 10,230 series of records (the introduction).
- **The two rules.** Section 9: "Records pertaining to claims and demands by the Government of the United States or against it, or to any accounts in which the Government of the United States is concerned, either as debtor or creditor, shall not be disposed of ... until such claims, demands, and accounts have been settled and adjusted in the General Accounting Office, except upon the written approval of the Comptroller General" (the film leaves out the exception). Section 15: "The procedures herein prescribed are exclusive and no records of the United States Government shall be alienated or destroyed except in accordance with the provisions of this Act."
- **$927,000.** "The dollars and cents value of such active programs is attested in the annual report of The Adjutant General of the War Department, who stated that in the Army Air Forces branch alone there were in the fiscal year 1944 known savings of $927,000 (a sum equal to the entire appropriation for the National Archives for that fiscal year)" (records administration; the figure read on the scanned page, since the text layer drops a digit). The Archives' appropriations for that year were $885,000 for salaries and expenses and $7,000 for printing, with $49,400 more for overtime (its statement of funds), so the film says "about as much". "The records program" is the Army Air Forces' records management, of which schedules were the core.
- **The useless on time, the valuable kept.** "By weeding out useless papers—or better still segregating them when they are filed—and eliminating them promptly, the recognition and preservation of valuable records is fostered" (the disposal of records).
- **1 to 3 percent today.** "Of all documents and materials created in the course of business conducted by the United States federal government, only 1%-3% are so important for legal or historical reasons that they are kept by us forever. Those valuable records are preserved and are available to you, whether you want to see if they contain clues about your family's history, need to prove a veteran's military service, or are researching a historical topic" (archives.gov, *About the National Archives*, read 5 November 2026).
- **The university's platform and the schedule.** Invented and believable, from the module's `numbers.md`: pay records 7 years after each contract ends, then deleted; academic records kept for good; the tutors' mailing list until the person says no, or 2 years without contact; applications from people not hired, 12 months after the decision. The reasons are *A reason to keep it*'s green pills (law, public task, agreed, fair interest); "fair interest" for applications not hired is this film's (assumptions.md). Owners decide each row (Ingrid Holm for HR, Mei Tanaka for the registrar's office); data governance (Yusuf Demir) keeps the schedule. Signed on Wednesday 14 October 2026, this film's date. - **"The law asks for them."** The film names no country's law, as the module decided. The 7 years is the university's own; it keeps pay records at least as long as laws that ask for seven years (for example Australia's *Fair Work Act 2009*, s 535, as *Hot, warm, cold* reads it), counted from each contract's end, so never shorter.
- **Three stages.** "Live" (in use: read and changed often), "archived" (kept cold: rarely read, kept cheaply, by fewer people) and "deleted": the three stages of a record in storage, as the author names them (12 November 2026; the film said "three lives" and "in use" before, and still glosses "live" as "in use"). "Kept for good" is not a fourth stage: it is the few records with no end date, which never reach "deleted". *Hot, warm, cold* defines cold as "rarely read, kept cheaply until the date it may be deleted"; its notes have each pay record go cold a year after it's paid, which the film follows ("her pay records from past years"). Who may read archived records is narrower by the film's choice, which privacy practice supports (fewer people, fewer risks).
- **Linh's contracts.** The example project's `hr_system/contracts.csv`: T-23014 (20 February to 23 June 2023), T-23187 (to 17 November 2023), T-24021 (to 21 June 2024), T-24166 (to 15 November 2024), T-25033 (to 27 June 2025), T-25172 (to 21 November 2025), T-26041 (to 27 June 2026), T-26188 (20 July to 20 November 2026). Seven years after each: 23 June 2030, 17 November 2030, 21 June 2031, 15 November 2031, 27 June 2032, 21 November 2032, 27 June 2033, 20 November 2033 (T-26188's, as *Forgetting a person* says). 20 November 2026 is a Friday; 14 October 2026 a Wednesday.
- **The hold.** The former tutor is invented, unnamed and not drawn; her claim is about hours in 2019 and isn't settled; the film doesn't say who is right. A hold (a legal hold) is records practice: when a claim, an audit or a case is pending, the records it needs are kept past their date whatever the schedule says, and only those; it names its records, its reason, who placed it and who lifts it, and once it's lifted, the schedule applies again. The GDPR keeps the same exception: erasure doesn't apply where processing is necessary "for the establishment, exercise or defence of legal claims" (Article 17(3)(e)).
- **"Keep personal data no longer than its reason needs."** GDPR Article 5(1)(e): "kept in a form which permits identification of data subjects for no longer than is necessary for the purposes for which the personal data are processed" ("storage limitation"), with longer storage allowed "solely for archiving purposes in the public interest, scientific or historical research purposes or statistical purposes", which is where the degrees kept for good sit. Other laws make the same rule, for example Australia's *Privacy Act 1988*, APP 11.2 (an entity that "no longer needs the information for any purpose" must "destroy the information or ... ensure that the information is de-identified", unless a law or a court requires it kept), and Canada's PIPEDA, its principle 5 ("Keep personal information only as long as it is needed to serve those purposes", in the Privacy Commissioner's words). So the film says "many privacy laws", never "most", as *A reason to keep it* set the module's wording on 5 November 2026; it names none.
- **Every copy.** Old table versions are kept 7 days before they're cleaned up, and logs and agent traces that may hold personal data 30 days (the module's `numbers.md`); how is *Deleted means deleted*'s, named in a line.
- **No release status** is shown or said (the author's rule of 5 November 2026); the film names no product.

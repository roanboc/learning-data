## The promise

Every line passes two tests: anyone who has ever wondered who changed something can follow it, and a security engineer finds nothing wrong in it. Locks are a promise; a record is the proof. In 1954 David Warren proposed a box that keeps the last hours of a cockpit, kept where a crash is least likely to reach, so no one has to guess what happened; a data platform needs the same: a record of who did what, kept before anything goes wrong, sent where none of the people it records can change it, and read to find out what happened, not to watch anyone. On top of it sit a few alarms that someone always answers, a set of steps for the night something is stolen, two emergency accounts for the day every lock jams, and a copy of the data nobody can delete. **If you can't see it, you can't say it's protected.**

## The story in one paragraph

Australia, 1953. David Warren, a chemist at the Aeronautical Research Laboratories who knows aircraft fuel tanks, is asked to help find out why two Comets, the world's first jet airliners, have crashed. In 1954 two more break up over the sea, and no one on board survives: no one can say what the crew saw or heard, the experts can only guess, and while they guess the planes fly again, full of passengers. In April 1954 Warren proposes a small box that records the cockpit's voices over and over, so the last hours are always kept, placed in the tail, where a crash is least likely to reach it. Pilots fear a spy in the cockpit and his boss tells him to get on with fuel tanks, but by 1958 his team's box keeps four hours of voices and instrument readings, and after a crash in Queensland in 1960 that no one could explain, Australia is the first country to require recorders on its airliners, from 1963: investigators can hear a lost plane's last minutes, find the cause and fix it on every other plane. At the university, Sam Okafor has put locks on the platform: on sign-ins, doors, power and changes. The security review asks him to show they held, with three questions about the last 90 days, and the answers are in four records that each keep their own time, one of them only a month. So every record is sent, as it's written, to one store the security team keeps apart from the platform, with a copy locked for a year. A few moments ring a bell at once: admin power switched on (Jun restores the learner table after a bad load, Sam approves, and the record keeps all of it), a risky sign-in, an emergency account used, a record switched off. One evening at 21:40, Jun's browser session is used from another country: an add-on had changed hands and copied his sign-ins. The on-call ends every session in twelve minutes, then reads in the record what the copy did: one download of the platform's code, which holds no secret and no learner's data, and nothing else; the platform has no door on the open internet. Nothing changed, so nothing is put back, and a review without blame closes the way in. Two emergency accounts wait in two safes for the day every lock jams, tested every 90 days. Every night a copy of production's data goes to a place apart, locked for 30 days, and once a month the team restores one to check it. Three months on, Sam answers the review in an afternoon. Like Warren's box, the record doesn't stop a crash; it means no one has to guess what happened.

## The reference, and practice

The module's reference holds (its proposal, *The reference*): DAMA-DMBOK's data security area for what is protected and why, and zero trust (NIST SP 800-207) for how; NIST's Cybersecurity Framework 2.0 places the films on one map, and this film is its last three functions, Detect, Respond and Recover. **Where the film differs, on purpose.** The standards speak of logs, events, SIEMs and playbooks; the film says "a record", "a bell" and "the steps", and shows only the few alerts that matter. It doesn't teach query languages, detection rules or forensics: it shows where each answer lives, why one store out of reach is needed, and the order of the steps. *Proof it held* reads who read personal data from the same records; this film reads sign-ins, power and changes.

**Practice.** What practitioners recognise: a review that asks for 90 days of sign-ins when the directory keeps 30; records that live on the same platform, under the same admins, as what they record; logging switched off during a "quick fix" and never back on; so many alerts that nobody reads them; a stolen session that walks past a passkey, because the passkey was used before the theft; a first hour lost reading logs while the intruder is still in; break-glass accounts nobody has tried in years, or whose keys are in the same drawer as the admin's laptop; backups that sit beside the data, where the same admin, or the same attacker, can delete them; and restores never tested until the day they're needed. And what works: records sent as they're written to a place apart, kept long enough; a handful of alerts, each with a person who answers it; end it first, then read; review the causes, never the person; two emergency accounts tried every 90 days; copies locked for a set time, restored every month.

**The author's practice** (his brief for *Power you switch on*, 3 November 2026): each data specialist's second, personal admin account for delicate work, "raised for a task with a reason, approved, logged, then off". The film shows what "logged" buys: an alert each time, and a record of what was done.

- **Warren and the Comets.** IEEE Spectrum (Allison Marsh, 18 June 2021, from Warren and Ken Fraser's own account): "Given Warren's expertise in fuel tank design, the ARL assigned him in 1953 to a panel investigating two recent crashes of the de Havilland Comet"; the ARL, the Aeronautical Research Laboratories, was "a part of Australia's Department of Defence". "The planes fly again" between them: the Comets were grounded after Elba and flew again before Naples, which failed the same way (metal fatigue, found later in a water tank); the film doesn't name the cause, since the point is that no one could say.
- **The note and the box.** "In April 1954, David Warren submitted a four-page note, 'Mechanical Engineering Technical Memorandum 142: A Device for Assisting Investigation into Aircraft Accidents.'" "He proposed an instrument that would record cockpit conversation on a closed circle of wire" and "suggested it be placed in the part of the plane least likely to receive extensive damage, namely the tail." The film's "over and over" is the closed circle of wire, and the 1958 unit's loop: "The recording continued in a loop, erasing all information as it rerecorded." "Completed in 1958, the ARL Flight Memory Unit ... could store up to 4 hours of voice as well as various instrument readings": "by 1958 his team's box keeps four hours of voices and the instruments' readings".
- **The doubts.** His boss told him to "get on with blowing up fuel tanks"; the Federation of Air Pilots called it "a spy flying alongside—no plane would take off in Australia with Big Brother listening" (IEEE). The voice says it in short, "Pilots fear a spy in the cockpit, and his boss tells him to get on with fuel tanks"; the two cards on screen quote their own words.
- **1960 and 1963.** Trans Australia Airlines Flight 538, 10 June 1960, a Fokker F27 near Mackay, Queensland: "All 29 people on board were killed"; "The inquiry did not determine a particular cause"; the inquiry recommended flight data recorders (Wikipedia, read 5 November 2026). "Australia became the first country to mandate both data and voice recordings on all aircraft, in January 1963. A judge ordered the move after a fatal crash in Queensland in 1960" (IEEE); Wikipedia says first for voice recorders. "From 1963, Australia is the first country to require recorders on its airliners" holds on both. "No one can say why" is the inquiry's finding.
- **What the box gave.** Its purpose is its note's title, assisting the investigation of accidents; "investigators can hear its last minutes, find the cause, and fix it on every other plane" is what flight recorders do. The film doesn't say how recordings are protected by law (not checked on the day).
- **Four records, four memories.** Databricks: `system.access.audit`, free retention "365 days" (*System tables*), with `source_ip_address`, `user_identity`, `action_name` and `identity_metadata` (`run_by`, `run_as`). - **A copy nobody can delete.** *Walls, doors and keys*' copies, exactly (`numbers.md`).

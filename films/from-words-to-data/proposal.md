## The brief

Four videos, from the author (28 September 2026): how we understand the world through language and define it, how people have organised concepts through history, and why that matters to a business even without technology; how it links to systems, and why conceptual and logical models matter; modelling for transactional, analytical and hybrid systems; and how semantic layers, ontologies, industry standards and AI can keep it all right, end to end.

## Analysis

**What works**

- **The order is right: meaning, then systems, then shapes, then upkeep.** Most material on modelling starts with notation or a tool. Starting with language explains *why* models exist, and it's the part that never goes out of date.
- **It continues what *A Sharper Sketch* started.** That film's lesson was "the difference isn't in the data, it's in the meaning". This series explains where meaning comes from, and how to keep it.
- **Opening with a film that has no technology is the strongest and rarest idea.** It shows the business that the model is theirs, not IT's. Leaders who would never watch a film about databases can watch this one.
- **The ending is timely.** AI agents answer only as well as the meaning they can read, so semantic layers and ontologies are now a business topic, not just an architecture one.

**What changed from the brief, and why**

- **One thing to follow: the credential.** Four films on four themes risk becoming four lectures (PLAYBOOK §1). The series follows one idea that is centuries old and still true.
- **Seven films instead of four.** At the site's pace, about 125 to 130 words a minute, a 6-minute film is around 800 words and carries six to eight ideas. Transactional, analytical and hybrid each need their own side-by-side comparison; so do semantic layers and standards, and AI keeping them current.
- **Every film starts in the past.** The history is spread across the series, one moment per film, instead of crowding the opening film. Each moment is still true today.
- **No repeats of *A Sharper Sketch*.** It already teaches grain, the three levels, reference models and versioning; this series uses them.
- **What the brief didn't name, added:** identity (what makes one thing one thing, across systems), the same word meaning different things in different places, who owns each definition, and how history is kept.
- **Two common confusions, cleared up:** bronze, silver and gold describe how refined data is, not what shape it has; a glossary, an ontology and a semantic layer are different things that stack, not rivals.
- **Fast-moving parts last.** Hybrid databases, semantic layer formats and AI tools change names and maturity every few months. Keep product names to labels and the rigour sheet, script those films last, and check them on the day (PLAYBOOK §3).
- **AI without hype.** Show what it does well (noticing drift, drafting changes, answering from governed definitions) and where it fails (confident answers when meaning is missing). The agent recommends; people approve.

## The spine: how do you prove what you know?

People have always needed to show what they know to someone who wasn't there to see them learn it. For centuries, the answer has barely changed: someone trusted makes a claim about someone, in a way that others can check. Only the materials changed: a wax seal, a folded diploma, a transcript, a digital badge, a digitally signed credential.

The words changed too, and their history is part of the story:

| Word | Where it comes from |
|---|---|
| credential | Latin *credere*, to believe or trust. Ambassadors still present their credentials: letters asking the host to trust the bearer. |
| credit | The same root. A credit point is, literally, a unit of trust. |
| diploma | Greek for a paper folded in two. |
| degree, graduate | Latin *gradus*, a step. |
| course, curriculum | Latin *currere*, to run. A curriculum was a racecourse. |
| enrol | Old French, to write a name on a roll. The first enrolments were records. |
| student | Latin *studere*, to be eager, to apply oneself. |
| microcredential | The newest form: a small, checkable claim that can stack towards a bigger one. |

It still continues *A Sharper Sketch*, whose last scene asks how to count short courses and microcredentials while the stamp flickers "sketch v3?". In this series, v3 adds the credential.

**The question that runs through the series:** "How many credentials did we award this year?"

| Film | What happens to the credential |
|---|---|
| What's in a word | Four offices give four answers. They agree, on paper, what a credential is. |
| Older than the systems | Its idea turns out to be centuries old. It meets five systems, each with its own model of it. |
| Built to write, built to read | Awards are issued one by one on graduation day, and read ten years at a time by planners. |
| Many ways to read | Credentials from three platforms are integrated, presented and served as one learner record. |
| Both at once | An employer checks a credential live; a learner's wallet shows, now, how close they are to a graduate certificate. |

| Keeping it true | The government adds a reporting rule, and a credential issued in error is revoked. AI spots both and drafts the change; people approve sketch v3. |

The idea travels beyond universities: professional licences, safety certificates, driving licences, vendor certifications. Any business that says what someone can do, or checks it, has this model.

## The films

### What's in a word

**Logline.** In graduation week, the university asks how many credentials it awarded this year, and gets four answers. None is wrong. To see why, the film goes back before writing, and before words: to how minds sort the world into kinds, and how words came to point at them.

| Chapter | What happens | What it teaches |
|---|---|---|
| Four answers | 7,420 degrees and diplomas. 10,600 with microcredentials. 14,650 with badges. 26,900 with every certificate of completion. | The numbers aren't wrong. The word is shared; the ideas behind it aren't. |
| Kinds without words | Pigeons learn to pick out photos that have people in them. Honeybees learn "same" and "different". No words at all. | Concepts come before words. Sorting the world into kinds is older than language. |
| Calls that point | Vervet monkeys give one alarm call for a leopard, another for an eagle, another for a snake. Each sends the group a different way: up a tree, into a bush, up on two legs to look down. Dolphins, elephants and marmosets use calls that work like names. | A signal can stand for a kind of thing, or for one individual: the first categories, and the first identifiers. |
| What words add | A baby points before it speaks, to share attention on a thing. Human words combine without limit, and reach what isn't here: yesterday, a promise, what someone knows. Speech leaves no fossils, so nobody knows exactly when it began. | Pointing is the root of reference. Words can describe what can't be seen, like what a person knows: the need behind every credential. |
| One idea, many forms | In the brain, a single cell can respond to photos of one person, a drawing of them and their written name. A word points to an idea, and the idea to things. | Word, idea and thing are different, and the idea sits in the middle: the triangle every data model inherits. |
| Which part do you mean? | Someone points at a running rabbit and says "gavagai". The rabbit? Its ears? That moment of running? Children guess "the whole thing" first. | Every word has a grain. A model has to say what one of anything is. |
| Fuzzy edges | A robin is a "better" bird than a penguin. People agree on typical cases, and argue at the edges. A degree is surely a credential. A workshop badge? | Categories have typical members and fuzzy edges. A business needs its definitions written down exactly where the edges are. |
| Words drift | "Nice" once meant foolish. "Credential" comes from trust; "enrol" meant writing a name on a roll. | Meaning evolves, and a word's history often explains its edges. |
| Agreed on paper | Back at the university, with no system in sight. A *credential* is a trusted, checkable statement that someone has shown what they know or can do. *Award*, *microcredential* and *badge* are kinds of credential; a certificate of attendance isn't one. Each word gets an owner. Aristotle's recipe helps: name the kind, then what sets it apart. The sketch gains a box in pencil: v3, draft. | A conceptual model is a business's shared language, written down. It needs no technology. |
| Pull back | About five thousand years ago, people began pressing marks into clay, and words could outlast the person who said them. That's where the next film begins. | From minds, to marks, to systems. |

**Why a business needs this without technology.** Every contract opens with its definitions. Professional bodies decide who may call themselves an engineer or an accountant. Mergers stall when two companies each have "a customer" and mean different things. Funding, fees and rankings depend on who counts. Shared words cost little; disagreement is expensive, and it's paid for in meetings, rework and wrong decisions.

**Pictures.** Three alarm calls as three coloured arcs, each to its own predator's silhouette. A pointing hand. One cell lighting up for a photo, a drawing and a name, forming the triangle. A rabbit with three possible outlines: whole, ears, a moment of running. A cloud of birds with the robin at the centre and the penguin at the edge. The word "credential" changing through its history. All drawn in code, in the series' style, as original art.

**Labs.** *Name the kind* (sort pictures and see where people disagree: at the edges). *Gavagai* (choose what one of something is, and watch the count change). *Write the definition* (the kind, then what sets it apart). *Count the credentials* (pick a definition and watch the number move).

### Older than the systems

**Logline.** The definition is agreed. The idea behind it is centuries old, and has outlived every material it was written on. Now it meets five systems, each with its own idea of a credential.

**Why conceptual first.** It's the only model the business can read, the only one that survives a change of system, and the one every other level is checked against.

| Chapter | What happens | What it teaches |
|---|---|---|
| Same idea, new materials | A guild's masterpiece. China's imperial examinations, for thirteen centuries. A licence to teach under a wax seal. A folded diploma, a transcript, a digital badge, a digitally signed credential. | The material changed every few centuries. The idea didn't. |
| The model underneath | An issuer makes a claim about a holder, backed by evidence, on a date. A verifier checks it. Some expire; some are revoked. Today's standard for digital credentials uses almost the same words. | A conceptual model can outlast every system built on it. |
| Every system has a model inside | The student system holds awards; the learning platform, completions; the careers office, badges; a digital wallet, the signed copies. The new short-course platform, bought off the shelf, holds microcredentials, and calls learners "customers". | Buying a system means adopting its model, whether you look at it or not. If you don't model your business, your vendors will. |
| Where meanings meet | Five systems connected in pairs need up to ten translations. In 1999, a spacecraft is lost because one team's software works in pound-force seconds and another's in newton-seconds. | Meaning breaks at interfaces. Translate each system once, to a shared model, instead of pair by pair. |
| One person, many records | One learner has four IDs. Their credentials come from three systems, their name from one, their email from another. | Identity across systems: master data, a system of record for each fact, and code sets (reference data). |
| Precise, but not yet technical | The logical model: what identifies a credential, its attributes and allowed values (level, volume of learning), how many of one relate to another (a microcredential can count towards several awards), and the rules. Still no technology. | The logical model is the yardstick every system is held to: to choose a package (fit and gap), to map its fields, to migrate. |
| Who owns what | The registrar owns *award*; the short-course team, *microcredential*; the architect, the logical model; each team, its tables. A change travels down; news of a change travels up. | Ownership and stewardship. The two halves of a change from *Silent change*, now for the model itself. |
| Pull back | The platform's "customer" maps to *learner*, and its "certificate" to *microcredential*. Nothing in the platform changed; the meaning is joined. | Next: one logical model, many physical shapes. |

**Labs.** *Same idea, new materials* (match old and new credentials to issuer, holder, claim, evidence and verifier). *Who is the source?* (give each fact its system of record). *Fit and gap* (hold a vendor's model up to ours: adopt, extend, map).

### Built to write, built to read

**Logline.** The same award, stored twice: once when it's issued, once for the planners who read ten years at a time. Merchants in Venice did the same five hundred years ago.

| Chapter | What happens | What it teaches |
|---|---|---|
| Journal and ledger | Pacioli, 1494: each transaction goes into the journal as it happens, in time order, then into the ledger, grouped by account, where it's read and balanced. Five centuries later, the university has the same two jobs: issue thousands of awards on graduation day, each complete and right, and read ten years of them for planning. | One set of facts, two shapes: one for writing, one for reading. The balance is the first data quality check. |
| Built to write | Each fact in one place: the learner's name once, not on every award. Keys, rules, and an award that's issued completely or not at all. | Normalisation, keys and constraints, transactions. |
| What goes wrong without it | The name is stored three times and corrected in two. A certificate prints the old one. | Update anomalies: why "each fact once" matters for writing. |
| Another way to write | A digital credential saved as one signed document, with its evidence inside. | Documents: keep together what's written, and signed, together. Easy to write and check one; harder to query across many. |
| Built to read | Declare the grain first (one row per credential awarded), then facts in the middle and dimensions around them: learner, credential type, faculty, date. | The star: grain, facts and dimensions. |
| Keeping history for reading | A learner changed faculty mid-year; an award was revoked and reissued. Keep both, each with its dates. | Slowly changing dimensions and status history, for reading. |
| Side by side | The same question on both shapes: many joins and a history puzzle, against two joins. Then a name correction: easy in one, awkward in the other. | Each shape is fast at its own job. |
| Pull back | One sketch, two shapes. And a common confusion: bronze, silver and gold say how refined data is, not what shape it has. | Medallion layers are not a modelling technique. Next: more shapes for reading. |

**Labs.** *Journal to ledger* (post the same entries into both shapes). *Break the update.* *Declare the grain.* *Which shape answers this faster?*

### Many ways to read

**Logline.** Kimball, Inmon, Data Vault, one big table: the arguments are loud. In a lakehouse, each shape has a job and a place. This is the engineers' film *A Sharper Sketch* parked.

| Chapter | What happens | What it teaches |
|---|---|---|
| One book, three cards | A library's card catalogue files the same book under its author, its title and its subject. | The same thing, reached many ways: copies arranged for reading, kept in step. |
| The argument | Four engineers, four favourite shapes, and credentials now arriving from three platforms. | There's no best shape, only a best shape for a job. |
| Integrate first | Many sources, one meaning. A normalised core, or a Data Vault: hubs for business keys, links for relationships, satellites for the history of descriptions, each with its source and load time. | Built for new sources, change and audit; not for people to query. |
| Present for people | Stars for awards, enrolments and fees share the same Learner and Time dimensions. | Conformed dimensions: stars that agree with each other. |
| Serve an entity | One wide row per learner: every credential, the credit it gives towards each award, and what's next. | Entity-centric tables: easy for people, machine learning and AI. The cost: many columns to maintain, and measures defined twice unless you're careful. |
| Where each lives | Silver integrates; gold presents and serves; a semantic layer sits on top. | Each shape placed on the platform from *The Inner Life of Data*. |
| Choosing | Many changing sources: a vault. Many processes sharing context: conformed stars. One entity, many questions, AI: wide tables. | Choose per question, not per fashion. |

### Both at once

**Logline.** New databases promise to write and read in one place. They remove a copy. Do they remove the need to model?

| Chapter | What happens | What it teaches |
|---|---|---|
| The till that kept the total | In 1879, a saloon keeper patents a cash register: one machine records each sale and keeps the day's total. | Recording and counting in one place is an old wish. |
| The distance | Today: the app's database, a copy, a nightly build, an answer tomorrow. But an employer wants to check a credential now, and a learner's wallet wants to show, now, how close they are to a graduate certificate. | Why hybrid is appealing: the distance between writing and reading. |
| Two engines, one place | A row store for writes and a column store for reads, kept in step by the database. Or an operational database that lives inside the lakehouse, with tables synced both ways. | What hybrid (HTAP) databases do. Products named in labels and the rigour sheet only. |
| What it removes | Copies, pipelines, delay, a second set of permissions. | The real gains. |
| What it doesn't | Count awards straight from the app's tables, and revoked ones slip in: 131 against 118 again. History, conformed dimensions and definitions still need modelling. | Hybrid removes the copy, not the model. |
| Data flowing back | Gold data served back to the app: the next microcredential to suggest. An AI agent that reads what's known and writes what it did. | Data flowing back to apps, and agents that need both shapes. |
| New questions for the modeller | Which shape is the source of truth for each idea? Which way does each table sync? How fresh must each answer be? | Contracts in both directions, and freshness per use. |

**Labs.** *Where does it live?* (choose the source of truth and the direction of sync for each table).

### Meaning machines can read

**Logline.** The definitions from *What's in a word* were written for people. Now Genie gets the questions. What does an AI agent need to read, and how do semantic layers, ontologies and industry standards differ?

| Chapter | What happens | What it teaches |
|---|---|---|
| They tried before | Linnaeus gives each species one name, in a hierarchy. Wilkins, in 1668, designs a language to classify everything; it never takes hold. Nightingale, in 1860, asks every hospital to record the same things the same way; an international list of causes of death follows in 1893, today's ICD. | Shared definitions let strangers compare. Keep them bounded, and made for a purpose. |
| Genie guesses | "Which learners are one microcredential away from a graduate certificate?" Genie finds the tables, but the stacking rules are in a policy document no tool reads. It's wrong. | An agent answers from what it can read. Meaning kept in documents is invisible to it. |
| Four ways to write meaning down | A glossary (words, for people). A taxonomy (kinds, in a hierarchy, like Linnaeus's). An ontology (concepts, relationships and rules a machine can reason with). A semantic layer (how each number is calculated, once). | A stack, not rivals. Each answers a different question. |
| The ontology | "A microcredential is a credential. A graduate certificate accepts up to four approved microcredentials towards its credit." Data organised by these rules becomes a knowledge graph. | Rules a machine can check: Aristotle's definitions, made formal. |
| The semantic layer | "Credentials awarded this year" defined once: the measure, the filters, the time and the grain. Dashboards, spreadsheets and Genie all ask it, instead of each writing its own. | One definition, every tool. Open formats for sharing these definitions between tools are emerging. |
| Standards | Digital credentials have published standards, and so do finance, health, insurance and retail. Even *microcredential* has three official definitions, from Australia, the European Union and UNESCO, and they don't quite match. | Don't start from blank, and choose deliberately: check, adopt, extend and record, from *A Sharper Sketch*, at every layer. |
| Genie, again | Genie asks back, "approved for stacking, or all microcredentials?", then answers with its definition. | Grounding an agent in explicit meaning makes its answers measurably more accurate. |
| Pull back | The triangle from *What's in a word* returns: the word, with labels in English and Spanish; the idea; the data. | Next: keeping it true while everything changes. |

**Labs.** *Glossary, taxonomy, ontology or semantic layer?* (sort the statements). *Map to the standard.*

### Keeping it true

**Logline.** The meaning is written down end to end, from a word to a number. Then the world changes again. AI can watch, draft and check. People decide.

| Chapter | What happens | What it teaches |
|---|---|---|
| A dictionary is never finished | Johnson, in 1755, hoped to fix English, and wrote that no dictionary can embalm a language. The Oxford English Dictionary is still being revised. In 2006, "planet" got a definition, and the count went from nine to eight; in 2019, the kilogram got a new one. | Meaning moves. Definitions need owners, dates and versions. |
| Change arrives | The government adds a reporting element for microcredentials. A credential issued in error must be revoked. A new metric appears in three dashboards, with three definitions. | Models go stale through drift: the gap between what's written and what's used. |
| AI as a watcher | An agent reads the catalog, the lineage, the queries and the new data. It flags each drift, with evidence. | Where AI helps first: noticing. |
| AI as a drafter | It drafts the change: the ontology, the logical model, the mapping to the standard, the semantic layer, the contract, the tests and the change note. | Drafting is cheap now. Judging the draft is still the job. |
| People decide | The registrar approves the meaning, the architect the model, the teams the build. The draft goes through the same review as anyone's. | The agent recommends; people approve. Everything is versioned. |
| What can go wrong | A confident draft that invents a definition. A change nobody reviewed. | Tests and contracts check the agent's work too. |
| End to end | One change, followed through: glossary, ontology, logical model, the shapes for writing and reading, semantic layer, contract, Genie's answer. All in step. The stamp reads sketch v3. | Upkeep, end to end, and why each layer matters. |
| Pull back | Back to the start: someone trusted makes a claim, in a way others can check. A number in a report is a claim too. | The tools changed; the job didn't. |

**Labs.** *Approve or reject* (the agent's proposals, some plausible and wrong). *Follow one change end to end.*

## Rigour to check when scripting

To confirm against primary sources, and record in each film's rigour sheet with the date checked. *What's in a word* makes claims about animals, brains and language, so it needs the most care: say "nobody knows" where nobody does, and ask a linguist or cognitive scientist to read the script.

- ***What's in a word*:** pigeons and pictures of people (Herrnstein and Loveland, 1964). Honeybees and "same" and "different" (Giurfa and others, 2001). Vervet alarm calls and the responses to each (Seyfarth, Cheney and Marler, 1980). Name-like calls in dolphins (King and Janik, 2013), elephants (Pardo and others, 2024) and marmosets (Oren and others, 2024). Infant pointing (Tomasello, Carpenter and Liszkowski, 2007). Hockett's design features of language, including displacement (1960). What is and isn't known about when speech began.

## The promise

Every line passes two tests: anyone who has found a table on their platform owned by whoever happened to build it can follow it, and an experienced data or enterprise architect finds nothing wrong in it. Who owns a table is a decision, and the default, whoever built it, gives it to the one team that can't know what its numbers mean. The rule the owners agree: every table belongs to a data product, and each product to whoever owns the capability that creates its data; a measure that crosses domains belongs to the enterprise domain. The data team builds and runs every product; the centre keeps only what all domains share: the platform, the catalogue, a few rules. A data product is data made for others to use, with someone who answers for it, and it's published only with five things: an owner, documentation, lineage, a contract and an approved way in. Warehouse, lakehouse, mesh and fabric aren't rivals: they answer different questions, where the data is kept, who owns it and how it's found, so they mix. The gaps between today and the target go into the roadmap, each with an owner and a quarter, starting where it hurts; what nobody claims in time is archived, its readers told first. **Let the business that knows its data own it, and answer for it.**

## The story in one paragraph

In 1972, Cybersyn's engineers went from factory to factory across Chile, and in each, with its workers and managers, they chose about five numbers to watch. If one went out of its range, the software would send a warning, and Stafford Beer and the team had to decide who that warning would go to. If every warning went to Santiago, a few people there would be buried in small problems, and factories would wait for orders; and President Allende had insisted the system be decentralising, with workers taking part, and not bureaucratic. Factories needed room to solve their own problems, and the centre needed to hear only of the ones they couldn't. So, in Beer's design, a warning would go first to the factory's own manager, and nobody else, with a set time to deal with it in their own way; only if it wasn't solved in time would the team tell the next level, that industry's committee at CORFO. The engineers chose each factory's time, and Beer called it designing freedom: each factory, he argued, would keep almost complete control of its work, and the centre would step in only on serious problems. At the utility, Inés opens the catalogue: of the platform's 412 tables, 300 name the data team as their owner, because it built them. One is hosting capacity, how much solar each street can take. On the map it's Grace's: her engineers defined it, and how to work it out; the data team built the table from their method, and households check it on the website. In May, the engineers found older cables carry less and changed their method, but the table kept the old one: the engineers didn't see the table as theirs to tell, and the data team had no way to know. For four months the website told households their street could take more solar than it could, and ninety families bought panels, then found they could send back only part of the power they made. Nobody was careless: the table's owner was the one team that couldn't know what its numbers meant. Who owns a table is for the owners to decide, so Inés brings them one rule, to make the platform match the map: every table belongs to a data product, and each product to whoever owns the capability that creates its data. Planning the network creates hosting capacity, so its product is Grace's, method and all; serving customers creates accounts, so theirs is the head of retail's; a measure that crosses domains belongs to the enterprise domain, and for the regulator's measures that's Ama. The data team builds and runs every product, and the centre keeps only what all domains share. The owners agree, and the rule goes on the map with its date. A product is published only when it has five things, an owner, documentation, lineage, a contract and an approved way in, and the one catalogue lists them all. Warehouse, lakehouse, mesh and fabric answer different questions, so they mix: the utility's target is a mesh of owners, on its lakehouse, with one catalogue. Inés draws the baseline and the target; each difference is a gap, and goes into the roadmap with an owner and a quarter, hosting capacity first. Each domain has three months to claim its tables; what nobody claims is archived, its readers told first, and retired after that. On the data practice on the map, each data product goes in its domain, and every table in the catalogue points to its product. In three months, the domains claimed 246 of the 300 tables, in 31 data products; nobody claimed the other 54, and nobody has asked for one back. When Grace's engineers next changed their method, hosting capacity changed with it, as a new version; its readers knew a week ahead, and no family bought panels its street couldn't take. But with so many owners, who keeps them all speaking one language?

Someone who has never heard of Cybersyn can answer, from the narration alone, why it was a problem (every warning sent to Santiago would bury a few people there in small problems while the factories waited for orders, and the president had insisted the system not be bureaucratic) and what the fix gave people (each factory would keep almost complete control of its work, solving its own problems first, and the centre would step in only on serious problems). The series follows Cybersyn in all six films (the author, 11 November 2026): this film tells Beer's design for where a warning would go, said as it was designed ("would"), since Medina (2006, p. 592) records that in practice the results went to CORFO's telex room without the warning step; how little of the design was built, and its end, come in the last film.

## The reference, and practice

The series follows DAMA-DMBOK2's Data Architecture, and this film takes its words from three of its neighbours: Data Governance, whose data owner is a business role with authority over decisions about the data in its domain; Metadata Management, whose catalogue says what exists and who answers for it; and Data Warehousing and Business Intelligence, for the warehouse. Domain ownership and data as a product come from data mesh (Z. Dehghani, "How to Move Beyond a Monolithic Data Lake to a Distributed Data Mesh", 2019, and *Data Mesh*, O'Reilly, 2022): its four principles, domain ownership, data as a product, a self-serve data platform and federated governance, are the mesh's line, and its product's qualities (discoverable, addressable, understandable, trustworthy, natively accessible, interoperable, valuable on its own, secure) are what the utility's five things ask for, said as practitioners say them: an owner, documentation, lineage, a contract and an approved way in. The lakehouse is the CIDR 2021 paper's (M. Armbrust, A. Ghodsi, R. Xin and M. Zaharia): open file formats, with a warehouse's tables and controls on top, for reports and machine learning alike. The warehouse is Inmon's and Kimball's (W. H. Inmon, *Building the Data Warehouse*, 1992; R. Kimball, *The Data Warehouse Toolkit*, 1996), usually built and run by one central team; the data fabric is the analysts' word (Forrester, from 2016; Gartner, from 2019) for a design that uses metadata to find, connect and govern data across many stores, automating what it can. The baseline, the target and the gaps between them, each into the roadmap, are TOGAF's (Phase C, Data Architecture, and Phase E, Opportunities and Solutions), and the capability that creates the data is *Business architecture for data*'s. On the data practice on the map, a data product is drawn as ArchiMate draws a product: something the business offers as a whole, which brings together the data it offers and its contract. Under the site's rules (the author, 10 and 12 November 2026), owners decide what their data means, the data team builds, runs and reviews the code, and a product's contract with its readers is agreed team to team; only a canonical table has an enterprise contract, and this film needs none.

What practitioners would tell a newcomer, and the film shows: before anything else, look at who the catalogue says owns each table, because an owner by default, whoever built it, is the most common gap and the least seen; give each table to the team whose work creates its data, not the team that reads it most or built it, and write that rule down, with its date, where everyone can find it; don't start with all of them: start where it hurts, with the products whose wrong numbers reach customers, then those with the most readers; give the domains a fixed time to claim their tables, and archive what nobody claims, telling its readers first, rather than keeping everything for ever; publish a product only when it has an owner, documentation, lineage, a contract and a way to ask for access, since a table without them is still nobody's in practice; and don't choose between warehouse, lakehouse, mesh and fabric as if they were rivals: decide where the data is kept, who owns it and how it's found, and the words follow.

## Sound

The film's palette (12 November 2026, its row in the four new architecture series' palettes register) pairs two instruments the site has, which no film pairs: **rototoms** and **timpani**. The register allows a pairing where nothing new fits, and this one is an assumption in the film's assumptions file, with the question on the author's checklist. The rototoms are small tuned drums with no shell, struck softly with felt mallets, dry and warm, never rolled and never a run: each drum is tuned to its note by turning it on its own stand, so whoever plays it sets its note, as each factory in Beer's design solved its own problems first and each domain answers for its own data. They play middle notes (F 3 to D 5), one drum at a time, in short phrases with room between them, never a steady beat. Their partner is a pair of timpani, tuned to B flat 2 and F 2, struck softly with felt sticks and left to ring, never rolled: the orchestra's centre, played only where something isn't solved in time and goes up (the warning that reaches that industry's committee in the opening, and again when the film comes back to Chile; the tables nobody claimed in time at the utility, archived), and under the series' mark. Kept apart from the films that have them: the drums sit lower and warmer than *Five stages, not a switch*'s, high and dry, and are never turned up a step; the timpani sound only where something goes up, never as a wall's weight or under a held organ. Nothing Chilean or Andean stands for Cybersyn (no bombo, no kultrun, no rhythm of a dance): tuned orchestral drums, soft, never a rhythm. Both play dry, with no room round them. Everything is made in code, in [`source/tools/score.py`](source/tools/score.py): nothing is sampled.

## Pause and think

The film stops at the end of three chapters, with one question each ([`site/assets/who-owns-which-data/think.en.js`](../../../site/assets/who-owns-which-data/think.en.js), Spanish in `think.es.js`):

Near miss: the engineers forgot to tell the data team (they didn't tell, but they didn't see the table as theirs to tell: nobody was careless, and the owner was the one team that couldn't know).
- **After *The business that knows*:** who owns the regulator's minutes without power, made from the network's outages and retail's customers. Right: the enterprise domain, Ama, since the measure crosses domains. Near miss: Grace, since the outages happen on her network (it crosses domains, so it's the enterprise domain's).
- **After *Four words*:** why the utility can be a mesh, on a lakehouse, with one catalogue. Right: they answer different questions: who owns the data, where it's kept, and how it's found. Near miss: a mesh is a newer technology, built on top of a lakehouse (a mesh isn't a technology: it's who owns the data, as products).

## Labs

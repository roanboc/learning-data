## The promise

Every line passes two tests: someone new to data work can follow it, and a data manager, a data governance lead or an analytics engineer finds nothing wrong in it. The film's idea is decentralisation and emergence: a leader who makes every decision becomes the place where every decision waits. Give people a few clear rules, each with its reason, good tools and trust, and let decisions be made where the knowledge is; then watch what emerges, learn from it, and add a rule only when a real problem shows. The team stops making room reports by hand, and the people who know the courses find what the centre never looked for.

**Takeaway:** "A few clear rules, good tools, and trust."

## The story in one paragraph

Bali, in the 1970s. For over a thousand years, rice farmers have shared the water that runs down from the mountains: the farmers of one canal form a subak, and the subaks along a river meet at a water temple and agree a calendar of who plants first and who follows. Planting in turns shares the water; fields harvested together lie empty together, so the pests go hungry. Then Indonesia set out to grow more rice, fast: farmers were told to plant new, quick-growing rice as often as they could, whatever the temples' calendar said. The planners wanted the country fed, and the new rice gave more grain; but with every field on its own clock, the pests always found rice next door. In 1977 the brown planthopper destroyed about two million tons of rice across Indonesia, and in the dry season farms downstream waited for water that didn't come. In the 1980s the anthropologist Stephen Lansing and the ecologist James Kremer built a computer model of two rivers and their 172 subaks, which showed that the temples' calendar gave the biggest harvests; in 1988 they took it to the bank that had paid for new irrigation works, and the bank agreed. The temples set the planting again, the pests came under control, farmers sprayed less, and the bank's final report found no project it had reviewed that ran itself as well. At the university, a year from now, every room question comes to the data team: from July to September 2027 the four faculties ask for 27 room reports, each a day and a half to make and two weeks in the list, and Helena Novak, the data manager, decides whose rooms come first. So she decides to stop deciding. Yusuf Demir's first list of eleven rules comes down to the three needed now, agreed with the faculties' four timetable officers; Ivy and Jun make a template that makes the right way the easy way, with one box that says who decides what; and from Monday 4 October the officers plan their own rooms. That Monday, Lucy Tran, Engineering's officer, puts her faculty's lab bookings beside the sensors' counts and finds nine of its twelve teaching labs booked and empty every Monday morning, which no report had shown; within three weeks Health and Arts have copied her page, and nobody sent an instruction. By Friday 26 November all four faculties have sent their plans, moving one peak class in six; one real problem, an old copy, brings a fourth rule out of the drawer.

## The reference, and practice

The module's reference for this film is decentralisation and emergence (the briefs, film 10): Elinor Ostrom's commons, Lansing's water temples, data mesh and *Team Topologies*. The film names each once, and teaches what they share: decisions made where the knowledge is, within a few shared rules, on tools that make the right way easy, and a leader who watches what emerges.

| Body of knowledge | What it adds | Where the film uses it |
|---|---|---|
| Elinor Ostrom, *Governing the Commons: The Evolution of Institutions for Collective Action* (1990) | Fisheries, forests and irrigation systems that people shared for centuries without a central owner; the ones that lasted share design principles: clear boundaries, rules fitted to local conditions, rules made and changed by the people who use them, monitoring, graduated sanctions, ways to resolve conflict | *Three rules, each with its reason*: "The political economist Elinor Ostrom studied fishing grounds, forests and irrigation that people had shared for centuries." | Data mesh: Zhamak Dehghani, *Data Mesh* (2022), and "Data Mesh Principles and Logical Architecture" (martinfowler.com, 2020) | Data owned by the domains that know it, served as products, on a self-serve platform, with governance shared across domains (its four principles) | *The right way, the easy way*: "Data mesh and the book Team Topologies agree: decisions with the people who know the work, on a platform that makes the hard parts easy." |

**Where the film adopts, extends or differs.** It adopts Ostrom's finding that lasting rules are few, fitted to the place, and made and changed by the people who live by them: the three rules are agreed with the four officers, not for them, and any of them can ask to change one. It adopts data mesh's ownership and *Team Topologies*' platform at the level a newcomer needs: the faculties decide their classes, Ivy, the product's owner, decides what the numbers mean, the team keeps the platform. It differs from a common reading of data mesh, that every domain builds its own data products: here the faculties read one governed product and add a measure only through its owner and the glossary (rule three), so a new number means one thing before anyone publishes it, as the site's governance films ask. It extends the commons' rules with the practice the briefs ask for: each rule carries its reason on its face, the rest wait in a drawer, and one comes out only when a real problem shows.

**Practice, from the module's briefs.** A practitioner will recognise the central team that becomes the queue for every question, the manager who ends up deciding whose request comes first, the long list of rules written against problems from somewhere else, the template that does more for consistency than the rules do, the faculty that knows something the centre never asked, and the old copy of a report that someone plans from. The advice is the briefs': write few rules, each with its reason; make the right way the easy way; say who decides what; watch what emerges, and learn from it; and add a rule only when a real problem shows.

**Who decides, as the site's films say it.** The definition starts in the glossary, and the code is built from it: Lucy asks Ivy, the product's owner, Ivy writes lab use in the glossary the same way as room use, and the team builds it into the product. Owners decide the meaning; the data team builds and reviews the code; no owner or steward reviews a pull request (the author, 10 November 2026).

### 2 · Every question, one door · 2:47–4:05

## Rigour to check before recording

Checked on 10 November 2026; the sources are listed below.

- **"For over a thousand years."** Lansing and Kremer (1993) open: "For over a thousand years, generations of Balinese farmers have gradually transformed the land". The film says "over a thousand years" and gives no first date.
- **The subak and the water temple.** A subak is the group of farmers who take water from one canal and decide together how to share it; the subaks along a river meet at water temples, which set a calendar of planting so that the water is shared and fields harvested together lie fallow together, starving the pests (Lansing 1991 and 2007, chapters 1 to 3; Lansing and Kremer 1993). The calendar's "Days 1 to 10 / 10 to 20 / 20 to 30" on screen is an illustration of planting in turns, not a real temple's schedule.
- **The push to plant fast.** Lansing (2007, introduction): the Green Revolution gained "a firm foothold in Indonesia by the early 1970s. In Bali, the Green Revolution was accompanied by new government agricultural policies that promoted continuous cropping of the new rice in an effort to boost rice production. Farmers were encouraged to plant rice as quickly as possible, without regard for traditional irrigation schedules"; and in chapter 6, a report for the Department of Public Works found that "the farmers were pushed to plant rice at the highest possible frequency each year, which gave rise to disorganization in water use". The film says it without blame: "The planners wanted the country fed, and the new rice gave more grain."
- **Two million tons, 1977.** Lansing (2007, chapter 6) on the brown planthopper's plague of the late 1970s; the film's "about two million tons of rice across Indonesia in 1977", and the screen's "Indonesia, 1977 · 2 million tons · of rice destroyed", follow his figure.
- **Water downstream.** Lansing's model (1993) and his account of the 1980s (2007, chapter 6) show water shortages for downstream subaks when cropping is uncoordinated; the film's "farms downstream waited for water that didn't come". "For a farming family, a lost harvest meant a season's food and income gone" is said generally, with no number.
- **The model.** Lansing and Kremer (1993): a simulation of the Oos and Petanu rivers with "each of the 172 subaks"; it showed the temple-scale pattern giving the best harvests by balancing pest damage against water stress. In the second run, "each subak then compared its yield with those of its four closest neighbors" and copied the best; "after eight years, average yields peaked", and "a complex structure of coordinated cropping patterns emerged, which bore a remarkable similarity to the actual pattern of water temple coordination along these rivers". The film's picture is its own toy re-run, 172 cells between two drawn rivers, and its harvest line has no numbers (their average rose from slightly more than 5 to 8.57 tons a hectare, which the film doesn't say).
- **1988, the bank, and its report.** Lansing (2007, chapter 6, *Reevaluation*): "We reported these results to the Asian Development Bank in 1988 and received a sympathetic audience. The final evaluation report for the Bali Irrigation Project reversed the bank's earlier skepticism towards water temples"; the report "concluded with the observation that 'no post-evaluated project of the Bank exhibits self-sustained and high performance comparable to Bali'" (Lansing's note: Asian Development Bank, Post-Evaluation Office, PE-241 L-352-INO, Manila, 1988). The film's voice says it in plain words ("said no project it had reviewed ran itself as well as Bali's"), the sheet quotes it as Lansing does, and the bank is "the bank that had paid for new irrigation works", not named, since its name adds nothing for a newcomer. Pesticide use has also declined, as pest populations were brought under control by the system of regional fallow periods"; and in chapter 6, the water temples "have achieved recognition by state irrigation bureaucracies". The film's "Officials let the temples set the planting again" is that recognition, in plain words.

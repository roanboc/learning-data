## The promise

Every line passes two tests: a newcomer can follow it, and a data quality lead who has run Thomas Redman's measurement and a platform engineer who profiles tables on Databricks each find nothing wrong in it. *Good enough for what?* agreed what the graduation list is for and what each use needs. This film measures it before anyone writes a rule: first by looking, the people who know the list marking a hundred of its most recent records by hand and counting the perfect ones (79 of 100), then by profiling, the platform describing every column of the whole list and showing where to look (names cut at 30 characters, names in capitals, accents gone). Only then come the checks, starting with what the profile showed, each with an owner and a level agreed in advance. And it ends on what no check of the list can see, which is the next film's subject. The takeaway: "Look at a hundred records before you write a single check."

## The story in one paragraph

Dublin, 1899: a young chemist, William Gosset, joins a large brewery that wants better barley. A trial means a few plots and a whole season, so it gives only a handful of results, and a handful can mislead; the mathematics of the day worked only for large numbers. Gosset works out how far a few results can be trusted, tries it by hand on 3,000 cards drawn four at a time, and in 1908 publishes it with tables, as "Student". Now a few careful trials are enough to decide, and his tables say when more are needed. At the university, the graduation list passed every check, yet 41 degree certificates were printed again in December 2025. On Friday 6 February 2026, Malia Fonoti, Mei Tanaka and Jun Park look at the hundred most recent records of December's list, mark every wrong field, and count the perfect ones: 79. Good to about eight either way, it's enough to act on, and it raises a puzzle *The hidden factory* answers: over 400 wrong records, only 41 reprints. Then Jun profiles the whole list on Databricks: the longest family name is exactly the field's 30 characters, the same names appear in capitals, and with three custom metrics he counts 41 names cut short, 312 in capitals (none in the hundred, which were all students admitted since 2020) and not one accent. Only then does he write checks, from what the profile showed: three that hold the list for a person, owned by Mei. The swapped names, awards, ceremonies and dates are allowed values, and wrong: no check of the list can see them, so the next film finds something outside it to compare with.

## Sound

A new palette ([`source/tools/score.py`](source/tools/score.py)), checked on 5 November 2026 against every `score.py` on main and on every branch and against every palette in the project files (the module's `palettes.md`, where the module's setup reserved the lead and this film's thread took the partner): **a Savart wheel** and **Savart's trapezoid violin**, both made in the score. The pair is one scientist measuring sound two ways, as the film measures a list two ways: by counting and by listening.

- **The Savart wheel**, the toothed wheel of the 1830s: a card held against its turning teeth makes one click per tooth, and the clicks, close enough, become one note; Félix Savart used it to measure the pitches the ear can hear. Several wheels on one axle, their teeth in the ratios of a chord, play a chord. Played as short plain notes, the rasp kept soft, lower and drier than *Modern data basics*' telegraph reeds, and never a burst of clicks. It counts: the records, the measures, the checks.
- **Savart's trapezoid violin** of 1819: the same scientist's plain violin, a flat, symmetrical box with straight sound holes, built from his theory and then judged by ear, when a committee of the Academy of Sciences and the Academy of Fine Arts heard it played and found its tone very clear and even, but somewhat subdued. Bowed softly and low (G3 to D5), plain, with no vibrato: a few long notes and two-note chords. It listens: the people who know the list.
- **The mark:** a few separate clicks that, faster, become one clear note (the hundred records becoming one measure); at the end, once the wheel has stopped, the violin plays that note on. G major; E minor for the problem.
- **Under the opening,** the violin's low G, held, and the wheel's short chords at the scenario's lines; E minor at "mislead"; the wheel's single clicks, slow, at "a handful"; "Something had to change" on E minor, let go; the mark as Gosset works it out; one soft click for each of the four cards drawn, one at a time; G major at "1908"; two notes for the two tags; the violin's G again at "for data", and the mark, softer, under the title card.
- **After the opening,** music comes only at a chapter's turn, under a moment on screen or in a pause without words, never a bed under the voice: a click as each field is lit; one soft texture as the 79 tags come, never one sound for each; the mark under the gauge; a violin note for each person named; E minor at the puzzle; the wheel at speed, one clear note, as the profile reads every row; three short notes for the three custom metrics; three chords for the three checks; a falling minor third for "Allowed, and wrong"; the mark and the violin's note at the end card.
- **Effects,** each tied to something on screen at its moment, with the same word and offset as the picture: a card set down, a pencil mark, a tag tied on, a drawer pulled, a page turned, a key pressed. All low and soft, below about 2.5 kHz, about 16 dB under the music; one at a time.
- **Nothing borrowed** from the opening's culture: nothing Irish, as the module's palettes ask. No urgent pulse (`film.json`'s `urgent` is empty).

## Rigour to check before recording

Checked on 5 November 2026.

- **Gosset at the brewery.** William Sealy Gosset (1876 to 1937) joined the brewery in Dublin in 1899 after a degree in chemistry at New College, Oxford (MacTutor). The brewery isn't named, as the module's brief asks. "A young chemist": he was 23. "It wants better barley": the brewery ran trials of barley varieties and of how to grow them, which is what made its experiments small (MacTutor: the t-test was invented "to handle small samples for quality control in brewing"). "Live with it for a year" is the film's plain word for a choice that waits a season to be tried again.
- **The problem, in his words.** *The probable error of a mean* (*Biometrika*, vol. 6, no. 1, March 1908, pp. 1 to 25), introduction: "it is sometimes necessary to judge of the certainty of the results from a very small sample", and such experiments, "most agricultural and large-scale experiments", have "hitherto been almost outside the range of statistical inquiry". The narration's "The mathematics of the day worked for large numbers of measurements. Nobody could say how far a few could be trusted" says that plainly.
- **By hand, on cards.** Section VI: "Before I had succeeded in solving my problem analytically, I had endeavoured to do so empirically", with measurements "written out on 3000 pieces of cardboard, which were then very thoroughly shuffled and drawn at random… each consecutive set of 4 was taken as a sample—750 in all". The measurements were of 3,000 criminals, from a published table (heights and finger lengths); the film says only "measurements", as nothing in the idea needs whose they were.
- **"As Student".** The paper's byline is "By STUDENT". The brewery let its scientists publish on condition that they mentioned neither the brewery nor beer, and didn't sign with their own names (the published accounts, from Ziliak; Wikipedia cites them). His internal report of 1904 was *The application of the "law of error" to the work of the Brewery*. Not used, because no source read today confirms them: the barley varieties, hops, and the story of a "Pupil" pen name.
- **"Tables anyone could use".** The paper gives tables for samples of 4 to 10 (its conclusion 4: "Tables are given by which it can be judged whether a series of experiments, however short, have given a result which conforms to any required standard of accuracy or whether it is necessary to continue the investigation"), the film's "and his tables said when more were needed".
- **The kiln-dried seed.** The paper's barley illustration, from published agricultural trials: seven varieties sown both kiln-dried and not kiln-dried in 1899, and four in 1900, eleven pairs; "the odds are about 14 to 1 that kiln-dried corn gives the higher yield" (the film's "probably gave a little more grain") and "the odds are very great that kiln-dried seed gives barley of a worse quality" (its "almost certainly worse barley"). "In his paper" because his illustrations use published data, not the brewery's own.
- **"Still used".** Student's t, as it came to be called, is taught and used in medicine, agriculture and industry today; the film says "his way", the idea of judging how sure a few results can make you.
- **The university, the list and its people.** The module's invented ones (numbers.md): 2,031 graduates in December 2025; 41 certificates printed again; Malia Fonoti, Mei Tanaka and Jun Park. "In January, they agreed what the list is for" is *Good enough for what?*'s story; its words are to be matched to that film's on main before this one merges.
- **The hundred-record measure.** Redman's method, as the module's numbers give it: the people who know the data take the most recent hundred records and mark every field that's wrong; a record with nothing wrong is perfect; the share of perfect records is the measure. Redman's own words: "I call it 'the Friday Afternoon Measurement'" and "many people report that FAM works well any day of the week" (his interview with EWSolutions, updated 19 March 2026); and the 2017 summary in *Harvard Business Review* (Nagle, Redman and Sammon): "assemble 100 data records completed by your department and work through each". Five fields, not the ten to fifteen often advised, because those are the fields the certificate and the ceremony use. Two hours: the length of the meeting usually advised. "A pioneer of data quality": Redman has worked on data quality since the 1980s.
- **79, and its errors.** numbers.md: the first measure, Friday 6 February 2026, on the last hundred records added to December 2025's list, the last list built in full: 79 perfect; 21 records with at least one error, 26 errors in all: names 15 (accents lost 8, cut short 3, swapped 4), awards 6 (a double degree as one award 4, a major printed as the award 2), ceremonies 3, conferral dates 2. All hundred are of students admitted since 2020, so none is in capitals.
- **"Good to about eight either way."** For 79 of 100, the usual margin for a share at 95% is 79 ± 8.0 (71 to 87); the Wilson interval is 70.0 to 85.8 and the exact one 69.7 to 86.5, so "about eight" and "between 71 and 87" hold. This is the margin for a share, not Gosset's formula, which is for an average: the film calls it "Gosset's question", not his answer. It holds only if the hundred are like the rest, which the narration says: the most recent hundred aren't a random sample. The hundred are taken by the day each joined the list (`added_on`, film 1's model), then by key. Databricks' `TABLESAMPLE` would give a sample, but its docs warn that `TABLESAMPLE (num_rows ROWS)` "is not a simple random sample but instead is implemented using LIMIT"; the film uses the most recent, as Redman's method does.
- **The puzzle.** 41 of 2,031 is 2.0%; 21% of December's list (a row per certificate, so a little over 2,031 rows) is over 400. *The hidden factory* explains it: two people fixed most errors by hand before the list went, and the hidden work hid the true error rate (numbers.md). This film says only that someone had been putting most of them right by hand.
- **Databricks' data profiling.** *Data profiling* (updated 11 September 2026): "For each profile, Databricks automatically creates a dashboard"; a snapshot profile "calculates data quality metrics over all data in the table" ("The maximum table size for a snapshot profile is 4TB"). *Create a profile using the Databricks UI* (updated 11 September 2026): in Catalog Explorer, the table's **Quality** tab, **Enable** or **Configure**, then in the **Data Quality Monitoring** dialog, **Data profiling**, **Configure**, and the **Profile type**; "To configure a custom metric, click **Add custom metric**" (Name, Type: Aggregate, Derived or Drift, Input columns, Output type, Definition). *Profile metrics table* (updated 11 September 2026): for a column, `count` ("Number of non-null values"), `num_nulls`, `percent_null`, `distinct_count` (approximate: "This uses the approx_count_distinct function, so the result may not be exact"), `min_len`, `max_len`, `avg_len` ("Minimum/Maximum/Average length of string and binary values"), and `frequent_items` ("Top 100 most frequently occurring items"). There is no pattern metric, which is why Jun adds his own. *Custom metrics* (updated 21 January 2025): an aggregate metric is one SQL expression, which "cannot contain joins or subqueries"; "To indicate that more than one column is used in the calculation, use `:table`", which lets the metrics count distinct graduates. The slice by ceremony season uses the profile's metric slicing expressions. None of these pages showed a release status on 5 November 2026, and the film says none.
- **The notebook's data profile.** In a notebook, the table's result shows **+ › Data Profile**, summary statistics and histograms for each column (Databricks, *Notebook outputs and results*); `dbutils.data.summarize` gives the same in code, and its docs page says "This feature is in Public Preview" (5 November 2026). The film shows the click, and says no status.
- **The profile's findings.** numbers.md, *What profiling found*: no letter outside A to Z in December's list; 312 graduates' family names in capitals only, every one of a student admitted before 2020; 41 family names exactly 30 characters long, the length of the student system's field. Why (the nightly file's old encoding, until September 2026; the older records entered in capitals; the field's length) is *The hidden factory*'s, and isn't said here. The frequent items on screen, "Nguyen" and "Smith" with their capital twins, are drawn for the film from common family names, with made-up counts, and aren't any person's.
- **"A list with none isn't chance."** Eight of the hundred had lost an accent, so at least about one name in twelve should have one; the chance that 200 such names have none is about 0.92^200, under one in ten million. The check is set at more than 200 names so a small slice of the list doesn't trip it.
- **The checks.** numbers.md, *The first checks*: owner Mei Tanaka; each holds for a person (amber; dbt `severity: warn`), none stops the list; level agreed with Malia in advance; nightly from Monday 9 February 2026; on December 2025's list, the length check holds 41, the capitals check 312, and the accent check the whole list. dbt's `severity` config: a test with `warn` reports its failures and doesn't fail the run. The checks' `meta` (owner, dimension) follows the project's tests (`tests/generic/builtin.sql`).
- **"Watches the rest."** Databricks' data quality monitoring, *Quality on the platform*'s subject (*Governance you can use*): the film names it and doesn't teach it again, as the brief asks.
- **"Allowed, and wrong."** The award on screen, "Bachelor of Laws" for a graduate of Arts and Laws, is one of the hundred's four double degrees recorded as one award (numbers.md), the error *Valid isn't true*'s reconciliation finds on the March list. That film is the next one: "That's the next film".
- **Not shown.** Time-series and inference profiles, drift metrics, anomaly detection's own checks, alerts, Lakehouse Monitoring's older names, how the profile's dashboard is laid out, and any other tool for profiling.

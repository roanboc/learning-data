## The promise

Every line passes two tests: someone who has only heard of AI agents can follow it, and a Databricks platform lead or a data engineer who builds agents finds nothing wrong in it. Series 2 so far showed how agents do data work well, with no product: what they read, their skills, tools with the fewest rights, memory the team shares, a check decided first, and the ways they go wrong. This film shows where that work can live. At the Faculty of Health, three people have wired their own agents to their own copies of the data, and one question gets three answers, none with its evidence. On Databricks, every agent reads the same governed tables and definitions through the same tools, and the answers agree. The platform gives agents four things: agents that answer business questions over governed data (Genie One and the Genie Agents the data team builds, tested on the traps that fooled Health's agents); tool servers any agent can use, run by the platform (queries, governed functions, search, and Genie's own); agents the team builds in code and tests before anyone relies on them (Agent Bricks); and coding agents that work on the pipelines and the dbt project, reading the team's own skills (Genie Code, and Jun's own agent). People still review and approve. **The platform gives agents the same data and tools people use, in one place.**

## The story in one paragraph

Monday, at the Faculty of Health. Anil Kurian plans next year's lab space, equipment and research support, and he starts from Health's active research projects: those that have started and not yet ended. He asks his agent, and two colleagues ask theirs; each set up their own, with their own copy of the data. Anil's reads a spreadsheet made for the yearly research report, which counts a project shared with another faculty as half, and reads it as a count: 141. The second, with its own copy of the projects table, counts every project in a unit with Health in its name, and so the 9 projects of Engineering's Bridge Health Lab, which tests bridges, not people: 177. The third reads a folder of reports and says 168. One is right, and nobody can tell which, because none shows what it read. Planned for the lowest, 27 projects would have no lab space; and a copy of the projects table, with every researcher's name in it, sits on a laptop. Something had to change. Sam, who runs the platform, and Jun, who builds the data project, bring the agents to the data, on Databricks: every agent now reads the same governed tables, definitions and tools as everyone else, and only what its own person may read. A week later all three answer 168, each with its query, its table and its definition; Anil checks it in a minute and plans space for every project, and the copies are deleted. The film then opens the platform's four ways. Databricks holds the data, governed in Unity Catalog, where people already work. Genie One answers business questions and says where its words come from; for questions that need care, the data team builds Genie Agents, and its *Research projects* agent reads the governed table of projects and who leads each, takes the team's definitions as its instructions, and is tested on the questions behind Health's three answers, each with its right answer in SQL. The platform runs tool servers for queries, governed functions and search, and Genie One and each Genie Agent have their own: Anil's agent connects to them with his own sign-in, and every agent calls the count of active projects the data team wrote once, as a function; the servers follow the open standard, MCP. The team builds a project check agent in code, with Agent Bricks, for the night the count disagrees with the Research Office's list: Agent Runtime runs it, every step is traced, and before anyone relies on it, it's tested on past cases (the 12 projects given more time, whose first end date has passed; two projects entered twice; Health's three answers), scored by the platform's judges, a plain test of the sums, and Mei and Jun; its note is evidence, and Jun decides. Genie Code works beside Sam in the pipeline editor, with his rights, and reads the team's skills, in the same open format as Jun's agent; Jun's own coding agent works on the dbt project and reaches the platform through the same tool servers, and Databricks' own skills teach it the platform. What either writes is a draft; a person reviews it, and a second person approves what reaches production. Back at the faculty, the three agents are still their owners' own, but they read the same governed data through the same tools, and each answers 168, with its evidence. One question is left for the next film: when an agent reads on its own, whose reads are they, and who pays for its model?

**Where the film adopts, extends or differs.** It adopts the platform's own split, as Databricks' docs put it: "Skills teach the agent how to work; MCP servers let it do the work." It extends the docs with the module's rules: the count of active projects is a plain function, deterministic, which agents call rather than write; the team's agent is tested on its own past cases before anyone relies on it; its note is evidence, and a person decides; drafts are reviewed by a person and approved by a second one before production. It leaves out what the docs offer and the film doesn't need: Genie Code's own classifier for approving each tool action (the film shows only that it works with its user's rights), Genie Ontology, agent memory in Lakebase (*Context and memory, governed*), and the gateway the managed tool servers run through, with its policies and costs (*Why Unity Catalog and Unity Gateway govern them*).

**Practice, from the author (the plan's insights):** from each person wiring their own agent to a shared team context, the insight this film carries; automate deterministically, and keep agents for what isn't deterministic; a person in every critical loop, and every change to production approved by a second person, for agents as for people. A practitioner will recognise the opening: a number exported once, for one purpose, read later for another, by an agent that sounds sure and shows nothing.

**What the film leaves to its siblings.** Who an agent is, what it may read and who pays for its model are *Why Unity Catalog and Unity Gateway govern them*'s (grants, masks, row filters, audit, the gateway); the team's context and memory as governed assets are *Context and memory, governed*'s. *Meaning on the platform* showed Genie One citing the glossary and a Genie agent built on a metric; *An agent on the team* showed the agent's own identity and schemas: this film names them, and doesn't show them again.

### 2 · One place · 2:06–2:48

## The promise

Every line passes two tests: someone who has only heard of AI agents can follow it, and a platform lead, a data engineer or a security specialist finds nothing wrong in it. The series so far showed how an agent does data work well: what it reads, its skills, tools with the fewest rights, memory the team shares, and the check decided before it starts. This film shows the six ways it most often goes wrong at the university, each blameless, each met by a design: a column that isn't there, a confidently wrong number, a test weakened to pass, an instruction hidden in a ticket, a loop that runs all night, and a simple job made complicated. The author's insight runs under all six: agents can overcomplicate things and cost more, so automate what can be automated with plain steps, and keep agents for what can't.

**Takeaway:** "Expect these, and design for them."

## The story in one paragraph

Lisbon, 1 November 1755, All Saints' Day: at twenty to ten in the morning the churches are full when the ground shakes for several minutes. Churches and houses fall with people inside; about forty minutes later a great wave from the sea rushes up the river into the lower town, and fires burn for days. By most estimates, tens of thousands die, and the heart of the city lies in ruins. It had failed in every way at once: walls that broke, fire that leapt from house to house, narrow, crowded lanes; and the ground would shake again. So the king's engineers rebuild the lower town for each way it failed: a cage of timber inside the walls, braced with crosses, that bends instead of breaking; stone walls between the houses raised above the roofs, so fire can't leap across; wide, straight streets in place of the lanes. The king's minister sends thirteen questions to every parish: when did it start, how long did it last, what did the sea do, what fell, and had one struck before? The lower town still stands, more than 250 years on, one of the first places in Europe built to withstand an earthquake, and scientists still read the parishes' answers. At the university, Jun's agent works on the census-day count, the number the university reports to the government, and agents go wrong in a few known ways too, none of them anyone's fault. Six of them, each with its design: names checked in the catalog and every draft built in the sandbox first; every number with its query and a second way to reach it; tests that only a person changes; what the agent reads treated as data, never orders, with hands that can't send; limits on rounds, time and money for every loop, and a plain check first; and plans that name their tools, with plain scripts for the steps that can be written down. As Lisbon's builders did, the team expects each one and builds for it, so it can stand behind the number it sends: 18,412 students on census day.

## The reference, and practice

The brief names two references: **OWASP's agentic threats** and **workflows versus agents**. The film names security experts once and teaches what the references share, in plain words, not their codes.

| Body of knowledge | What it adds | Where the film uses it |
|---|---|---|
| OWASP GenAI Security Project, *Top 10 for Agentic Applications 2026* (December 2025) | Agent goal hijack first (ASI01): agents and their models "cannot reliably distinguish instructions from related content", so hidden instructions in web pages, documents or email from outside steer them; its first mitigations, "Treat all natural-language inputs ... as untrusted" and least privilege for tools. Tool misuse (ASI02), with "Loop amplification: Planner repeatedly calls costly APIs, causing DoS or bill spikes" and "Adaptive Tool Budgeting"; cascading failures (ASI08), with "quotas, progress caps, circuit breakers" | *A line hidden in a ticket*: "Security experts put this risk first" |
| OWASP, *Top 10 for LLM Applications 2025* | Indirect prompt injection, "when an LLM accepts input from external sources, such as websites or files" (LLM01); misinformation, "false or misleading information that appears credible", answers "that sound correct but are completely unfounded" (LLM09); unbounded consumption, "excessive and uncontrolled inferences, leading to ... economic losses" (LLM10) | *A confident wrong number*, *A line hidden in a ticket*, *A loop all night* (none named) |
| OWASP Agentic Security Initiative, *Agentic AI: Threats and Mitigations* (February 2025) | Resource overload (T4) and cascading hallucination (T5) as agent threats, with rate limits and quotas as their controls | *A loop all night*, *A confident wrong number* (not named) |
| METR, *Recent frontier models are reward hacking* (5 June 2025) | "The most recent frontier models have engaged in increasingly sophisticated reward hacking, attempting (often successfully) to get a higher score by modifying the tests or scoring code" | *A test weakened to pass*: "Researchers have caught recent models changing tests to get a pass" |
| Schluntz and Zhang, *Building effective agents* (19 December 2024) | "Workflows are systems where LLMs and tools are orchestrated through predefined code paths"; agents "dynamically direct their own processes and tool usage"; "we recommend finding the simplest solution possible, and only increasing complexity when needed. This might mean not building agentic systems at all"; "The autonomous nature of agents means higher costs, and the potential for compounding errors"; "stopping conditions (such as a maximum number of iterations) to maintain control" | *A simple job made complicated* and *A loop all night* (not named) |

**Where the film adopts, extends or differs.** It adopts the security experts' central point, that anything an agent reads can carry instructions and that a model can't reliably tell them from data, so the design must not depend on the model telling them apart: least privilege and a person, not a filter. It adopts the workflow guidance in the module's words, "automate deterministically first". It extends both with the university's practice: every number with its query and a second, independent way to reach it; tests that only a person changes, written first; limits on rounds, time and money, and a stop when the same error comes back; and a plain check of the inputs before any agent starts. It differs from the security lists in tone: it tells each failure blamelessly, as a design gap, not as an attacker's success or an agent's fault, except where an attacker is the story (the hidden line).

**Practice, from the author (the plan's insights):** agents can overcomplicate things and cost more; automate deterministically, and keep agents for what isn't deterministic; a plan that doesn't say what the agent may use wastes its effort (the author's example, told in full in *Plan, define the check, do, check*); agents are never approvers; tool servers get the fewest rights; in test, an agent may do agreed tasks alone and report. A practitioner will recognise each of the six from their first months with agents: the invented column name that the compiler catches, the number that sounds right and isn't, the test "fixed" to pass, the injected line in a pasted email, the bill after a night of retries, and the nine-file change for a one-line job.

## Sound

**The palette, its own** (reserved for this film by the series' thread on 4 November 2026, after checking every `score.py` on main and on every branch): a **trautonium**, the electronic instrument of 1930 played by pressing a wire onto a rail, its sawtooth made soft by its filters, one note at a time, with its undertones sounding below it (what lies under each failure); and **bell plates**, low ones, C3 to E4, struck softly with a felt mallet and damped by the hand. Both are made in the film's own `tools/score.py` and low-passed, so nothing rings above about 2.5 kHz. No vibrato, nothing that trembles, no slides and no drone under the voice; nothing borrowed from Portugal.

- **The film's mark is a note with its undertone, resolved.** Each failure turns on a trautonium note with an undertone that doesn't sit (the seventh undertone, a seventh below the note, at 4/7 of its pitch); each design resolves it: the undertone is switched to the octave below, or to the fifth below, and it sits. A divider is set by a switch, so the undertone moves at once, with a short crossfade, never a slide. Each way has its own note (G, A, E, D, C and F), so no figure comes back within 20 seconds; at the close, the six resolve one after another as the six cards turn green.
- **Keys, all tuned pure** (just intonation from C): C minor for 1755, made from the trautonium's own undertones; C major for the city rebuilt and for the close. No two held notes beat, and each keeps its own level in the room: the room is set by its gain where the notes are, and each held note is set back to its own level after it.
- **Under the opening:** a low bell plate as the film opens; C minor under the city; the music thins under the trembling, a falling G, E flat, C, and stops before "By most estimates": that line goes without music. ## Rigour to check before recording


Checked on 4 November 2026; the sources are listed below.

- **The day and the hour.** Wikipedia, *1755 Lisbon earthquake* (read 4 November 2026): "it took place at approximately 09:40 local time" on 1 November 1755, All Saints' Day (the film says "the morning"); "Contemporary reports state that the earthquake lasted from three and a half to six minutes" (the film's "for several minutes"); "Approximately 40 minutes after the earthquake, a tsunami engulfed the harbor and downtown area, rushing up the Tagus river". Churches full: the earthquake struck during the morning's services of a holy day; churches fell with people inside. The film says "a great wave from the sea" rather than "tsunami", so no word needs explaining.
- **Fires for days.** Britannica, *Lisbon earthquake of 1755*: those killed include people who died "in fires that burned throughout Lisbon for about six days following the shock". Wikipedia's article says only that the fire "burned for hours"; the film says "for days", with Britannica.
- **How many died.** The estimates differ: Álvaro Pereira (*The Journal of Economic History*, 2009), 30,000 to 40,000 of Lisbon's roughly 200,000 people (as Wikipedia reports it); Britannica, about 60,000 in the city; one recent evaluation of the records, reported second-hand, about 12,000. The film says "By most estimates, tens of thousands of people died", which all but the lowest support, shows no number on screen beyond those words, and shows no one hurt.
- **How the old town failed.** The fire after the earthquake "caused a large number of deaths" (Cardoso, Lopes and Bento, 2004); the lower town was densely built, on narrow medieval streets that the new plan replaced with "an orthogonal city" (Wikipedia, *Pombaline style*). "Walls that broke, fire that leapt from house to house, and narrow, crowded lanes" says those three in plain words.
- **"The ground would shake again."** Lisbon had been struck before (the inquiry's tenth question asks for memory of another earthquake) and has been shaken since; the film claims no later test of the rebuilt town.
- **The engineers and the minister.** Wikipedia: "On 4 December 1755, a little more than a month after the earthquake, Manuel da Maia, chief engineer to the realm, presented his plans for the rebuilding of Lisbon"; the plan chosen was drawn by the military engineers Eugénio dos Santos and Carlos Mardel (dated 12 June 1758). The film says "the king's engineers" and "the king's minister", not naming Sebastião José de Carvalho e Melo, later Marquis of Pombal, a contested figure (the series' note): the story needs only the office.
- **The timber cage.** Cardoso, Lopes and Bento, "Earthquake resistant structures of Portuguese old 'Pombalino' buildings", 13th World Conference on Earthquake Engineering, Vancouver, 2004, paper 918: each building has "an interior three-dimensional braced timber structure named 'gaiola' enclosed in masonry walls", aiming at "providing resistance to horizontal forces"; tests on its wall panels show it "exhibits ductile behavior and allows some energy dissipation". Wikipedia, *Pombaline style*: "A flexible wooden structure implanted on the walls, floors and roofs". The film's "a cage of timber, braced with crosses, that bends instead of breaking" is that in plain words. The paper's PDF refused automated reading on the day (HTTP 403); its words come from the search results' extracts, so a reader with access can confirm them.
- **The walls above the roofs.** Cardoso, Lopes and Bento (2004): "the masonry walls between adjoining buildings have no openings and extend beyond the roofs", "in order to prevent the fire propagation between buildings"; a study of one Pombaline building in Lisbon (Rossio 62) finds its party walls about 0.60 m thick and "projecting above the roof", which gives them "the role of a fire-break" (in Portuguese). Wikipedia, *Pombaline style*: "Each building is isolated by walls to stop the spread of fires."
- **The streets.** Wikipedia, *Pombaline style*: the new city "was laid out on a grid plan with roads and pavements fixed at 40 ft wide (12 m)"; "the medieval streets gave place to an orthogonal city". The film says "wide, straight streets, in place of the lanes", and doesn't say they were meant as escape routes: no source read on the day says so.
- **The soldiers, left out.** Wikipedia's article says "Small wooden models were built for testing, and earthquakes were simulated by marching troops around them", without a citation; LisbonQuake's page on the cage says it was perhaps tested at full scale in the riverside square, or demonstrated rather than tested, and that no technical drawings of the system are known. No source of the time was found, so the film leaves the soldiers out.
- **The thirteen questions.** LisbonQuake, *Inquérito* (from the Torre do Tombo, the national archive): on 26 January 1756 (its reproduced letter is dated 20 January) the minister sent the bishops of the kingdom "an inquiry, or Inquérito, with 13 questions", which each bishop sent to every parish priest; "A period of one month was given to parish priests to answer". Its questions, as the page gives them in English: "1º. - At what time did the earthquake of the first of November begin and how long did it last?", "3º. - How many houses were ruined in each parish, if there were any notable buildings in it, and the state in which they remained.", "5º. - What new things were seen in the sea, the springs and the rivers?", "10º. - If there is memory that at some time there was another Earthquake and what damage has it done in each place?". The film's "when did it start, how long did it last, what did the sea do, what fell, and had one struck before?" is those four in plain words; the sheet on screen shortens them.
- **The answers, still read.** LisbonQuake: the inquiry "is considered by many as the origin of modern seismology", and the archive keeps the answers. Wikipedia, *Parochial Memories of 1758* (the later inquiry, 60 questions, one of them on the earthquake's damage): "By analysing and cross-referencing the parish priests' accounts, modern scientists are able to reconstruct the event with some degree of scientific precision". The film's "And scientists still read the parishes' answers" and "every parish answered the same questions, so each answer could be checked against the others" rest on these two.
- **Still standing, and among the first.** Wikipedia: "The Pombaline buildings are among the earliest seismically protected constructions in Europe" (no citation); Cardoso, Lopes and Bento (2004) describe their "anti-seismic provisions". The lower town is on Portugal's tentative list for World Heritage as *Pombaline Lisbon* (UNESCO, submitted 31 January 2017). The rebuilding began in 1758 and went on for decades, so the film says "more than two hundred and fifty years on". People live and work there today.

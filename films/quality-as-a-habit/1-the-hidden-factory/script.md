## The promise

Every line passes two tests: someone who has never worked on data quality can follow it, and a data quality lead, an analytics engineer and a postal historian each find nothing wrong in it. The film shows what Thomas Redman calls the hidden data factory, after Armand Feigenbaum's hidden plant: where people quietly fix data by hand, there's a cost nobody counts and a cause nobody fixes, and the fixing hides how good the data really is. It shows how to count it (the hours, the rounds, the corrections), the rule of ten as a rule of thumb, and how to trace each kind of error back to where it's born, with five whys, a fishbone of people, process, systems and interfaces, and the lineage, then fix it there, with its owner, and keep a check at the source. It builds on *Fixed where it starts*, named in a line and not taught again. Takeaway: **Count the work spent fixing data by hand. Then fix where it's born.**

## The story in one paragraph

Washington, 1899: more than six million letters and parcels a year end up in the Dead Letter Office, letters the post can't deliver and can't send back. Most often people have moved on without leaving an address; or a letter to a city gives no street and number; or a town is spelt the way it sounds. In one room, clerks, many of them women, work out where each should have gone; the best known, Patti Lyle Collins, works out about a thousand a day. It's skilled work, unseen by the senders, and it never fixes the next letter, and nearly three million a year are destroyed. In 1943, with experienced clerks gone to the war, the largest cities get numbered zones, written by the sender, so new clerks can sort by the number; in 1963 the post brings in the ZIP Code, five digits on every letter, written by the sender; anyone, and soon machines, can sort a letter by its number; by 1969, 83% of Americans use it, and it has saved the post billions. The fix moved to the start, written once by the person who knows the address. At the university, Malia Fonoti and a colleague check every name on the graduation list by hand before every ceremony, in a spreadsheet: for December 2025, from Monday 20 October to Thursday 13 November, 2,031 names, and still 41 certificates printed again. A hundred records looked at closely in February found 21 with an error: one in five, but only 41 reprints? Redman calls it a hidden data factory: careful, needed until the errors stop, but it hides the hours, how good the data really is, and the cause. In August 2026 Noor counts it with Malia, three ways: the hours from the team's calendar (about 240, 50 and 30: 320 a year), the rounds from Databricks' system tables (the query history and the audit log show the list queried and downloaded on three Mondays), and the corrections, the copy as it went to the printer against the list the platform built the night before: 517 names changed by hand and 26 more that got past, so 543 of 2,031 names were wrong when the list left the platform, about one in four. A right name takes about two minutes to check, a wrong one about twenty, so the wrong names took about 170 of December's 240 hours: the rule of ten. Beatriz Fonseca de Albuquerque Cavalcanti, whose family name has 33 characters, corrected it in the portal in February and her March certificate was right; in August her master's joins December's list, cut short again. Five whys find two causes, a field too short and no way back, and Unity Catalog's lineage shows the same path inside the platform. A fishbone finds them all: paper forms with one box for the whole name (35 swapped), every fix made in a copy, the 30-character field (39 cut) and an online form that took only A to Z in capitals until 2019 (312), and the nightly file's old encoding (143 names lost their accents on the way in). Nobody did anything wrong. Each is fixed where it starts, with its owner: on the night of Monday 14 September the nightly file keeps every letter and the field holds 100 characters; every correction is made once, in the student system, by Mei's office; old names are put right as each graduate confirms theirs; and Jun's checks in dbt, on the student system's records, hold a name cut at 30 or in capitals for Mei's office, and stop a night's file with not one accent. By the next morning, 161 names on December's list have their accents back, and nobody typed them; Beatriz's name is written in full, once, for every list after; and each round's hours go on the scorecard. The post put a code on letters at the start, written by the person who knew; at the university, each name is now put right where it's born, by the people who own it.

### 5 · Cut short again

**Narration.** Beatriz Fonseca de Albuquerque Cavalcanti graduates twice this year: a graduate certificate in March, a master's in December. Her family name has 33 characters. In February, the portal showed it cut short, and she corrected it: March's certificate was right. In August, her master's joins December's list, cut short again. - **The Dead Letter Office, 1899.** Patti Lyle Collins, "Why six million letters go astray every year", *The Ladies' Home Journal*, September 1899, p. 4: "during the last year 6,000,000,000 pieces of mail matter ... were posted in the United States. Of this number, 6,312,731 were sent to the dead-letter office, making an average receipt of about 21,000 letters and parcels for each working day"; the causes, "First and chiefly, to the migratory habits of our people, who move on without leaving address", then circulars sent to old lists, then "the necessity of giving the street and number when writing to a city"; 32,000 with no address at all; 2,973,987 "letters and circulars without inclosures of obvious value which could not be returned to the senders and were destroyed"; and her examples, among them "Aj tpar mass" for Hyde Park, Massachusetts. The film says "a year", not "that year": her figures are for "the last year" before September 1899. It doesn't use her words for the senders ("ignorance or carelessness"): their reasons are told without blame. Circulars sent to old lists are left out, for length.
- **The readers.** A dead letter is one the post can't deliver as addressed and can't return. The clerks who worked out the hardest addresses did "blind readings, so called because to the average postal worker the address would appear as though it was read blindfolded" (Smithsonian's National Postal Museum, quoted by *Smithsonian Magazine*); Collins was "popularly known as 'The Blind Reader'" (her own article's note) and redirected about a thousand letters a day (*Wikipedia*). The nickname is left out of the narration, for length. "Many of them women": the office's clerks were mostly women from the 1860s on (the Smithsonian's accounts), but no source gives a count for 1899, so the film doesn't say "most".
- **The zones, 1943.** "During World War II, thousands of experienced postal employees left to serve with the military. To offset the loss, in May 1943 the Post Office Department began a zoning address system in 124 of the largest cities" (USPS, 2014); "two-digit postal zones, which were written by mailers in the lower line of addresses" (USPS Office of Inspector General, *The Untold Story of the ZIP Code*, RARC-WP-13-006, 1 April 2013). Sources differ on the number of cities (124 in the postal service's accounts, 178 elsewhere), so the film says "big cities".
- **The ZIP Code, 1963.** Five digits, from 1 July 1963, written by the sender; "the basis for future postal code expansion and mechanical sorting"; in 1969, 83% of Americans said they used it (Roper's survey, in the Inspector General's Table 1); "It has saved, and continues to save, the Postal Service billions of dollars by streamlining the processing of mail" (the same report). The film doesn't claim that the ZIP Code ended dead letters: only that the fix moved to the start, to the person who writes the address.
- **The hidden data factory.** Thomas C. Redman, "Bad Data Costs the U.S. $3 Trillion Per Year", *Harvard Business Review*, 22 September 2016: "Department B, in addition to doing its own work, must add steps to accommodate errors created by Department A ... I call the added steps the 'hidden data factory'"; "many individuals simply make corrections themselves to complete the task at hand. They don't think to reach out to the data creator"; "very near term, you probably have to continue to do this work" (so the film calls the hand check careful and needed until the errors stop); and his advice, that the team that uses the data "must reach out to Department A, explain its requirements, cite some example errors, and share measurements". The same article names the rule of ten and the Friday Afternoon Measurement (*A hundred records*) as the two tools that "shine a harsh light" on hidden data factories.
- **The rule of ten.** Redman's rule of thumb: work done with a record that has an error costs about ten times as much to finish as with a perfect one. Said as a rule of thumb, never a law, and shown in the team's own minutes: about 2 for a right name, about 20 for a wrong one (find the document, phone the graduate, type it again).
- **The hidden plant.** Armand V. Feigenbaum's phrase, in *Total Quality Control*, for the part of a plant's capacity spent redoing work that went wrong. Named in a line, without a share of capacity: the figures in circulation (15 to 40 percent, 20 to 40 percent) trace to secondary accounts and a 1994 interview, not to a measure.
- **The five whys and the fishbone.** The five whys, Taiichi Ohno's practice at Toyota (*Toyota Production System*, 1978; in English, 1988): ask why until you reach a cause you can fix; here five, and two causes. The fishbone, Kaoru Ishikawa's cause-and-effect diagram (*Guide to Quality Control*): the problem at its head, the causes along its bones. Its bones for data, as the module's brief gives them, are people, process, systems and interfaces (where data passes from one system to another), not manufacturing's six Ms.
- **The tools, read 5 November 2026.** Databricks' query history system table (`system.query.history`) records queries run on SQL warehouses; its `statement_text` "returns `<REDACTED>` by default" and "account admins and members of the `databricks_pii_access` group can read the full query text", so Sam, who runs the platform, reads it.

## The promise

Every line passes two tests: anyone who has been stopped by a rule they didn't understand can follow it, and an experienced data governance lead, or someone who has led a change at work, finds nothing wrong in it. The film closes the first part of the series and is about its people side. In Bogotá in 1995, drivers stopped on the zebra crossings and people on foot had to weave between the cars, on roads where more than a thousand people a year died, and fines alone hadn't changed the habit. So the mayor sent mimes into the streets: when a car stopped on a crossing, the people around it whistled, a mime invited the driver to back up, and only if that failed did an officer write a fine, which the people watching applauded. The fine still came, but last, and it worked: by 1996 people on foot could cross in front of the cars, not between them. Most people keep a rule because they understand it and the people around them keep it; the fine is for the few. It's the same with data: when most people keep the rules, everyone who uses the data can trust it. So data governance's key challenge, and its first priority, is getting people to take part, to understand the data and its rules, to look after their data and answer for it, to make the decisions that are theirs and respect the ones that aren't, and to do what their role asks; and all of it is harder without tools that guide people and show them what their work changes. Governance has fines too, its checks, and most of its work comes before them: a page beside every check, written for the person it stops; office hours in place of a queue of approvals; stewards who share what broke and what helped; a new rule agreed with them, explained, and run as a warning until the day it's required, a date everyone knows; and measures read from the work, not a maturity score. The governance team's lasting job is to teach how the pieces fit, as the mimes did; as the other parts of data work get easier to run, the centre matters more, and the people in it most of all. Scattered tools have long made governance hard to organise and to see; that's changing, as the rest of the series shows. Kept up, governance does for data what the mimes did for the crossings: most people keep the rules before a check has to stop them, nobody has to guess what the data means, and a check stops only the few, who fix it in minutes. **Governance is people's work. Tools remember what people agreed.**

## The story in one paragraph

It's dangerous, more than a thousand people a year die on the city's roads, and fines alone haven't changed the habit: the city needs another way. The new mayor, Antanas Mockus, sends mimes into the streets. When a car stops on a crossing, the people around it whistle; a mime invites the driver, without a word, to back up; if the driver still won't, a traffic officer writes a fine, and the people watching applaud. It works: by 1996, three drivers in four keep the crossings clear, and people on foot cross in front of the cars, not between them. It's the same with data: when most people keep the rules, everyone who uses the data can trust it. Scattered tools have long made it hard to organise and to see; that's changing, as the rest of the series shows. Kept up, it does for data what the mimes did for the crossings: most people keep the rules before a check has to stop them, nobody has to guess what the data means, so the numbers agree, and a check stops only the few, who fix it in minutes.

## The reference, and practice

The series' reference is DAMA-DMBOK, named once, in *Off the shelf*; this film comes back to its wheel without naming it. For change, the plan names two models once, Kotter's eight steps and Prosci's ADKAR, and the film teaches what they agree on: people need to know why, to want it, to know how, to be able to, and to see it last. Other bodies of knowledge say the same in their own words, and the film draws on them without naming them:

| Body of knowledge | What it adds | Where the film uses it |
|---|---|---|
| Kotter's eight steps (*Leading Change*, 1996) | Urgency and a reason; a coalition of the willing; quick wins; anchoring the change so it outlasts its sponsors | The reason said first; the few who wanted to come; the habits that faded in Bogotá |
| Prosci's ADKAR (Hiatt, 2006) | Five things each person needs, in order: awareness, desire, knowledge, ability, reinforcement | Named, with its five needs, in chapter 8 |
| Mockus's law, morality and culture (*Cultura ciudadana*, 2001; his slides of 2003) | People keep a rule by conscience, by what the people around them do, and by the law; harmonise the three, the law last | The opening, and its order |
| Communities of practice (Lave and Wenger, 1991; Wenger, 1998) | People who do the same work learn from each other, regularly, by sharing practice | The stewards' monthly meeting |
| Diffusion of innovations (Rogers, 1962) | A practice spreads from the willing few to the rest, by what people see working | "Others joined once they saw it working" |
| Non-invasive data governance (Seiner, 2014) | Recognise the stewards already doing the work, rather than appoint new ones | The stewards are the people who already look after the data |
| Software practice: deprecation warnings, policies run as advisory before they're enforced | A change announced, warned and dated before it breaks anyone | Warn first, then required from a known date |

**Practice.** What practitioners recognise: rules people don't understand get switched off or worked around; error messages that say how to fix them get fixed; a new rule that fails everyone the day it lands teaches resentment; a community that meets to share, not approve, keeps coming; a maturity score says little about whether anyone uses governance, and coverage, time to fix and who comes back say more; and habits fade when the people who started them move on.

**The author's view** (27 October 2026), which chapters 2, 7 and 8 follow: the key challenge in data governance, and its first priority, is people's engagement in general, not only their agreement. That covers communicating, so people understand the data and data governance itself; taking part; responsibility and accountability; being willing to decide, and to respect the other authorities; and being willing to do the duties of their role. All of it is harder without practical tools that give people guidance, visibility and feedback on the impact of their work. Data governance has long been hard partly because its tools were scattered in many places, which left it disorganised and hard to see; that's changing, with tools the series shows later. People stay at the core, and will matter even more, as governance stays at the core of data management and matters more as data management gets easier to run.

## The look

*Watercolour*, the series' look since 17 October 2026, shared with the series on master and reference data, so the two look like one university.

- **Pencil and wash.** Every place is painted on paper, and people are drawn as the master data films draw them, with a soft wash of their side behind each one.
- **Only the university's places,** most of them the series' shared places, painted once: the floor of offices behind glass, Mei's desk in the registrar's office, Yusuf's office and its board (with the newest DMBOK standing on his desk, as in every film of the series), the stewards' meeting room, the faculty analyst's desk in the old building (and the same office seen from its other side, where a new analyst sits), and Noor's map. Each room keeps its wall colour. The film paints one place of its own: a crossing in the centre of Bogotá in 1995, and the same crossing years later.
- **Bogotá, painted the same way,** with the place and year in the corner: a crossing on a wide avenue of brick and concrete buildings, the green ridge of the eastern hills behind, with the white church on Monserrate, under a high grey sky. Drivers in small cars of the 1990s; people on foot in jackets and sweaters, a few with umbrellas; mimes in black with painted faces; a traffic officer in a plain uniform and cap, with a notebook. A warm umber wash behind each, as for people of the past. Nothing on screen is in Spanish: the signs are pictures, and the corner names the place. No style, pattern or music borrowed from Colombian culture: the story is told in the series' own voice.
- **Colour carries meaning,** as in the series: gold is governance's decisions, and the business side; cyan the technical side; green what runs in the work; grey what can go; red what nobody owns yet. Here, too, amber is a warning: a rule that warns, and hasn't stopped anyone yet.
- **The university's papers, not tools' screens.** The series' first part shows no files, code or tool screens (the author, 21 October 2026; the plan in three parts, approved on 23 October 2026). Where the work happens on a screen, the film shows the university's own papers: a table's headings in plain words, the sign-off sheet that films 2, 4 and 5 use too, a guide of a few plain lines, and plain scorecards. A change, not a pull request; a check, not a test; a table, not a model.
- **Fewer diagrams, more situations.** The ideas are people doing things: a mime's gesture, a page opened, a door card pinned up, a meeting, a date written down, a frame taken off a wall. The one diagram-like picture is the wheel, drawn by Yusuf's hand on a whiteboard.

## Rigour to check before recording

Checked on 20 and 21 October 2026, and the plain words of the series' first part, the opening's danger, need and relief, the story's close, and the author's notes on people on 27 October 2026, and the opening's four beats on 28 October 2026; the sources are listed below.

- **Bogotá, 1995, as Mockus wrote it.** Antanas Mockus was elected mayor for 1995 to 1997 (he left office on 10 April 1997 to run for president, and Paul Bromberg took his place for the rest of the term), and again for 2001 to 2003. The date and his successor are from Wikipedia's article on him; that he resigned to run for president is from Restrepo (note 3) and the same article. His own account (*Cultura ciudadana, programa contra la violencia en Santa Fe de Bogotá, Colombia, 1995-1997*, Inter-American Development Bank, technical study SOC-120, July 2001) describes the sequence the film shows, at the crossing of Avenida 19 and Carrera 7: people whistled at drivers who stopped on the zebra crossing; if the driver didn't back up, a mime appeared and tried to persuade him, with a "friendly and playful invitation"; if he still didn't, a traffic officer intervened, and "people ended up applauding, in sight of everyone, the officer's action of imposing the fine. So police repression was the last measure of a pedagogically ordered sequence." The film's "in an order meant to teach" is that last sentence. The mimes didn't replace fines, as the plan first said; they came before them.
- **When, and how many.** El Tiempo's *Diccionario de la lengua mockusiana* (26 January 2003): the mimes "went out on Calle 19 in March 1995, to teach pedestrians and drivers to cross at the zebras and keep the intersections clear". Mockus's account: from July 1995, for three months, 400 young mimes "with painted faces and dressed in black" taught without words and without shouting, then moved to the city's 19 urban localities. Other sources give 20 at first, and 420; the film gives no number. A contemporary report (El Espectador, 7 April 1995, quoted by Restrepo) found the first trial too local, a few blocks of Avenida 19, and warned that people might soon forget it.
- **"Three drivers in four."** Mockus's account: in 1996, 76.46% of drivers and 72.25% of pedestrians respected the zebra crossing; the campaign ran at 482 crossings with 425 people (mimes, police officers and others), and 364 crossings were marked. It gives no figure from before, so the film says only where it got to; the relief, "people on foot could cross in front of the cars, not between them", is the same figures in the street's terms. Road deaths: the film names them once, as the danger, "more than a thousand people a year die on the city's roads". Mockus's slides of 2003 chart Bogotá's deaths in traffic accidents, from the Instituto Nacional de Medicina Legal: 1,089 in 1991, 1,387 in 1995 and 1,301 in 1996, then falling to 697 in 2002; every year from 1991 to 1996 is over a thousand. His account gives a yearly rate of 25 per 100,000 at the start, falling to 20 over 1995 to 1997, alongside a 1 a.m. limit on alcohol sales, designated driver campaigns and more seat belts; the slides mark the police taking over traffic and better care before hospital on the same chart. El Tiempo (2012) gives 24 to 7 over his two terms, which the other sources don't confirm. Because the fall can't be credited to the mimes, the film doesn't say it: its relief is the crossings kept clear.
- **Why the crossings were a problem, and why another way.** Mockus's account gives the campaign's aim, its "desired change": "a better coexistence between pedestrians and drivers, and a greater awareness of why it's worth having and keeping rules for living together". His government's plan for 1995 to 1997 promised to "break with the excesses of the culture of the shortcut" (the slides of 2003), and the slides count among its results a "change in the habit of paying a bribe to avoid a traffic fine". The film says this in plain words, "Fines alone haven't changed the habit. The city needs another way.", and leaves the bribes out. The student the car edges towards is the film's own: neither source describes a near miss.
- **The opening's four beats.** The author's rule for every opening story (28 October 2026): scenario, problem, solution and benefits, each said outright, so a viewer who never heard of the event can say why it was a problem and what the fix gave people. Here: the scenario, Bogotá's centre in 1995; the problem, drivers stopped on the zebra crossings, so people on foot had to weave between the cars, in a city where more than a thousand people a year died on the roads, and fines alone hadn't changed the habit; the solution, the mayor's mimes, the people around whistling, and a fine only last; the benefits, by 1996 three drivers in four kept the crossings clear and people on foot crossed in front of the cars, not between them; and the same benefit for data, "When most people keep the rules, everyone who uses the data can trust it", which the film's end shows: the numbers agree.
- **"It worked."** Mockus's account, right after the sequence at the crossing: "the programme to instil respect for the zebra crossings was one of those that gave the best results; the citizens themselves began to exercise strong social control over offenders. In 1996, 76.46% of drivers and 72.25% of pedestrians respected the zebra crossing", and what set these campaigns apart was "their playful, not repressive, conception". The film's "It worked" is the account's "best results", and "the fine is for the few" its "social control" in plain words. It still credits no fall in road deaths to the mimes.
- **"Bogotá measured its crossings the same way."** The account gives its results as what people did: the drivers and the people on foot who respected the zebra crossings in 1996, and at the bus stops, the passengers who kept to them, 26.2% in 1995 and 38% in 1996 (and 43% of the small buses from February to May 1997). It doesn't say how they were measured, so the film says "measured", not "counted"; the ticks on three cars of four are the film's picture of the figure.
- **Why people keep a rule.** Mockus's slides for the IX Inter-American Conference of Mayors (Miami, 2003): three systems regulate behaviour, the law (admiration for it, or legal sanction), morality (one's own conscience, or guilt) and culture (social recognition, or social rejection), and the aim is to "strengthen the three regulatory systems" and "seek their harmony"; the mimes were "a new form of intervention by the authority", and the citizen cards (a white side with a thumb up, a red side with a thumb down, handed out in their hundreds of thousands) "peaceful and accepted interpersonal regulation". A 2009 profile quotes him: "Each of us must control our own inner lout. When we can't, our neighbour must. If that's not enough, then the police come in." The film's "most people keep a rule because they understand it, and because the people around them keep it; the fine is for the few" says this in plain words. Mockus's phrase "but in that order", quoted in an early note for the film, wasn't found in a source, and isn't used.
- **"The habits had faded."** El Tiempo, *Bogotanos enterraron en una década la cultura ciudadana de Mockus* (Carol Malaver, 15 September 2012): "What happened, for the ground gained by Mockus to be lost in little more than a decade?

## The promise

Every line passes two tests: anyone who has cleaned the same data twice can follow it, and an experienced data quality lead finds nothing wrong in it. The film is about where a quality problem is fixed. In Vienna in 1847, mothers died of fever far more often in the clinic where doctors worked than in the one where midwives did; Ignaz Semmelweis found the cause on the doctors' hands, on their way in from the autopsy room, and stopped it at the door. Where a problem shows is often not where it starts. At the university, the binder's policy 15 says the root causes of data quality issues are addressed; yet a warning comes back every week, and every week the issue is closed because staging cleans it. Followed back, it starts at a form. Fixed there, the clean-up can go, and its rule stays as a test that should never fail. Then something no rule names happens, and monitoring catches it, because it knows what the data usually looks like; once the surprise is confirmed, it becomes a rule. Nobody is blamed: people get the evidence and help to change. **Fix the cause, then make the fix a check.**

## The story in one paragraph

Vienna, 1847. The general hospital has two maternity clinics. In the first, where doctors and medical students work, about one mother in ten dies of fever; in the second, where midwives work, about one in thirty. Ignaz Semmelweis, a young doctor in the first clinic, looks for what's different: the doctors and students come to the ward from the autopsy room, and the midwives don't. He has everyone wash their hands in chlorinated lime before examining a mother, and within months deaths in the first clinic fall to the level of the second. Today, Yusuf opens binder 15: its policy says the root causes of data quality issues are addressed. But one cell on the quality scorecard is amber every Monday: short-course emails with spaces and capitals. Every week an issue goes to their owner, Tom's learning team, and every week it's closed, "cleaned in staging": for a year, staging has trimmed and lower-cased every email, every night, so a learner doesn't end up with two wallets, and every other system that reads the emails cleans them again. Yusuf follows one email back, from the wallet through staging to the short-course sign-up form: its email box is a plain text box, so phones start each email with a capital, and picking a suggestion can leave a space. This time the issue has an owner, a cause, a date and a check. Tom's team makes the box an email box; the form trims, lower-cases and checks each email before it's saved; they correct the old emails in their platform, and the clean-up in staging can go. The rule stays as a test that should never fail, and the cell turns green. One night, half the new badges from the learning platform arrive with zero credit, most of them microcredentials that should carry five: every test passes, but the wallet shows them as plain badges. Monitoring knows the usual share of badges with no credit, one in twelve, and last night's, one in two, and tells the owner the metadata names. The cause is an update to the platform; Tom's team fixes it there and sends the badges again, and the surprise becomes a rule. Nobody blamed Tom's team. Semmelweis was right, but his washing was slow and hurt the skin, germs weren't known, he took years to publish, and he blamed the doctors who doubted him; washing became the rule only after his death. A fix at the start lasts when it's easy to keep, and when people get the evidence, not the blame. Yusuf marks policy 15's line green: it runs in the work now.

## Labs (candidates)

1. **Where did it start?** Five problems as they show downstream (duplicates in a wallet, a report short of rows, a date in the wrong year, a code nobody knows, a blank email). Trace each to where it starts (a form, an import, an update, a code list, a person's habit) and say what fix goes there.
2. **Rule or monitor?** Eight things that can go wrong with a table. Which can you write as a rule, and which would only monitoring catch: freshness, volume, empty values, a shift in the values?
3. **Close it for good.** Four issues closed with "cleaned downstream". Rewrite each with an owner, a cause, a date and the check that proves it's closed.

## From practice

What practitioners do, and what the film keeps:

- **Don't close the issue with the workaround.** A clean-up downstream is a workaround: keep the issue open, as a known problem, with its cause and its owner, until the cause is fixed. The film's issue cards, closed every week, are the habit to break.
- **Follow one record, end to end.** Tracing one example back through lineage finds the cause faster than reasoning about a whole table. The film follows one email.
- **Fix it with the system's own features first.** Input types, validation, pick lists, required fields: training people to type more carefully rarely lasts. The form does the work.
- **Correct the history too.** Unless the records made before the fix are corrected at the source, the clean-up never goes, "just in case". REQ-SC-01 is that step.
- **Keep the old rule as a test.** After prevention, the rule stays at error, so a vendor's update can't undo the fix without anyone noticing.
- **Watch the tables that matter, and route to the owner.** Freshness, volume, empty values and the spread of values, with alerts to the owner the metadata names, not to a shared inbox.
- **Bring evidence and help, not blame.** The team at the source usually doesn't know what the problem costs downstream. Counts and examples, and an offer to help test the change, get the fix made.

## How the film follows its references

The series' reference is DAMA-DMBOK, named once, in *Off the shelf*; this film teaches what its chapter on data quality shares with the other bodies of knowledge, and names none of them.

| Insight | Where it comes from | What the film does |
|---|---|---|
| Find the root cause, not the symptom; an issue has an owner, a cause, a date and a check that it's closed | DAMA-DMBOK2 (data quality: issue management, root cause analysis); ITIL 4 problem management (problem, known error, workaround) | Adopts: the weekly issue closed by a workaround, then one closed for good |
| Prevent at the source rather than clean downstream | DAMA-DMBOK2 (preventive and corrective actions); Deming's third point ("cease dependence on inspection to achieve quality"); Thomas Redman's "hidden data factories", the extra work each team does to fix what others left (*Harvard Business Review*, 22 September 2016) | Adopts, and extends: after prevention, the rule stays as a test that should never fail, so the fix can't silently undo itself |
| Make the mistake impossible where it's made | Shigeo Shingo's source inspection and poka-yoke (mistake-proofing) | Adopts: the email box, which trims, lower-cases and checks the email itself |
| Quality promised between the team that makes data and those who use it | DAMA-DMBOK2 (data quality service levels); data contracts (*Silent change*, *Promises and proofs*) | Says it in a line: the test is "a promise to everyone who reads them"; *A product with an owner* writes service levels down |
| Monitor for what no rule names | Data observability practice: freshness, volume, schema, distribution, lineage (Moses, Gavish and Vorwerck, *Data Quality Fundamentals*, 2022) | Adopts four of the five in plain words; lineage is *Follow it back* |
| A confirmed surprise becomes a rule | Common practice in quality monitoring and in postmortems (an action item that prevents a repeat) | Adopts: DEC-LMS-03 |
| Blameless: people get the evidence, not the blame | Google's SRE book, *Postmortem culture*; *When things go wrong* | Adopts, and grounds it in the opening: Semmelweis was right, and blame didn't make his fix last |

## Rigour to check before recording

Checked on 20 and 21 October 2026; the sources are listed below.

- **The two clinics.** Doctors and medical students worked in the first clinic and midwives in the second, and patients were allocated on alternate days (Loudon, JLL). Mortality, 1841 to 1846: about 10% in the first clinic and under 4% in the second (Wikipedia, from Semmelweis's own tables: 1,989 deaths in 20,042 births, 9.9%; 691 in 17,791, 3.9%); 98.4 and 36.2 per 1,000 for 1840 to 1846 (Loudon); "close to 10%" and 3.3% (Semmelweis University). "About one in ten" and "about one in thirty" sit inside these. Sources give the ratio as three, four or five times, by year, so the film gives the two rates, not a ratio. The split of the clinics dates from 1839 or 1840 (sources differ); the film doesn't say.
- **Semmelweis** was appointed assistant in the first clinic on 1 July 1846, at 28; "a young doctor" is fair. The film doesn't tell the death of his colleague Jakob Kolletschka in 1847, which gave him the clue (a cut at an autopsy, then the same illness): it keeps to the difference between the two clinics.
- **"The doctors and students come to the ward from the autopsy room."** Semmelweis University: "physicians carrying out autopsies went over to the maternity ward without disinfecting their hands, where they proceeded to examine expectant mothers"; Wikipedia: washing "between autopsy work and the examination of patients". The midwives did no autopsies.
- **The washing.** Chlorinated lime (calcium hypochlorite), from mid-May 1847 (Wikipedia; Semmelweis University: "settled on chlorinated lime in May 1847"); Loudon: "before entering the maternity ward". The basin's stand by the ward's door is the film's picture: no source says where the basin stood. Later he had hands washed between patients too, and instruments; the film doesn't say.
- **The fall.** April 1847, 18.3%; June 2.2%, July 1.2%, August 1.9% (Wikipedia, from his tables). For 1848: 12.7 per 1,000 in the first clinic and 13.3 in the second (Loudon); "from 11.4% in 1846 to 1.3% in 1848" (Carson and Toodayan, MJA). "Within months ... to the level of the second" holds. The register on the desk is the film's picture of his tables.
- **Why it didn't spread at once.** The washing was "a very tedious, time-consuming procedure that also caused skin irritation", and he "could only prove its effectiveness statistically" (Kölnei, Képmás); his results "lacked scientific explanation at the time" (Wikipedia). He published first in 1858, in Orvosi Hetilap, "after repeated urging from his friends" (Semmelweis University), and his book in 1860, dated 1861; Loudon: "he waited for thirteen years". After unfavourable reviews of the book, he wrote open letters in 1861 and 1862, in Pest, "at times denouncing [his critics] as irresponsible murderers" (Wikipedia); the last, in 1862, was to all professors of obstetrics. The film says "he blamed them, in angry open letters", and no more. "Washing became the rule only after his death, once germs were known": Wikipedia's summary, "widespread acceptance only years after his death, when Louis Pasteur confirmed the germ theory, and Joseph Lister ... practised and operated using hygienic methods". He died on 13 August 1865.
- **Not the myth.** The film doesn't tell him as a lone, rejected martyr: Loudon calls that telling a legend ("presented as an unjustly neglected hero and a martyr"), and Kölnei calls total rejection "semi-true": many doctors tried the washing; colleagues defended him; what was rejected was his claim of one cause. The film says he was right, and gives the four reasons it spread slowly, two of them his own.
- **Clothes, 1847.** Dark frock coats, waistcoats and cravats for the doctors; no white coats, which came in the 1880s and after; the midwife in a dark dress, white apron and cap; the mothers in white nightgowns. None of the three English sources describes the clothes: to check against a costume history before recording.
- **Phones and the email box.** Where a page doesn't say how to capitalise, "Chrome and Safari default to on/sentences", and the setting "affects the behavior of other input mechanisms such as virtual keyboards on mobile devices"; it "has no effect on the url, email, or password `<input>` types, where autocapitalization is never enabled" (MDN, *autocapitalize*). So a plain text box starts an email with a capital, and an email box doesn't. A suggestion picked on an iPhone's keyboard is followed by a space, which breaks the checks of emails, postcodes and phone numbers (Apple Developer Forums, *Predictive Text Bug?*; Stack Overflow, *iOS textfield autocomplete adds blank character*): these are developers' reports, not Apple's statement. A leading space usually comes with a pasted address. An email box itself drops spaces at either end ("strip leading and trailing ASCII whitespace from the value") and won't send a value that isn't shaped like an email (it's "suffering from a type mismatch"), in the HTML standard; the form's own check is DEC-SC-04's. Lower case: strictly, mail software must treat the part before the @ as case-sensitive, though using that "impedes interoperability and is discouraged" (RFC 5321, section 2.4); in practice the big providers ignore case (for example, Mailchimp's guide). Keeping emails in lower case is the university's decision (DEC-SC-04).
- **The amber cell.** In the film's past, the rule that emails arrive trimmed and in lower case ran at warn, on every row, so the build never stopped and staging cleaned the emails: the scorecard's learning · validity cell, "7/7 (2 warn)", with the enrolments' rule 6 rows (it checks every version of a row) and the learners' 3: *Rules that run*'s last scorecard, written by the project's generator (`scripts/generate/scorecard.py`, on pull request #53's commit 6af84af) with that film's story changes, so the two films show the same morning; drawn as that film draws it. "Every Monday" is the scorecard's weekly issue to each owner. The example project holds the rule as the decision left it: DEC-SC-04 (`sources/short_courses/_short_courses__decisions.yml`, lines 48 to 64), "Emails are checked where they're typed, from 2 November 2026", decided by "Learning team, with data governance" on 20 October 2026; the test `entered_clean` on the source (`sources/short_courses/_short_courses__sources.yml`, lines 38 to 40 and 60 to 62, and `tests/generic/entered_clean.sql`), at error, on every email recorded since 2 November 2026. These are in pull request #53, not yet merged.

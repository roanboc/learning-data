## The promise

Every line passes two tests: someone new to working in a team can follow it, and an experienced data manager finds nothing wrong in it. The film answers the last of the series' four questions, how do they work together? Its idea: how a team works together counts for more than who is in it. It shows four habits (a turn each, promises kept, clear who does what, and disagreement about the work, never the person) and one page that holds them, written by the team in its own words and read again. It labels nobody, ranks no trait and names no test; each person's way is shown in their own words. It says how sure each piece of research is, and what comes from practice and not from a trial. As the series' last film, it also says what the bridge's story didn't achieve, and where it stops being a guide.

**Takeaway:** "Agree how you work together, and keep to it."

## The story in one paragraph

Quehue, in the high Andes of Peru: every June, four communities make a new bridge of grass rope across a gorge of the Apurímac river, twenty-eight metres long, built by about a thousand people in three days. No family could raise it alone, and it has no drawing and no written plan; yet it has to hold for a year, with people crossing about fifteen metres above the river, and its strength depends on every cord in it. What they have is a way of working that everyone knows: each family's promised cord is checked in on both banks; each community knows its part; men twist the cords into ropes and braid three ropes into each cable of the floor, one man braiding until he tires and another taking his place; many hands pull the cables across and tie them to old stone bases, the older guiding the younger; and on the last day two builders weave the sides and the floor from the two banks until they meet in the middle. By that afternoon four communities have a bridge for another year. Nobody was handed a plan: what they share is how they work. A data team's work is like that, and Google found the same when it studied 180 of its own teams. At the university, on Wednesday 24 February 2027, in Helena Novak's fourth week, Jun checks his new model against an old report that Tomás Ortega built, for census day last year: the report says 18,412 and the model 19,549. At the team's table it turns to the person, two people do all the talking, and Erin Walsh and Marta Silva haven't said a word. Helena stops it, says why, and asks each of the two for three things: what you saw, what you feel and need, and what you ask. Then everyone has a turn: Erin says the old report reads only the student system, and Marta says what each number counts, 18,412 students and 19,549 learners, the students and 1,137 people on short courses. Both numbers are right. Which one the university publishes belongs to the registrar's office, asked in a ticket, and what each word counts goes into the glossary before a line of the model changes. On Thursday the eight of them write one page, how we work together, five lines in their own words, and on Friday it's on the wall. Then the film says where the bridge stops: the way of working hasn't always kept it (let go for a time around 1970, fallen in March 2021 after the pandemic stopped the work, cut down in a night of May 2025, and each time woven again); the weaving is taught only inside a builder's own family; fewer of the young speak Quechua; most people cross on the steel bridge; and custom settles who does which part, which is the communities' own way and no model for a data team. A data team has to ask each person, and can't leave what it knows with one person or one line of people. On Monday 1 March, Ivy Reyes opens the door, and the page is read with her.

## The reference, and practice

The series follows no single framework: its frame is four questions a leader asks before the work, with ISO 27500, the standard on the human-centred organisation, as its backbone (the series' plan; named in *People before roles*). This film is the fourth question. The brief names the research for it, each named once; the film teaches what they share: how a team works together is something its people can say out loud, agree, and keep.

| Body of knowledge | What it adds | Where the film uses it |
|---|---|---|
| Google, re:Work, *Understand team effectiveness* (the study of 180 of its teams that its researchers called Project Aristotle) | "What really mattered was less about who is on the team, and more about how the team worked together." In order of importance: psychological safety, dependability, structure and clarity, meaning, impact | The film's title and its second chapter: "Google once studied 180 of its own teams, to learn why some worked better than others." | Amy Edmondson, psychological safety | People speak up when they believe it's safe to | Named once, as first on the study's list, and left to *The team comes first*, which teaches it |
| Frank de Wit, Lindred Greer and Karen Jehn, "The paradox of intragroup conflict: a meta-analysis" (*Journal of Applied Psychology*, 2012) | Across 116 studies and 8,880 groups, conflict about relationships goes steadily with worse results for a group; conflict about the task doesn't, and goes with better ones where the two kinds stay apart | "Across 116 studies, conflict about the person harms a team's work. | Bart De Jong, Kurt Dirks and Nicole Gillespie, "Trust and team performance" (*Journal of Applied Psychology*, 2016) | Across 112 studies and 7,763 teams, teams whose members trust each other perform better | "Across 112 studies, teams whose members trust each other do better work." |

"That's one small study." |
| Practice (the brief's advice to show, and one idea from the author's papers: feelings belong in a hard conversation) | Write one page as a team: how we meet, how we ask for help, how we disagree, how we decide, how we say "I can't make that date". Read it each quarter, and when someone joins. Disagree about the work, in the open; when it turns to the person, stop and say so. Keep small promises, and say early when you can't | The page, the three things, and *On Monday*. "The page comes from practice, not from a trial." |

**Where the film adopts, extends or differs.** It adopts Google's finding in its guide's own words and in its order, shows all five on screen, and says the second and third in plain words (keeping your word; being clear about who does what). It adopts de Wit and others' two kinds of conflict as "about the person" and "about the work", and leaves out their third kind, conflict about how the work is shared out, which the study also finds harmful: the page's lines on who decides are the film's answer to it. It says Rosenberg's four parts as three, as the brief has them, without the method's own vocabulary. From Woolley and others it takes one of the three things that went with a group doing well, equal turns, and leaves the other two (how well members read each other, and the share of women in a group), because the film ranks no trait and sorts nobody; it says "they called" it collective intelligence, since whether there is one such factor is debated (Credé and Howardson, 2017, find weak support; Riedl and others, 2021, across 22 studies and 1,356 groups, support it, and find that how a group works together predicts it better than its members' skill). It says trust and good work go together, not that one causes the other. It extends the research from practice: the page is in the team's own words, short enough to keep, and read again. And it differs from the bridge on purpose, in *Where the bridge stops*: at the gorge custom settles each person's part and the weaving stays in one family; a data team asks each person, and writes down what it knows.

**Practice.** A practitioner will recognise two numbers "for the same thing" that turn out to count different things; the person who built the old report hearing it called wrong in front of the team; the two people who hadn't spoken, and who held the answer; a manager who stops an argument and says why; a working agreement short enough to keep; the owner of a definition deciding which number is published, asked in a ticket; a definition written into the glossary before the code changes; and a page read again when someone joins.

## Sound

**The palette, its own** (reserved by this film's thread on 12 November 2026 in the module's `palettes.md`, before a note was written, after every score on main and on the open branches was searched): **a viola and a cello, as a pair,** a player to each, the cello from the left and low, the viola from the right and high. Both are on the site, never as two alone, and no film of this module plays either. They are two players of one family, neither the leader, who make one tune only by taking turns, as the two builders weave from opposite banks to the middle. Made in `source/tools/score.py`: each note is one short pull of the bow, with no vibrato and never plucked, and nothing sounds above about 2.4 kHz.

- **The mark, the weave:** a note each in turn, the cello climbing (D♭, A♭, D♭, F, G♭) and the viola coming down (C, A♭, F, D♭, B♭), then both on the same A♭, in the middle. It is put together through the opening as the bridge is, heard whole as the builders weave and meet, a pair of its notes for each line as the team writes its page, and whole again at the close. There is never a chord, and nothing is held under another note: two notes sound together only at the meeting note, and in the disagreement.
- **Keys, all tuned pure** (just intonation from D♭): D♭ major; B♭ minor for the problem; G♭ and A♭ as everyone learns their part. The scale's fourth and seventh notes are in the mark, so it can't read as a five-note tune.
- **The story in the sound.** In the disagreement both bow at once, a step apart, and neither waits; when each has said what he saw, they take turns again. Where the bridge stops, the cello plays twice without passing the turn (what is taught in one family), the viola plays alone and fades (the language), and the two lines draw near and don't meet (not a model for a data team); then a tap as each person at the table is asked, and the turns come back.
- **Nothing Andean:** no pipe, flute or breath (panpipes there are often played in pairs that take turns, note by note, so the turns here are on bowed strings), no harp, guitar, small guitar, violin, drum or brass, and no five-note tune. No river, wind or birdsong as music.
- **After the opening,** music only at a chapter's turn, under a moment on screen, or in a pause without words: a note each for the study's two halves; one each for the two numbers, and the two bowing at once as it turns to the person; a note for each thing Jun and Tomás say; a turn of four notes as the rings light, and another for "both numbers are right"; a pair for each line of the page; the weave's first notes, quick, for "woven again"; and the whole weave under the last line, into the end card. ## Rigour to check before recording

Checked on 12 November 2026; the sources are listed below. The bridge's sources are the three bridge films' own notes, which this film's thread and the thread of *Wellbeing is in the work* wrote that day from the pages themselves; where a news page differs from the study that Peru's Ministry of Culture published in 2015, the study stands.

- **The place, the people and the size.** The Ministry's study, page 175: the bridge is on the Apurímac river, 28 metres long and 1.20 wide, tied to large stone bases at each side of the gorge, and "la segunda semana de junio de cada año, cerca de mil personas" of the four communities (Huinchiri, Chaupibanda, Choccayhua and Ccollana Quehue) meet to replace it. The film says "the high Andes of Peru" (the river bed is at about 3,700 metres), "about a thousand people" and "in three days" (Thursday to Saturday in the study, pages 178 to 185). "Every June" is said of today only: into the mid 1990s the bridge was made on 6 January (page 156).
- **No drawing, no written plan.** "The plans for the bridge are communicated not through written instructions or diagrams, but through apprenticeship" (NEH, *Humanities*, 2017). The communities do keep written statutes and a register of their members (the study, page 67), so the film says "there's no drawing of it, and no written plan", and never that nothing is written down.
- **It has to hold.** "About fifteen metres above the river": the study, page 183, "a una altura de aproximadamente quince metros sobre el lecho del río" (other pages say 18 or 28 metres, and the study stands). People still cross the bridge on foot (National Geographic, 2018), though most now cross on the steel bridge, which the film says in *Where the bridge stops*. "Its strength depends on every cord in it": the study, page 177, "la fortaleza del puente depende de la elaboración adecuada de estas soguillas".
- **The cord, and the check.** The study, page 177: "Cada familia está comprometida a proporcionar una q'eswa de 40 brazadas de largo (alrededor de 70 metros)". Page 180: on both banks the communal authorities "verifican la entrega de una q'eswa por cada comunero en representación de su familia". The screen says "Forty arm spans: about 70 metres".
- **Each community's part.** The study, page 180: Huinchiri makes two cables of the floor; Chaupibanda and Ccollana Quehue a floor cable and a handrail each; Choccayhua the mat for the floor and the wooden rods across it. So six cables, four for the floor and two handrails, as UNESCO says. The voice says "which of the six cables to make, or the mat for the floor", and the screen names each community's part. - **Twisting and braiding.** The study, page 180: the cords are laid out "en grupos de treinta o cuarenta" and twisted into a rope; three ropes are braided into each cable of the floor; "uno de los comuneros realiza esta labor con las manos y los pies, y, en el momento en que se cansa, es reemplazado por otro". - **Across the gorge.** The study, page 181: a long cord is taken over last year's bridge, a strong rope is pulled across with it, and the cables with that; "es necesaria la fuerza de muchos comuneros para atar adecuadamente las grandes sogas en los estribos de cantería", and "algunos comuneros mayores suelen guiar el trabajo de los jóvenes". - **Conflict.** De Wit, Greer and Jehn (2012), abstract: "a meta-analysis of 116 empirical studies of intragroup conflict (n = 8,880 groups)"; "Stable negative relationships were found between relationship and process conflict and group outcomes"; no strong negative association between task conflict and group performance; and "Task conflict and group performance were more positively related among studies where the association between task and relationship conflict was relatively weak". Hence "need not, if it stays about the work".
- **The three things.** Non-violent communication has four components, "observation (distinguishing concrete observation from evaluation), feelings, fundamental needs, and requests", developed by the clinical psychologist Marshall Rosenberg in the 1960s and 1970s (English Wikipedia, *Nonviolent Communication*, and the Center for Nonviolent Communication's own page; his book wasn't read).

# Platform and solution architecture · One solution, end to end: script

*The script of *One solution, end to end*, film 6 of 8 of *Platform and solution architecture*, the fourth series of the topic *Enterprise architecture*, in twelve chapters, in English, drawn in the look of *The map before the data*. Written on 12 November 2026 from the author's brief of 11 November 2026 and the series' plan (`../proposal.md`), which the author confirmed that day. Made while the author was away; this film's questions and assumptions are in `docs/assumptions/05-enterprise-architecture/4-platform-and-solution-architecture/6-one-solution-end-to-end.md`. It carries no draft label (the four series' shared *Decided* table). The narration is in `source/src/narration.js`; this file gives its reasons.*

## The promise

Every line passes two tests: someone new to data work can follow it, and an enterprise architect, a solution architect or a data engineer finds nothing wrong in it. Its idea: a solution is designed end to end, on the platform everyone shares, before anyone builds a piece of it. Three architects work side by side: the enterprise architect keeps the map and is asked first about what would change it; the platform lead builds into the platform what every team could use, once; the solution architect designs one solution, from where its data starts to the outcome a person sees. The design starts with the context, everything the solution touches, on one page, with what's out of scope for now and what's fixed. It names the few qualities that matter, each with a measure a design can be tested against, and tests at least two options against the same measures, saying how the chosen one's risk is handled. It writes the decision down, with what was considered, why and its consequence, in a record kept with the code. It counts what already exists, and finds most of it there. And it tells every team its part months ahead, so nobody is surprised.

**Takeaway:** "Plan each solution end to end, on the platform everyone shares, and write down why."

## The story in one paragraph

Japan, 1635: the shogunate makes the lords live in Edo every other year, so every year each lord makes the journey, to Edo or home, a small lord with about a hundred people and the greatest with two thousand or more, with all the lord will need. On the Tōkaidō each station keeps a hundred porters and a hundred horses for everyone on the road, and an inn for lords. A procession of three hundred arriving unannounced would find too few: its loads would wait, the inn might be taken, and each day on the road costs the domain dearly. So the stations need to know what's coming, and when, and the domain needs the journey planned before it starts. Before the lord sets out, his officials plan the journey end to end: they list who goes, within the limits the shogunate sets for his rank, and send a notice ahead to every station, with how many porters and horses, on which day, and where the lord will rest and sleep. A day or two before, a board with his name goes up at the inn, and nobody else may stay there. So each station knows days ahead what's coming, and if its own aren't enough, calls on nearby villages in time; the loads go on, station to station, on the porters and horses that serve every traveller. At the utility, in November, the government sets a social tariff: from the first of July, a discount for every eligible household, with no forms, and only the social services agency knows who is eligible. By December billing's vendor offers to do it all inside billing, in its October release; the data team starts its own table of eligible households; IT orders a server to receive the agency's files. There are two lists of who's eligible, and nobody has drawn how the pieces join; nobody was careless, each team did what it could see from where it stood. The head of retail asks for one design, in a ticket, and it goes to Sipho Dlamini, a solution architect, who works beside Tomás, who keeps the map, and Ravi, who builds what every solution shares. Sipho draws the context on one page, picks three qualities from a standard's nine, each with a measure, and tests two options against them: all of it inside billing, which logs only who changes a record, waits for the vendor's releases and is ready in October; or the agency's answers on the data platform, as a data product of retail's, with billing asking through the integration platform for only what a bill needs, every read checked and logged, a change a pull request, ready in May, its risk handled by billing asking the day before each run. He writes the decision down; Ama checks it against two principles, and it's accepted. Of ten parts, six already exist; sending a file out, which every team will need, Ravi builds once, into the platform. Each team hears its part months ahead, and the vendor puts the discount step in its April release. It's built by May and tested in June; on the first of July the first bills carry the discount, for about nine in ten eligible households; when September's answers come two days late, billing uses August's; the log shows one reader; and the head of retail checks the first bills and closes the ticket.

## The reference, and practice

The series follows TOGAF 10 and ArchiMate 4, as *The map before the data* does (`../proposal.md`, *Reference and alternatives*), and the plan names TOGAF's solution level, the C4 model and ISO/IEC 25010 as this film's references. This film names only the standard, once, on screen (*ISO/IEC 25010*, as the voice says "A standard lists nine"), and takes the rest without naming them:

| The C4 model (Simon Brown): the system context diagram, the system in the middle and the people and systems it works with around it; arc42's context and scope, and its constraints | Everything the solution touches, on one page; what's out of scope for now; what's fixed: the date and the three rules already agreed | *Everything it touches* |
| ISO/IEC 25010:2023, *Product quality model*: nine product quality characteristics, functional suitability, performance efficiency, compatibility, interaction capability, reliability, security, maintainability, flexibility and safety | "A standard lists nine": the three that matter here, security, reliability and maintainability (said "easy to change"), each with a measure a design can be tested against; speed hardly matters | *Three qualities, measured*, *Two options* |
| Decision records (Michael Nygard, "Documenting Architecture Decisions", 2011, with its title, context, decision, status and consequences; MADR, which adds the options considered): a short record for each decision, kept with the code | The record: decided, considered, why, and the consequence; checked against the principles; agreed, its status accepted; a year on, whoever asks why finds the answer there | *Why, written down* |
| ArchiMate 4: application components, a data object and a function; technology services that serve them; a work package and an event; requirements in the motivation layer, realised by the elements that meet them | The solution on Tomás's map: its new parts beside billing; the platform's services under them, reused, and one new; a work package, ready by the first of July; each quality a requirement | *The data practice on the map* |

Where the film differs: the frameworks say what a solution design holds, not who designs it beside whom; the plan's position, from the author's brief, is that a solution architect designs one solution end to end, with the enterprise architect asked first about what would change the map and the platform lead building once what every team could use. The film tests the options against measures, not against opinions, and says how the chosen option's risk is handled. It names no product.

Practice, from the author's brief and the series' plan: a solution is designed on the platform, with its context, its options, its qualities and a recorded decision, and its place on the map; the data story is the plan's, the social tariff from *When strategy moves*, designed on the platform. The head of retail asks in a ticket and closes it after checking the first bills; nobody in the business reviews a pull request (AGENTS.md, *Business users ask in tickets*). Ama Owusu checks the record against the principles, which is the regulatory lead's part; she reviews no code. The answers stay a data product of retail's, the business that holds the customers, as the principle says.

## Rigour to check before recording

Checked on 12 November 2026:

- Alternate attendance (*sankin-kōtai*) was made a system by the revised *Buke shohatto* of 1635 (Kan'ei 12), whose second article set the lords' coming to Edo in turn, each year in the fourth month: in principle a year in Edo and a year in the domain, the lords' wives and children living in Edo as hostages. The journeys and the double life cost the domains greatly, and were a main cause of their money troubles. There were exceptions: the Sō of Tsushima came every third year, the Kantō *fudai* lords changed every half year, and Mito lived in Edo. Sources: Kotobank, [参勤交代](https://kotobank.jp/word/%E5%8F%82%E5%8B%A4%E4%BA%A4%E4%BB%A3) (*Nihon daihyakka zensho*; *Seisenban Nihon kokugo daijiten*; *Kaitei shinpan Sekai daihyakka jiten*; Yamakawa's *Nihonshi shōjiten*). The film says "every other year" and doesn't name the exceptions.
- A procession carried everything the lord needed for daily life, on a route the shogunate set, and the lord slept at the station's *honjin* (*Britannica kokusai daihyakka jiten*, on Kotobank, [大名行列](https://kotobank.jp/word/%E5%A4%A7%E5%90%8D%E8%A1%8C%E5%88%97)). Most lords travelled with about 100 to 300 people (Yamakawa), or 150 to 300 (*Sekai daihyakka jiten*); Kaga, the greatest domain, at most more than 2,500. The film says "A small lord brings about a hundred people. The greatest bring two thousand or more". The shogunate limited the numbers by rank, from the *Buke shohatto* of 1615 on, and set them again in 1721 (for a lord of 10,000 *koku*, three or four riders, 20 foot soldiers and 30 servants; *Hyakka jiten Mypedia*); the film says "within the limits the shogunate sets for his rank", without the numbers.
- From about the Kan'ei era (1624 to 1644), each Tōkaidō station kept, as a rule, 100 men and 100 horses (the Nakasendō 50 and 50, the other roads 25 and 25), with villages near it named to send more when those weren't enough (*Sekai daihyakka jiten*, on Kotobank, [宿駅](https://kotobank.jp/word/%E5%AE%BF%E9%A7%85)). The stations' men and horses served official travel first, at set rates, and other travellers for hire; the film says "for everyone on the road", meaning shared by every traveller rather than kept by any one lord. The *honjin*, the official inn for lords, court nobles and shogunal officials, became a fixed institution with alternate attendance from 1635 (*Seisenban Nihon kokugo daijiten* and *Mypedia*, on Kotobank, [本陣](https://kotobank.jp/word/%E6%9C%AC%E9%99%A3)); the film calls it "an inn for lords".
- *Sukegō*: when a station's own men and horses couldn't carry a large passage, villages near it supplied more; first as needed, then fixed, with helper villages set on the Tōkaidō from 1637 and registers from 1694 (Kotobank, [助郷](https://kotobank.jp/word/%E5%8A%A9%E9%83%B7): Yamakawa; *Nihon daihyakka zensho*). The film says "if its own aren't enough, calls on nearby villages in time". The villages' burden grew heavy over the period, which this film doesn't say.
- *Sakibure*: before a journey, a traveller sent each station on the road a letter with the porters and horses needed, the day of arrival and where they would rest and stay, which was sometimes sent separately (*Sekai daihyakka jiten*, on Kotobank, [先触](https://kotobank.jp/word/%E5%85%88%E8%A7%A6)). Before a lord set out, his household was given the road's rules and the list of who went (*tomo-sadame*), and the stations were sent the *sakibure* (*Sekai daihyakka jiten*, [大名行列](https://kotobank.jp/word/%E5%A4%A7%E5%90%8D%E8%A1%8C%E5%88%97)).

## The promise

Every line passes two tests: someone who has only heard of AI agents can follow it, and a platform engineer, a security lead and a data governance lead each find nothing wrong in it. The series has given the planning agent a place on the university's map, a role and an owner (*A place on the map*), and only the rights its role's steps need (*Rights follow the role*). This film is where the map meets the tools: the agent gets one name in every tool, as a person has, with no secret it can see; a script writes every tool's settings from its place on the map, one way, and a check each night fails on any right a tool holds that the map doesn't give; it works in a place of its own; every log says its name; and each tool can stop it, and only it. Databricks, GitHub and dbt are named where they do the work, each with its own mark. **The map says what it may do; every tool holds it to that.**

## The story in one paragraph

Today, at the university: when Planning needs a change to its tables, a new column or a filter, it writes a ticket. All year, those tickets went to Jun Park's helper, a coding agent in his editor, on his own sign-in, with his name and his rights; since Monday 2 November they go to the planning agent, owned by Clara Jensen in Planning, who answers for what it does. In October, the university's auditors asked which of the year's 214 changes to Planning's tables an agent had drafted. Every one said Jun Park, in the code's history and in the platform's log of queries; Jun spent two days going through them from memory, and for nine he couldn't be sure. So the university couldn't fully answer, and if one of the nine was wrong, nobody knew who to ask or what else to check; the helper could also do whatever Jun could, far more than a ticket needs, and to stop it they'd have had to stop Jun. So before its first ticket, the planning agent got a place on the map, and Sam Okafor, who runs the platform, wrote that place into every tool under the agent's own name: Databricks, GitHub and dbt. In Databricks it's a service principal, like Anil's workload agent; in GitHub an App, whose work reads `planning-agent[bot]`; in dbt its runs sign in as that same service principal, with short-lived passes, so it holds no secret it can see or leak. Its rights aren't typed into each tool: Noor's map says what each of its role's drafting steps reads, writes and calls, and a script writes every tool's settings from it, one way: a group for the role in Unity Catalog, with the agent its only member, reading production with personal and sensitive data masked and building only in its own schemas; in Unity Gateway, the one model and the tool servers its steps name; in GitHub, one repository and two permissions; in dbt, a target of its own. What it reads first is governed, the glossary's definitions and the data rules, read only; its memory, kept by the platform in Lakebase, comes second. Every night a check compares the rights each tool holds with the map: on Tuesday, to save a day, its group was given the right to change Planning's production tables by hand; that night the check failed, and in the morning Sam took it back. It works in a place of its own: branches named `agent/planning`, pull requests it never merges, the development workspace, where production can only be read, and schemas of its own. Now every log says whose work it is: its commits are made through its App, so GitHub marks them verified, every query it runs is under its service principal, and the auditors' question is one search. And it can be stopped in each tool on its own: on Wednesday, Sam deactivated its service principal and suspended its App's installation for ten minutes; its next call failed, Jun, Lucía and Anil's workload agent worked on, and turned back on, it had every right it had before. What never changes: Clara owns it, and it never approves a change or grants itself a right. Next: every change through review.

## The reference, and practice

The film's reference is the map the series has drawn, in ArchiMate 4 as *The map before the data* teaches it, and two principles every security and governance practitioner uses: least privilege and separation of duty. It names no framework on screen, and teaches what they share in plain words: an identity of its own for each actor, rights from its role and nothing more, declared in one place and applied from there.

| Body of knowledge | What it adds | Where the film uses it |
|---|---|---|
| Least privilege (NIST's glossary, from CNSSI 4009) | Each entity is granted "the minimum system resources and authorizations that the entity needs to perform its function" | The opening's "only the rights its work needs"; the group's grants; the App's one repository and two permissions; production read only |
| Separation of duty (NIST's glossary, from NIST SP 800-192) | "No user should be given enough privileges to misuse the system on their own" | "It never approves a change, its own least of all, or grants itself a right"; its approval never counts where the rules ask for a person |
| Workload identity with no long-lived secret (GitHub's OpenID Connect for Actions; Databricks' workload identity federation) | A run proves who it is with a token its platform signs, which lasts minutes, and swaps it for a short-lived token, so no secret is stored for it | *One name in every tool*: the passes, the tokens that end on their own, "no secret it can see or leak" |
| Infrastructure as code, as platform teams practise it (Kief Morris, *Infrastructure as Code*, 2nd edition, 2020) | Settings declared in files under review and applied from them, never by hand; drift, a difference between what's declared and what a system holds, found by comparing the two and fixed at the source | *Written from the map*: the script, "one way", the check each night, Tuesday's grant |
| The map, ArchiMate 4, as *A place on the map* and *Rights follow the role* write it | A role assigned to process steps; each step's access to business objects (reads, writes) and the application services that serve it | "Each step says what it reads, writes and calls": what the script reads |
| His practice | Agents as contributors with their own identity, a role specific down to what they can and can't do, and a person who owns them; every change to production approved by a second person; agents never approve; an agent in the wrong hands is more dangerous | The whole film: its own name, its role's rights, Clara who owns it, the switch |

**Where the film adopts, extends or differs.** It adopts least privilege and separation of duty as written. It extends infrastructure as code from servers to an agent's rights across three tools: the declared state isn't a list of grants but the map's process steps, so a right exists only because a step needs it (*Rights follow the role*'s view), and the script that writes the tools' settings is the project's own, `scripts/generate/agents.py`, tool-neutral, so no product's drift tool is named. It differs from the common practice of a team granting a service account what a ticket asks: here nobody types a right into a tool, and a right given by hand to save a day is drift the check finds that night.

**What practitioners would recognise.** A service account for each workload with a named owner; access through groups, never granted to the account itself; an access review made continuous by a nightly comparison; a quick fix made by hand, caught and reverted, with the source changed first if it's needed; CI that signs in through OpenID Connect with no stored secret; a separate development workspace where production is read only; commits from a bot shown as verified; an audit query by identity; and a kill switch for one identity that stops nobody else.

| 5 November 2026 | The film opens on Svalbard, as the module's openings set it, in four beats said outright. **Scenario:** February 2008, a vault in a frozen mountain opens, kept at minus 18 °C, holding copies of seeds from gene banks around the world; gene banks keep the seeds of crops and their wild relatives, so breeders can grow crops that stand drought and disease; a gene bank near Aleppo keeps 141,052 samples of wheat, barley, lentils and chickpeas from the world's dry lands, and from 2008 sends copies of most of them to the vault. **Problem:** a gene bank can be lost, and a variety lost is lost for good; in 2012 war came close to Aleppo, most of the bank's keepers had to leave, and it could no longer send seeds out, so breeders couldn't get the seeds they relied on and every variety was at risk. **Solution:** in 2015 the keepers did what no one had done before and asked the vault for their copies; 38,073 samples came out of the mountain and were sown that November in Lebanon and Morocco. **Benefits:** by 2020 nothing was left of the collection in Aleppo, yet no more than 1,657 of its samples were lost, and breeders can order its seeds again. Told with care: the keepers and the breeders are the story's people, the war is said in a line and never shown, and the bank isn't named on screen or in the voice (its sources are in the script). The film comes back to the vault twice: the archived pay records "sleep until someone needs them", and at the end the vault stays cold and still while the bank sends out its seeds again. |
| 5 November 2026 | The data story: in September 2026, Noor Rahman, the data architect, asks each data owner two questions, how fresh must it be, and how often is it read, and the answers set each kind of data's temperature, written into its contract (on screen, the pay records' contract: fresh each pay day; kept 7 years after each contract ends, then deleted). The film defines hot, warm and cold for the module, as its shared words have them: hot, needed within seconds or minutes and read all the time, kept small and close to where it's used; warm, fresh to the hour or the day and read often, worked out on a schedule; cold, rarely read but kept, cheaply, until the date it may be deleted. Three kinds of data, one per temperature, each with a before (until September) and an after (now), then one claim that cools: Linh's claim for last week's tutorials, hot on Monday morning while payroll's list of claims still waiting reads it in place, in the app; warm once it's paid, part of what Health has spent, worked out each night; cold a year on, her hours in her pay record in the archive, until the seven years are up. |
| 5 November 2026 | Hot: every contract as it stands, for the claims app, which checks each claim against the tutor's contract; claims approved by noon on Monday are paid on Thursday. Until September the platform took in the HR system's changes once a night, so the app saw a Monday morning's changes on Tuesday, and 23 tutors waited two more weeks for their pay. Now the HR system pushes each change as it's made, through Zerobus Ingest, and a pipeline (Lakeflow Spark Declarative Pipelines, AUTO CDC) keeps a streaming table of every contract as it stands: the app sees each change within a minute. This Monday, 2 November 2026, eleven contracts changed in the morning, and every claim against them was approved by noon. Hot costs the most, because something is always running (the pipeline in continuous mode): keep hot only what's needed within minutes. Ingrid Holm owns the contracts; no pay or rate of pay travels with them. |
| 5 November 2026 | Warm: what each faculty has spent this year against its budget, which Rohan Desai, in Finance, and the faculties' managers check each morning. Until September every look read the finance system's ledger in place: slow, and a load on it while Finance worked. Yesterday's figures are fresh enough, so now it's a materialized view, worked out each night at 02:00, Sydney time, from the ledger read in place through Lakehouse Federation, and ready to read; a dbt model Jun Park wrote, its schedule in its config, tested like any other; it keeps only the answer, never a copy of the ledger. Most of the university's data is warm, and should stay so: few questions need the minute, and hot costs more. The brief's seed for warm was casual hours by faculty for Rohan's budget; the film takes each faculty's spending instead, an assumption for the author, so it doesn't count casual teaching cost, which *Defined in the open* defines and *Reports on demand* and *More readers than people* serve, and doesn't copy the claims, which *One copy, one platform* keeps in one place. The bars carry no figures: the film shows how the answer is kept, not what it says. |
| 5 November 2026 | Cold: payroll's pay records, a record of each pay with the hours each casual tutor worked, sent to the platform every pay day. Australian law asks employers to keep pay records for seven years (the Fair Work Act 2009, s 535, and the Fair Work Regulations 2009, reg 3.33, which has a casual's record set out the hours worked: the voice says "Australian law"), and after the first year they're rarely read. Until September every record sat in the costliest storage, and none was ever deleted: a cost, and a risk to everyone in them. Now, a year after it's written, each data file moves to the cloud's archive tier (a lifecycle rule on the storage account, set by Sam Okafor), and the table is told (`delta.timeUntilArchived`), so queries for recent pay still run and one that needs an archived year says which files to bring back, which takes hours, fine for an audit. When a year's records turn seven they're deleted, on a date set in advance, and their files removed for good; in October 2026 the oldest, 2016 to 2018, were deleted. On screen the rule is worded as *How long, and then what*, in Data protection and privacy, words it, agreed with that film's thread: "7 years after each contract ends, then deleted"; a casual contract ends in the teaching period it's for, so a casual's records go when the year they were paid in turns seven, as the voice says. The archive tier and the one table, `pay_records`, agreed with *Deleted means deleted*, which shows the same records. How to delete well is named in a line, for the films on data protection. |

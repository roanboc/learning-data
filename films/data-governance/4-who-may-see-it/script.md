## The promise

Every line passes two tests: anyone who has asked for access to data and waited weeks can follow it, and a privacy officer or a platform engineer finds nothing wrong in it. In 1983, West Germany's Constitutional Court stopped a census and drew a line that still holds: counting people is one permission, knowing who they are is another. In a university's data, that line runs between columns. Each column other teams read carries a class, *direct*, *pseudonymous* or *none*, kept by its owner in a register, and no change goes through while a class is missing. The class travels with its column as a label, and one rule masks direct personal data for everyone outside the office that holds it, in every table the column reaches. Access is for a purpose, until a date, and a review reads the access list itself. And people are told why the mask is there, and how to get more when a purpose needs it. **Classify once; protect it everywhere.**

## The story in one paragraph

In April 1983, every resident of West Germany is to answer the census. The same law lets each town hall use the answers to check up on its residents, by name. Hundreds of groups call for a boycott, and two weeks before census day the Constitutional Court stops it. The country still needs a way to count without knowing who said what. In December the court rules that, in principle, each person decides what is disclosed about them and how it's used; with computers, no data can be assumed insignificant. It doesn't forbid counting: names and addresses are to be kept apart from the answers and removed as soon as possible, and answers given to be counted don't go back to the town hall to check up on anyone. The census runs that way in 1987: the country gets its numbers, and people keep their privacy. Today, the university has a census too: Planning counts its learners, and its list of learners near an award has no names. Clara, in Planning, needs each learner's status from the learner table, to plan next term's places. Yusuf's binder 6, *Data protection*, says personal data is classified and protected, but not which data, or how. So until now, access meant a form, an approval by email and, three weeks later, the whole table, names and emails included, granted to her by name, with no end: the platform team finds forty-one people who can read it, nine of them in other jobs now. This term the path starts at the column. Each owner writes the classes of their columns in a register, and a change that leaves one empty goes back with a question. One reaches Mei, for the registrar's office, and surprises her: a credential's code looks harmless, but an award's code holds the student number. The class travels with its column as a label, and one rule, which the platform team applies, shows direct personal data to the registrar's office and stars to everyone else. The registrar's office owns the learner table, and Mei, for her office, says yes to Clara: to plan next term's places, until 15 December, through a Planning group. Clara sees learner codes and statuses; student numbers, names and emails are stars, and even the codes come with a purpose and an end date, because with the registrar's list they lead back to a person. When she asks why, Yusuf tells her, as the court did, and shows her the way to more. On 15 December her access ends by itself, and later that month Yusuf's review reads the access list itself: two groups now, each with a reason to be there. Policy 6 now says which data, and how, who decides and who acts. Like the census of 1987, Planning gets its numbers, and learners keep their privacy.

## The look

*Watercolour*, the series' look, as in *Off the shelf*.

- **Pencil and wash.** Every place is painted on paper, with a pencil line and paint gathering in the paper's grain. People are drawn as the master data films draw them, with a soft wash of colour behind each one.
- **The university's own places,** from the series' shared set: Planning, the registrar's office and its desk close-up, the data team and its board close-up, the platform team's desk close-up, the floor of offices behind glass, and Yusuf's office and desk. Nothing is redrawn: the film adds its things on top.
- **1983 is painted the same way,** with the place and year in the corner, in places of work and public life, never homes: a town hall's register office with its card drawers and a counter of tied census forms; a street corner with a newspaper kiosk and an advertising column; the court's hall, glass and wood, with its long bench; a table seen from above with one form on it. The hall is painted plainly, not copied from the court's own, and carries no emblem. People wear 1983: a clerk in a blouse and cardigan, passers-by in parkas, corduroy and raincoats, the judges in their red robes with white jabots and red caps. Every word on screen is in English; the posters carry pictures, not slogans.
- **Colour carries meaning, as in the series.** Gold is governance's decisions and, behind a person, the business side; cyan is the technical side. The classes are the series' own, from this film: **plum** for direct personal data, a **paler plum** for pseudonymous (still personal, so the same family), and plain paper for none. A mask is a plum band with stars on it. Red keeps its series meaning, what's missing or nobody owns yet, as on the sign-off sheet, and is never a class or the mask.
- **Papers, not files.** Every word on screen is on the university's own papers and boards: Mei's classification register, the sign-off sheet the series shares, the request for access, the access card, the rule card, the access list and binder 6. The one diagram-like picture is the data team's board of cards on strings, on which the label and the mask follow the column.

## Rigour to check before recording

Checked on 20 and 21 October 2026, and the example project's lines again on 24 October; the sources are listed below.

- **The 1983 census.** The Census Act of 25 March 1982 (*Volkszählungsgesetz 1983*, BGBl I p. 369) ordered a census of population and occupations, with questions on buildings and housing, and a count of workplaces, "as of 27 April 1983" (§ 1, quoted in the judgment, German text ¶ 4). Every resident was obliged to answer. The questionnaires were to be handed out from 18 April and collected by early May 1983 (German text ¶ 133). Questions included citizenship, occupation and the means of transport used (bpb). "So the country can plan housing, schools and transport" simplifies the purpose; the court speaks of the information needed "for carrying out public planning functions" (¶ 162).
- **The register comparison.** § 9(1) of the 1983 Act, in the court's English text: "Census data collected pursuant to § 2 nos. 1 and 2 may be compared with the civil registers and used for the purposes of correcting the latter." § 9(2) and (3) allowed other transfers to public authorities. "Town hall" stands for the municipalities that keep the civil registers (*Melderegister*). "Use the answers to check up on its residents, by name" is that comparison, seen from the resident's side: each person's answers set against their entry in the register. The court gives the harm in its own words (checked on 27 October 2026): combining the statistical purpose with the register comparison "could significantly jeopardise the proper functioning of official statistics" (¶ 201), and "the state can only carry out its policy planning function properly if statistical data is sufficiently shielded", or "citizens might not be inclined to provide truthful information" (¶ 162). The narration's "The country still needs a way to count, without knowing who said what" is that need.
- **The boycott.** By the end of March 1983, about 500 groups supported the boycott (netzpolitik.org, citing Nicole Bergmann's history of the protests), so the film says "hundreds". The posters on the column are invented and carry no slogans.
- **The stop.** On 13 April 1983, the court suspended the census by an interim order (BVerfGE 64, 67, cited in the judgment's German text ¶ 2), by five votes to three; the Senate was unanimous that at least § 9 had to be suspended (the order, as reprinted by the Humanistische Union). Census day was 27 April: two weeks later.
- **The judgment of 15 December 1983** (BVerfGE 65, 1; 1 BvR 209, 269, 362, 420, 440, 484/83), in the court's own English text:
  - Headnote 1: the general right of personality "encompasses the protection of the individual against the unlimited collection, storage, use and sharing of their personal data. This fundamental right confers upon the individual the authority to, in principle, decide themselves on the disclosure and use of their personal data." The film's "each person decides what is disclosed about them, and how it's used" says the same, and keeps "in principle": restrictions are allowed in an overriding public interest (headnote 2).
  - ¶ 150: "in the context of automatic data processing, it can therefore no longer be assumed that any data is insignificant." The narration's "With computers, no data can be assumed insignificant" keeps the court's hedge.
  - ¶ 146: a legal order would not be compatible with the right "if citizens were no longer able to tell who knows what kind of personal information about them, at what time and on which occasion." The film's closing question (who may see each column, for what, until when) is the same question, asked by the holder of the data; chapter 8 says it again as what each owner decides.
  - The line: identifiers (name, address, identification number, census list number) "must be treated confidentially and kept separate from the rest of the data" (¶ 191); data is to be "rendered (effectively) anonymous as soon as possible" (¶ 161); personal data collected for statistics may not be shared, without being rendered anonymous, "for purposes pertaining to the exercise of administrative functions" (¶ 194): so answers given to be counted "don't go back to the town hall to check up on anyone". § 9(1) to (3) was declared void (operative part 2); the questions themselves were compatible with the Basic Law, with added safeguards (operative part 1). So "the court doesn't forbid counting" is the court's own result.
  - The court's English page abridges some paragraphs; the dates of 27 April and 13 April 1983 come from the German text.
- **1987.** The Census Act 1987 (*Volkszählungsgesetz 1987*, 8 November 1985, BGBl I p. 2078) has a section on separation and deletion (§ 15, *Trennung und Löschung*) and one forbidding re-identification (§ 17, *Verbot der Reidentifizierung*). § 15(1): the auxiliary characteristics (*Hilfsmerkmale*) are to be separated from the survey answers as soon as the statistical offices have checked the forms, and kept apart; street and house number stay with the answers only until each address is placed in its small area, then are deleted (§ 15(4)). § 15(2): the forms, with the auxiliary characteristics, are destroyed at the latest two weeks after the state's official population figure is set. The census day was 25 May 1987 (the Federal Statistical Office's series *Volkszählung vom 25. Mai 1987*); 99 percent took part, and it counted 61.08 million residents (bpb). The section texts were read in a search index of the act, not on a page that loaded: read § 8's list of auxiliary characteristics and § 15 in the *Bundesgesetzblatt* before recording. The film says that the count went ahead "that way", and what it gave. "The country gets its numbers": in May 1987 the count was carried out, after its procedure had been changed; by the Federal Statistical Office's count, 99 percent took part, and it counted 61.08 million residents (bpb, checked again on 27 October 2026). "And people keep their privacy": the court's line, which the 1987 act wrote into § 15 and § 17 (names kept apart from the answers and destroyed early; no re-identification). The film doesn't say the protests ended: opponents called for a boycott in 1987 too (bpb), and the film's 1987 shows only the forms. Its shredder, on "removed as soon as possible", is the court's requirement, which § 15(2) wrote into the 1987 act.

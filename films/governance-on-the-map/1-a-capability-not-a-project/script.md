## The promise

Every line passes two tests: anyone who has seen a good thing fade when its project ended can follow it, and an experienced enterprise architect or data governance lead finds nothing wrong in it. In Holland around 1300, the land behind the dikes had sunk lower than the water around it, and a dike is only as strong as its weakest stretch: where one stretch wasn't kept up, the water broke through and flooded everyone behind it. A dike is never finished, so the people behind the dikes formed water boards: each landowner kept a stretch, the board walked the dikes every year and fined anyone whose stretch wasn't kept, and whoever relied on the dikes paid for them and had a say. Rijnland's board has kept its land dry since 1255. Floods brought big projects; the boards were there before them, and after. It's the same with data: a project can put governance in place, and only something the university always does keeps it there. At the university, the project that paid for Yusuf's team ends in June, and the deputy vice-chancellor can't see what governance does. On Noor's map, Yusuf's first cards (a team, a tool, a meeting) fail the capability test and move to their own bands, and one card stays: *Govern data*. Under it go seven capabilities in the university's words, each put in place this term, and an eighth that two frameworks, used as checklists, find missing. Each is coloured by evidence from the work: green where it runs, amber where people still have to remember, red where nothing happens yet. Then the services people can ask for, named the way they'd ask, each with a promise. The deputy vice-chancellor reads one page, and governance gets a line in the budget every year, the capability's, not the project's. Kept up every year, governance gives everyone who relies on the data one meaning, one number, and someone to ask. **A project builds it. A capability keeps it.**

## The story in one paragraph

Holland, around 1300. Farmers have drained the wet, boggy land behind the coast, and as it dries, it sinks, lower than the water around it. Dikes keep the water out, but a dike is only as strong as its weakest stretch: where one stretch isn't kept up, the water breaks through and floods everyone behind it, their fields, homes, animals and the winter's food. And a dike is never finished: it settles, it wears, and storms break it, so they need a way to keep every stretch up, every year. So the people behind the dikes form water boards. Each landowner keeps a stretch of dike, the more land, the longer the stretch; every year the board walks the dikes and fines anyone whose stretch isn't kept; and whoever relies on the dikes pays for them, and has a say. Behind kept dikes, people can farm and live, safe from the water, and Rijnland's board has kept its land dry since 1255, in a country where a quarter lies below the sea. It's the same with data: a project can put governance in place, and kept up every year, it lets everyone trust the data. In December, at the university, the submission has gone in, and both reports gave the same number. Now David Mensah, the deputy vice-chancellor, is planning next year's budget, and the project that pays for Yusuf's team ends in June. He isn't against governance, but he can't see what it does, so he asks what the university would lose without it. Yusuf's project report lists everything the project did, and nothing after June; without the team, rules nobody keeps up would drift, like a dike nobody walks, and next year the reports could disagree again. Yusuf takes the question to Noor, the data architect, and her map. His first three cards, the governance team, the glossary and the stewards' meeting, fail the test from *The map before the data*: a team can be reorganised, a glossary replaced, a meeting changed, so each moves down the map to where it belongs, and one card stays at the top, *Govern data*. Under it they write what the university must be able to do: define what words mean, decide who owns what, protect who sees what, keep data fit for use, change without surprises, provide data products, and help people take part, each put in place this term. Checked against two frameworks, used as checklists, not as the map, the list is missing one: keep records only as long as they're needed, which nobody does yet. They colour each by evidence: five green, where the work itself shows they run every night; two amber, still checked by hand, who uses what and who may read each table; one red, as learner records are kept for seven years, and nothing checks it, or removes what's older. Then the services people can ask for, named the way they'd ask, from *What does this number mean?* to *Help me do it right*, each with a promise, such as a new term decided within ten working days; like the water boards, whoever relies on a service has a say in it, and each year its users review its promise. David reads one page: seven services, their promises, and what it costs to keep them, mostly people's time. Governance gets a line in the budget every year, the capability's, not the project's, and next year's new work starts with the red card. The project still ends in June, as planned; what it built stays, because something keeps it. Next November, the two reports can agree again: kept up every year, governance gives everyone who relies on the data one meaning, one number, and someone to ask.

## The reference, and practice

The series' reference is DAMA-DMBOK, named once, in the prologue of *Governance principles*. This series adds the architecture of *The map before the data*, drawn in ArchiMate 4 as that series draws it, and this film uses two of its films on governance itself: *What it must be able to do* (capabilities, the test, the heat map) and *Who it serves, and how it pays* (services). Other bodies of knowledge say the same in their own words, and the film draws on them without naming them; two of them are Yusuf's checklists in chapter 4:

| The author's brief (20 October 2026) | Governance designed as a capability in architecture terms, with its services and the elements end to end, before the tools | This series, and this film first |

**Practice.** What practitioners recognise: governance funded as a project fades when the project ends, and its people move on; a capability map full of team names and systems has to be redrawn at every restructure; an executive funds what they can see, and a list of services with promises is easier to see than a maturity score; most of governance's cost is people's time, owners', stewards' and the governance team's; frameworks are useful to find what's missing, and poor as the map itself; and a service whose users review it each year keeps them coming back.

**The author's view.** On 20 October 2026: "we would benefit of another section after video 10 with two or three videos focusing on designing data governance as a capability in enterprise architecture terms and the elements end to end (services, processes, business glossaries as business products, relevant DG roles and actors, information assets, models, etc). This should go before tooling and would link with enterprise architecture." On 27 October 2026, people's engagement is data governance's key challenge and first priority, so one of its capabilities is *Help people take part*, and one of its services *Help me do it right*.

## The look

*Watercolour*, the series' look since 17 October 2026, shared with the series on master and reference data, so the two look like one university.

- **Pencil and wash.** Every place is painted on paper, and people are drawn as the master data films draw them, with a soft wash of their side behind each one.
- **Only the university's places,** most of them the series' shared places: the floor of offices behind glass, Noor's room and her map, the stewards' meeting room, Yusuf's office. The film paints one new place of the university's: the deputy vice-chancellor's office, a corner room in the newer building, a long desk, a window onto the campus in winter light, and a wall planner of the year ahead. And one place of its own: the land behind a dike in Holland, around 1300, and the same kind of land today.
- **Holland, painted the same way,** with the place and year in the corner: flat land under a wide sky, long narrow fields between ditches of still water, farmhouses of timber and reed on low mounds, cattle, a stone church tower far off, and the long grass bank of a dike with a river behind it, higher than the fields. People in wool tunics, hoods and cloaks, with wooden spades; the board's members in longer gowns, with staffs. No windmills: they came later. A warm umber wash behind each person, as for people of the past. Nothing on screen is in Dutch or Latin: the charter's writing is drawn as lines too small to read, and the corner names the place. No style or music borrowed from Dutch culture: the story is told in the series' own voice.
- **Colour carries meaning,** as in the series: gold is governance's decisions, and the business side; cyan the technical side; green what runs in the work; amber what people still have to remember; red what nobody does yet. Noor's map keeps its bands' colours from *The map before the data*.
- **No files, code or tool screens.** This series shows governance on Noor's map and the university's papers: a report, a budget, a wall planner, one page of services. Applications are named for what they do (the glossary), never by product.
- **One diagram-like picture: Noor's map.** Everything else is people doing things: pinning a card, tapping a staff on clay, turning a page, writing a line in a budget, taking a card off a wall.

## Sound

A new palette ([`source/tools/score.py`](source/tools/score.py)): a saxophone quartet, soprano, alto, tenor and baritone, made in the film's own score, played softly and low, so nothing rings above the voice. Each saxophone is one voice, with no vibrato, and every chord is tuned pure, so no two held notes beat. The film is about what lasts whoever does the work, so its one figure is a note handed on: one saxophone holds it, the next comes in on the same note before the first lets go, and the note never stops, whoever plays it. It's heard on "Keeping the land dry never did", and as what the project built stays. A project is a phrase that ends: the storm barrier's chord, built as its piers rise, closes on a cadence as "projects end", and the deputy vice-chancellor's planner gets one low note that stops, on "ends in June". The budget's line is the baritone's one long note, drawn out as the line is written, and running on past June. The water boards' tune, five notes, 1-3-5-4-3, is played by the alto as the people behind the dikes form their boards, and copied by the baritone as each landowner keeps a stretch; it comes back whenever the film goes back to Holland. The voices come in one at a time as things arrive in order: the dike's layers, low to high; the three landowners' stretches, each note as long as its stretch; interest, payment and a say; Yusuf's three cards, and the three moving down the map; the green notes of evidence, rising. Two voices meet on one note when the two reports agree, and part when, next year, they might not. The series' mark, four notes, 1-5-4-8, on the alto, answered by the baritone, plays under the title and the end card. F major for Holland, F minor for the storm of 1953, and D-flat major for the university, tuned from the same F, so the notes the two keys share are the same pitch in both. As the series' rule says, music plays under the opening story, then only at a chapter's turn, under a moment on screen, or in a wordless pause: no bed under the voice and no urgent pulse, not even for the flood, which is wind and water. [`source/tools/quiet.py`](source/tools/quiet.py) checks it: after the opening, no chapter has music for more than half its length (the most is 45%, in *What it keeps*), or for 12 seconds without a break, and nothing beats or pulses. ## Rigour

Checked on 28 October 2026, before recording and again when the film was made; the sources are listed below.

- **The land sank.** In Holland and Utrecht, the great reclamations of the peat bogs ran roughly from 1000 to 1300: ditches drained the peat, and as it dried, it shrank and oxidised, so the ground fell, in time by several metres, and the land came to lie below the water around it (the reclamation of North Holland's peat, from the 9th and 10th centuries, "went together with draining, settling and oxidation of the peat soil… the land came to lie below sea level"). By the 14th century, the sinking had made the reclaimed land wetter. The film's "as it dries, it sinks, lower than the water around it" says this in plain words, and "around 1300" is the end of that period, when the dikes were already essential. No windmills are drawn: mills for draining land came in the 15th century.
- **Each landowner's stretch, and the yearly walk.** Under *verhoefslaging*, the dike was divided among the landowners who benefited from it, each keeping a stretch in proportion to the land they held, a "distribution key for dividing costs and labour fairly among the beneficiaries". A charter of 1399 for Rijnland gives a water board the right to make by-laws, to inspect (the *schouw*), to divide the dike into stretches (*verhoefslagen*) and to levy fines. The film's "the more land, the longer the stretch", "Every year, the board walks the dikes" and "fines anyone whose stretch isn't kept" are these in plain words. The inspection could come more often than once a year; the film says "every year" because that's when it surely came. The stakes with each landowner's sign are the film's picture: numbered posts along a dike are known later (the Alblasserwaard's ring dike, from 1671). The tally stick and its notch are the film's too.
- **Rijnland, 1255.** The Hoogheemraadschap van Rijnland is the oldest water board in the Netherlands. Its archive's oldest charters show heemraden overseeing the dikes in 1255, and a charter of Count William II of Holland, of 1255, promised not to interfere in water management within Rijnland without the heemraden's consent; the film's "has kept its land dry since 1255" rests on it: Rijnland has worked as a water board ever since. Written cooperation on a local dike system is older still (1122). The charter is drawn with its seal, and its writing too small to read. "Has kept its land dry since 1255": Rijnland is a working water board today. "More than 770 years" (1255 to 2026) is in *The promise*, not the narration.
- **"Whoever relies on the dikes pays for them, and has a say."** This is the principle the Dutch call *belang, betaling, zeggenschap*, "interest, payment, say", which the series' plan quotes: those with an interest pay, and have a say. It's the modern name for an old practice, and it's debated today: in 2015 the government's water advisory committee still saw it as a valid ordering principle, while the Council of State found that the link between payment and say no longer holds as it did. The film says it for the boards of the opening, not for today's elections and taxes, and puts its three words on screen in English.
- **"A quarter lies below the sea."** PBL, the Netherlands Environmental Assessment Agency: 26% of the country lies below sea level, and 29% is susceptible to river flooding (its correction of 2010 to the IPCC's 55%, which counted all land at risk of floods). Statistics Netherlands once gave 20%; the film says "a quarter", as PBL's figure. Twenty-one water boards keep it today, after mergers from about 2,600 in 1950; the number is in *The promise*' notes, not on screen.
- **Floods brought big projects.** The flood of 1953 killed 1,836 people in the Netherlands, and led to the Delta Plan; the Delta Works were completed in 1997, with the storm surge barrier at the New Waterway. The film's "1953–1997" tag says this. The water boards were there before and after; the film doesn't say they built the Delta Works, which were the state's (Rijkswaterstaat).

## The promise

Every line passes two tests: someone new to leading people can follow it, and an experienced data manager finds nothing wrong in it. The film's idea: measures are for learning how the work flows, not for judging people. Measure the flow of the work, pair each measure with one that balances it, read them together as a team and ask what slowed the work, never who; and when someone asks for a number to rank people by, say no, and show the team's flow instead. It never ranks anyone, never shows one person's number, and keeps each person's growth in their own goals.

**Takeaway:** "Measure the work together. Fix the system, not the people."

## The story in one paragraph

In 1997, 541 people died on Sweden's roads. Most crashes were put down to a driver's mistake, so the answer was to teach drivers, fine them and blame them. But people always make mistakes: a tired driver drifts over the middle line, and on a fast road that mistake could kill; for hundreds of families a year it meant someone who never came home. In October 1997 Sweden's parliament adopted Vision Zero: no one should be killed or seriously injured on the roads, and those who design and run the roads would answer for that, not only drivers. Every fatal crash was studied in depth; many people died in head-on crashes on fast roads with two lanes, so from 1998 those roads got a barrier of steel cables down the middle, roundabouts replaced crossroads, and speeds came down where people walk. On roads given a middle barrier, deaths fell by about three quarters, and across the country, while traffic grew, road deaths fell by more than half. Drivers still had to take care, but a mistake no longer had to cost a life. At the university, a year from now, Helena Novak is the data manager. Managing and leading are two sides of her one role, and a leader measures, fitted to thinking work. From July, the team's page of measures, from the data management module, shows how the work flows: days from a request to first use (41 in February, 24 by June) beside work sent back for changes, and promises met beside load, the pieces of work open at once against the team's limit of six. Every second Thursday from 15 July the team reads the page together for twenty minutes, and Helena asks one question: what slowed the work? Never, who was slow? At the first reading, June's 24 days come apart: seven were spent waiting for access to the data, asked for only when work started, one dataset at a time. From Monday 19 July access is asked for the day a request is accepted, and by September the wait is one day. One piece of work in eight came back, read one way and meant another; fifteen minutes with the person who asked, before work starts, halves it. By December the days are down to 14, and sent back to 6 in 100. On Thursday 12 August promises met reaches 82%, a number to celebrate on its own, but load stands at nine against six: the team kept its promises by carrying too much, so it finishes before it starts, and two requests get a not now, with a reason and a date. By mid-September load is back to six, and promises met reaches 86% in December. On Monday 13 September the director of digital services, Helena's manager, asks for a count of tickets closed by each person, each month, for fair reasons. Helena says no, and shows the team's page and what its readings changed; each person's growth stays in their own goals. The director takes the page to the quarterly review, and no one is ranked.

**Where the film adopts, extends or differs.** It adopts Goodhart's point in Strathern's words, Deming's estimate (said as an estimate), *Accelerate*'s pairing of speed with stability and SPACE's warning about counts of activity, without their lists of metrics. It extends them from the author's practice and the module's: the measures are read together, often, with one blameless question (Vision Zero's, asked of the work), and each measure is paired with one that balances it in the team's own terms (days to first use beside sent back, promises met beside load), rather than *Accelerate*'s four. It differs from Deming's call to drop numerical goals: the film keeps measures and dates, since managing needs them, but never one person's number. And it differs from a common practice, the dashboard of tickets or hours per person, which the director asks for and Helena declines.

**Practice.** A practitioner will recognise access to data asked for only when work starts, one dataset at a time; work sent back because the request was read one way and meant another; a short conversation before work starts that saves days after it; a team that keeps its promises by carrying too much; "not now, with a reason and a date"; and a manager's manager who asks, for good reasons, for a count of tickets per person.

## Sound

**The palette** (the module's `palettes.md`, film 12's line: the lead reserved by the setup thread on 5 November 2026, the partner this film's thread's choice on 10 November 2026): **tuned saw blades**, round steel discs struck softly with felt, tools of the work, tuned. Each blade is cut until its note and the octave above ring together; beside them rings the disc's own mode, about 2.83 times the note, a little shorter: the steel's colour, placed between a chord-mate's harmonics so nothing beats; two more modes sound only for the instant of the strike. It has no third or fourth harmonic, so it never sounds like the site's felt piano, and a felt damper stops each note after its ring. Its partner is **the largest blades, low (D2 to D3), damped by the palm as they're struck**, so each speaks once and stops within a third of a second: in a film where each measure has a partner, one blade rings while the other stops. Made in `source/tools/score.py`; nothing above 2.3 kHz.

- **The mark: a note heard alone, then in its chord, where it means something else.** A4, alone, as a number is said (541 killed; promises met at 82%; Strathern's "target"; the last line).

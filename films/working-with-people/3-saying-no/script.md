## The promise

Every line passes two tests: someone new to leading people can follow it, and an experienced data manager finds nothing wrong in it. The film's idea: when more is asked than a team can do well, a bare no helps nobody, and neither does a yes nobody can keep. Ask what the date is for, put the trade-off where everyone can see it, offer what the team can do well, and when, let the people who decide choose, and keep your word. It says no to a date or a scope, never to the person.

**Takeaway:** "Never a bare no: say what you can do, and when."

## The story in one paragraph

The Aral Sea, between Kazakhstan and Uzbekistan, was once the fourth-largest lake in the world. From the 1960s its two rivers were turned to grow cotton and rice in the desert, on farms many families depend on, and the sea shrank: by 2004 it had lost almost 70% of its surface, and its level had fallen almost 20 metres. Around 1990 it split in two. Fishing towns like Aralsk were left far from the water, the fish died, tens of thousands of jobs were lost, and salt and dust storms and polluted water made people ill. Bringing the whole sea back would have meant taking the rivers back from the farms. Kazakhstan chose to save the part that could be saved: with a World Bank loan it built the 13 km Kok-Aral dam to keep the Syr Darya's water in the northern sea, finished in August 2005, with works upstream to let more of the river arrive. The water rose much faster than planned; by 2006 the northern sea stood four metres higher, at the level the dam was built for; by 2007 it was half as salty and the catch had grown forty-fold; fish plants opened, fish were sold abroad again, and the shore came tens of kilometres back towards Aralsk. The southern sea, which the dam didn't save, went on shrinking. It was a no to the whole sea, said plainly, a yes to the part that could be saved, and that yes was kept. At the university, a year from now, Clara Jensen, in Planning, asks for a new student forecast model by the end of August, the same weeks in which the registrar's office needs the rooms' data for the timetable change, and both need Jun and Noor. Helena Novak, the data manager, asks first. On Wednesday 14 July she and Noor sit down with Clara, and find what the date is for: the 2028 budget round closes on Friday 17 September and needs next year's numbers, which the current model, refreshed, can give; the new model is for earlier warning before December's admissions round. Helena puts both asks on the one list, with their dates, what they're for and their weeks: the rooms' data takes three of August's four weeks, a new model six and its tests, the refresh one. The order comes from the list's criteria, not from who asks. Her positive no: yes to the timetable change and to work done well; no new model by August; the current model refreshed by Friday 27 August, and the new one by Friday 29 October. It's Clara's to choose; the data council would settle it in the open on Tuesday 27 July if she disagreed. She agrees, and both dates go on the list in writing. Each date is kept, and in November Clara brings Planning's next request early, with what it's for and when she needs it.

## The reference, and practice

The brief names two books by the same author, each named once; the film teaches what they share: find what a request is for, look for options that serve it, decide by what both sides can check, and say no in a way that protects what matters and offers what you can do.

**Where the film adopts, extends or differs.** It adopts Fisher and Ury's position and interest, options for both interests and fair criteria, and Ury's three steps, without the books' other tools (the best alternative to an agreement, or separating the people from the problem as a named step). It extends them from data practice: the trade-off goes on the team's one list, where both people asking can see it; the order comes from the list's criteria, not from the person who asks or from Helena; and the agreed dates are written down, so the no becomes a promise someone can check. It differs from the books' setting, a negotiation between two sides: Helena and Clara work for the same university, the choice is Clara's, and if they disagreed the data council would settle it in the open. Nothing in the film is a contest, and nobody wins it.

**Practice.** A practitioner will recognise the request that names a date and a solution at once; the two asks that need the same two people in the same weeks; the pull to say yes to both and do one of them badly; the date that turns out to be for something smaller (the budget needed the current model, refreshed); and the colleague who brings the next request early, with what it's for, because the last promise was kept.

## Rigour to check before recording

Checked on 10 November 2026; the sources are listed below.

- **The sea before.** "Until the 1960s, the Aral Sea was the world's fourth-largest lake" (World Bank, 2008); "once the fourth largest in the world" (NASA). It lies across the border of Kazakhstan and Uzbekistan; its basin is shared by five countries, which the film doesn't need.
- **The rivers.** From the 1960s, large quantities of the Amu Darya's and the Syr Darya's water were diverted for irrigation (World Bank, 2008; NASA); the desert was irrigated to grow cotton, rice and other crops (Wikipedia, *Aral Sea*). The film names cotton and rice, says the farms are ones many families depend on, and blames nobody: the reason a full yes was impossible is said, not judged.
- **What was lost.** "Between 1960 and 2004, the Aral Sea's surface area shrank almost 70 percent and its level dropped almost 20 meters" (World Bank, 2008). The split: the World Bank says 1990, Wikipedia 1987; the film says "around 1990". On the map, the sea's levels follow the same numbers: about 20 m down by 2004, the north held about 15 m down until the dam, then 4 m higher; the south about 27 m down by 2014.
- **The harm.** "Fresh fish production virtually disappeared, salinity and pollution levels rose dramatically, dust and salt storms occurred often… Drinking water supplies became polluted and human health problems increased sharply. Tens of thousands of jobs were lost in the fishing, agricultural, and service sectors" (World Bank, 2008). Aralsk "left far from the water": the shore 100 km from the former port (World Bank, 2008), 75 km in 2001 (World Bank, 2010).
- **Why not the whole sea.** The rivers' water irrigates the farmland it was turned to (Wikipedia, *Aral Sea*: the Syr Darya's alone about two million hectares), and the Soviet plan to refill the sea by diverting other rivers was dropped by 1986. The film says only what follows: bringing the whole sea back would have meant taking the rivers back from the farms.
- **The dam.** "The 13 km Kok-Aral Dike was constructed to separate the Northern Aral Sea from the South Aral Sea. Completion was in August 2005" (World Bank, 2008); Wikipedia's *Dike Kokaral* says 12 km; the film uses the World Bank's figure. Financed with a World Bank loan, as the Syr Darya Control and Northern Aral Sea Phase I Project (P046045). "Works upstream": the project built hydraulic structures on the Syr Darya and rehabilitated the Chardara Dam, to let more of the river reach the sea (World Bank, 2008).
- **The rise.** The northern sea "filled up rapidly to only a few meters short of viability just months after the Kok-Aral Dike was erected, considerably ahead of the project schedule. In 2006, 2007, and 2008, the North Aral Sea's full supply capacity was attained" (World Bank, 2008); its level rose "from a low 38m to the desired design level of 42m" (World Bank, 2010). So: "much faster than planned", "by 2006", "four metres higher, at the level the dam was built for".
- **Salt and fish.** Salinity halved, to under 10 g per litre, by 2007; "Between 2004 and 2007 there was a forty-fold increase in fish harvest from 50 tons to about 2,000 tons… Four fish processing plants were opened, and in 2008 fish was exported for the first time in many years" (World Bank, 2008). The 2010 report's 52 t in 2004 and 2,650 to 3,000 t in 2009 (with the delta lakes) are left out, so the film keeps to one source for one claim.
- **The shore.** From 100 km to 25 km from Aralsk (World Bank, 2008); from 75 km in 2001 to 35 km in 2010 (World Bank, 2010). The two differ, so the film says "tens of kilometres".
- **The southern sea.** "In 2014, the eastern lobe of the South Aral Sea completely disappeared" (NASA). Its eastern basin has held some water again in wet years since; the film says only 2014.
- **The university's numbers.** The module's `numbers.md`: Clara's request on Monday 12 July 2027 (row 86); the University Council's decision of Thursday 24 June 2027 and the timetable change it asked for; the faculties planning 2028's classes from October; the data council on the last Tuesday of each month.

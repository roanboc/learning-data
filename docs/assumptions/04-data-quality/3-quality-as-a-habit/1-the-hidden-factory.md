| 1 | Which opening story? | The Dead Letter Office in Washington and the ZIP Code, as the module's openings proposed, told for **1899**, the year Patti Lyle Collins counted it herself in *The Ladies' Home Journal* (September 1899): about six billion pieces of mail posted, 6,312,731 sent to the office, about 21,000 each working day, 32,000 with no address at all, 2,973,987 letters and circulars destroyed. | 2 | The ZIP Code wasn't made to end dead letters. What may the film claim for it? | Only what the sources say: the 1943 zones let clerks who didn't know a city sort by number, which the sender wrote under the city's name; the ZIP Code (1 July 1963) put five digits, written by the sender, on every letter, so anyone and soon machines could sort it. Before it, a letter passed through eight to ten pairs of hands; by 1969, 83% of Americans used it; it has saved the post billions (USPS Office of Inspector General, 2013). The film's lesson is where the fix was made (in the address, by the person who writes it), not that dead letters ended | Chapter 1 and the close |

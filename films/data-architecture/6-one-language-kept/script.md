## The promise

Every line passes two tests: anyone who has seen two teams use one word for different things can follow it, and an experienced data or enterprise architect finds nothing wrong in it. A word that means more than one thing at a company is never a name on its own. Each name is built from parts, as ISO/IEC 11179 suggests: the thing it's about, in the glossary's words; what about it; and the kind of value, from a short list; so every name says which concept it is, and older systems keep their own names, each mapped once to the glossary's. Where the industry already has words, as the IEC's Common Information Model has for electricity, the company keeps its own and maps each to the model, so a new system or a partner can be matched through words they already know. A few principles, each a rule, why it holds and what follows, and every decision written down, with why, what else was considered, who decided and when, are kept with the map in the repository, and every change is checked against them before it's made: the data team reviews the code, and what a new term means is its owner's, asked in a ticket and defined in the glossary first. None of it is ever finished: a data architecture matures one change at a time. **Say what each word means once, and check every change against it.**

## The story in one paragraph

By 1972, factories across Chile sent their numbers to Cybersyn's team in Santiago by telex, once a day. But one factory's number means nothing beside another's unless both are measured the same way: count workers absent one way in one factory and another way in the next, and a factory in trouble could look like the rest. Santiago needed every factory's words to mean the same, or it couldn't compare them, or add them up. So in every factory's model, social unease would be measured one way, the share of the workers on its payroll absent that day, and the operations room would show every factory with the same icons, so anyone could read them without training in science. After the strike of October 1972, numbers from across the country went into one report, written every day at CORFO and taken by hand to the presidential palace; it showed national production and the points of crisis from numbers a few days old, not six months. At the utility, a new team building the home energy app opens a change: its first table, called customer. Customer already means three things there, an account holder, a connection point and a contact, and this would be a fourth: whoever signs in to the app, an account holder but also a partner, a tenant or a grown-up child. Called just customer, anyone reading it would take it for one of the other three; the last time one thing had six names, the January storm's texts missed 180 households. The review catches it before it reaches a table, from what the data architecture keeps in the repository with the map. Names are built from parts, so account holder start date says which concept it is, and a word with more than one meaning is never a name on its own. The industry's reference model, the Common Information Model, has its own words; the utility keeps its own and maps each to them, and whoever signs in is none of the three: a new concept, which needs its own name and an owner. A few principles say why: one concept, one name, from the glossary; each fact has one home; data crosses a domain's edge only by a published flow; every table belongs to a data product. And each decision is written down: decision seven, customer is never a name on its own, decided by Grace, the head of retail and Ama on 14 October, with why, and what was turned down. So the data team reviews the change, the app team asks retail in a ticket, and the head of retail defines app user in the glossary first; the table is built from it, the check passes, and so does the review, and on the map app user joins retail's concepts, with its owner and a link to decision seven. By May 1973, Cybersyn reached a quarter of the nationalised industries, more than a hundred, with half their revenue, but it never ran as designed: the operations room could do very little, the simulator never left the experimental stage, and only the telex network was used regularly; Beer later wrote that the model couldn't adequately represent the changes of those years. On 11 September 1973, a military coup ended Allende's government; on the day of the coup, members of the team carried copies of its documents out of CORFO, and later the operations room was dismantled. The ideas held; the practice never had time to mature. At the utility, the data architecture is never finished either: in its first year, the review stopped nine names before they reached a table, and fourteen decisions were written down.

Someone who has never heard of Cybersyn can answer, from the narration alone, why it was a problem (counted two ways, a factory in trouble could look like the rest, and Santiago couldn't compare the factories' numbers or add them up) and what the fix gave people (one definition and the same icons for every factory, then one daily report for the government, from numbers a few days old instead of six months). The series follows Cybersyn in all six films (the author, 11 November 2026): this film tells the design in its own tense, "would", where it was a design (social unease measured one way in every factory's model; the operations room's icons), and as what happened where it happened (the telex, the daily report); and, as the series' last, it says how little of the design was built, and ends Cybersyn in one plain line, with no music.

## The reference, and practice

The series follows DAMA-DMBOK2's Data Architecture, and this film takes its words from two of its neighbours: Metadata Management, where the business glossary, naming standards and the metadata repository live, and Data Modeling and Design, whose naming conventions build a name from the thing, what about it and a class word for the kind of value. The naming is ISO/IEC 11179-5's (Naming principles, 2015): a data element's name joins an object class term, a property term and a representation term, which the film calls the thing, what about it and the kind of value. The reference model is the IEC's Common Information Model for the electricity industry (IEC 61968 and 61970), whose classes for the point where power is delivered (UsagePoint), the premises (ServiceLocation), whoever receives the service (Customer) and its account for billing (CustomerAccount) the utility maps its own words to; practitioners map to an industry model to share words with other systems and partners, rarely to replace their own. The principles take TOGAF's form (the TOGAF Standard, 10th Edition: a statement, its rationale and its implications), said as a rule, why it holds and what follows, and each one comes from an earlier film of the series. The decision records follow architecture decision records (M. Nygard, "Documenting Architecture Decisions", 2011: a decision, its context and its consequences) with the fields practitioners added, the options considered and who decided, as in MADR. The repository is the one the map lives in, as *Architecture that keeps up* keeps it; TOGAF's Architecture Repository likewise keeps standards, reference models and a log of decisions beside the models. Under the site's rules (the author, 10 and 11 November 2026), a definition starts in the glossary, owners decide what their data means, the data team reviews the code, and business users ask in tickets, never in a pull request.

What practitioners would tell a newcomer, and the film shows: build names from the glossary's words, not from a list of abbreviations, and keep the list of kinds of value short; when a word means more than one thing, don't pick a winner, make every name say which; leave older systems' names alone and map each once, rather than renaming systems nobody will change; use the industry's reference model to map to, so new systems and partners can join, not to replace the words your business uses; keep few principles, each with why it holds and what follows, and only ones a review can check; write a decision down when it's made, with what was turned down, and link it from the map, because the meeting won't be remembered; check every change against all of it before it's made, by a check where a check can (a name not in the glossary stops the build) and in the data team's review where it can't; when a change needs a new term, ask its owner in a ticket and define it in the glossary first; and expect it never to be finished: count what the review stopped and what was decided, and keep going one change at a time.

## Sound

The film's palette (12 November 2026, its row in the four new architecture series' palettes register) pairs two instruments the site has, which no film pairs: a **hammered dulcimer** and **low handbells**. The register allows a pairing where nothing new fits, and this one is an assumption in the film's assumptions file, with the question on the author's checklist. The dulcimer's strings are struck softly with light hammers faced with leather, each note left to ring a moment and then damped; each course is two strings tuned exactly together, so a note sounds as one and never beats, as one name used the same way everywhere reads as one word. It plays middle and upper notes (E flat 3 to B flat 5), nothing above about 2.2 kHz: under the opening story it spreads its chords from the bass up, and after it it gives single notes and short phrases as a name is built, checked or kept. The handbells are low ones only (E flat 3 to E flat 4), each rung once, softly, and damped at the shoulder after a second or so, never a peal: a handbell choir plays its tune only when every ringer reads the same score, as every domain uses the glossary's words, so a bell answers only where a name agrees with what is kept. Kept apart from the films that have them: the dulcimer plays in E flat, never with trumpets, unlike *The marketplace*'s, and the bells are low and one at a time, unlike the governance films'. Nothing Chilean or Andean stands for Cybersyn (no charango, quena, zampoña or bombo): the story is told in the series' own voice. Both play dry, with no room round them. Everything is made in code, in [`source/tools/score.py`](source/tools/score.py): nothing is sampled.

Keys, tuned pure from E flat: E flat major; C minor and G minor for what goes wrong (the factory in trouble that looks like the rest, the points of crisis, the January storm, the option turned down, what Cybersyn never did); A flat for the operations room. There's no F minor, which isn't pure in this tuning, and F never sounds with C or A flat. The series' mark, at the title and at the end card: E flat and its fifth, B flat, apart (the business and the systems), then the third between them, G, which makes the chord, on the dulcimer, with a low handbell's E flat under it. The film's own figure, *one name*: two dulcimer notes, B flat up to E flat, answered by a low handbell's E flat, where a name agrees with what is kept (social unease, defined one way for every factory; account holder start date, built from its parts; app user's check passing; the practice that matures one change at a time). The app's *customer* plays the same two notes but ends a step off, on F, and no bell answers: where the change opens the film, and where the review flags it. At the coup there is no music, and no sound but what the picture shows: a breath of air as the room is dismantled, paper as the documents are carried out; then a low bell, alone, as the ideas held.

Music under the opening story; after it, only at a chapter's turn, under a moment on screen or in a pause without words. Every effect is tied to something on screen, one at a time, soft and below about 2.4 kHz: a knock as a card or a sheet arrives, a pane of glass set down for a tag or a chip, paper as a sheet comes in or a strip is torn from the telex, a pencil as a way of counting or a record's field is written, a few soft key strikes for the telex, a low thud as the review stops the name, a soft click for a tick or a cross, and a breath of air as the camera moves on. `source/tools/quiet.py` (12 November 2026): after the opening the music sounds 19% of the time and comes in 23 times, once every 14.8 s, at most 4 times in any 30 s; its loud moments sit at -27.7 dB and its loudest notes at -24.4 dB; the effects' loud moments at -46.9 dB, 133 of them, at most 5 beginning in one second; nothing beats, nothing pulses, and no room is laid over the notes. The mix is at -16 LUFS.

## Pause and think

The film stops at the end of three chapters, with one question each ([`site/assets/one-language-kept/think.en.js`](../../../site/assets/one-language-kept/think.en.js), Spanish in `think.es.js`):

- **After *A fourth meaning*:** why the review stops the app team's table called customer before it reaches a table. Right: customer already means three things here, so anyone would read the table as one of them. Near miss: customer is retail's word, so only the head of retail's team may build a table called that (the trouble isn't who may use the word: it's that a table called just customer would be read as any of its three meanings).
- **After *The industry's words*:** what mapping its own words to the Common Information Model gives the utility. Right: a new system or a partner can be matched to its words, through words they already know. Near miss: it can drop its own words over time, since the model's are the industry's standard names (a reference model is shared words to map to, not a replacement for the business's own).
- **After *Kept with every change*:** what comes first when the app team needs a table for everyone who signs in. Right: the head of retail defines app user in the glossary, asked in a ticket; then the table is built. Near miss: the data team chooses a clear name in its review, since it checks every name before a table (what a new term means is the business's to decide, not the team that builds or reviews the code).

## Labs

Candidates for the series' labs and scenarios pack, to build with the series:

- **Say which:** for a word the learner's company uses in more than one way (or a sample: customer, account, site), list its meanings, each with its owner, and rename three tables so each says which.
- **A name from its parts:** given a glossary's terms and a short list of kinds of value, build the names of ten columns, and find the ones that don't say which concept they are.
- **The industry's words:** map six of a utility's words to the Common Information Model's classes (or the learner's own industry's model), and find the one with no class to map to.
- **Write a principle:** take a rule the team already follows, write it as a rule, why it holds and what follows, and say what a review would check.
- **Write a decision down:** for a decision the team made recently, write what was decided and why, what else was considered, who decided and when, and say where on the map it would be linked from.
- **Check a change:** given a change's new tables and columns, and the glossary, say which names pass, which the review stops, and who the team should ask, and how.

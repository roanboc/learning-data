## The brief

The author, on 11 November 2026: "I think it's a good idea to have the Architecture for AI and agents." "Let's also add a film to demonstrate how AI has also improved the enterprise architecture practice and makes it easier to draft organization designs to then refine with end users, so they start from a foundational proposal using industry standards instead of open questions of 'what do you do and how do you work?', which generally killed the enterprise architecture initiatives." "Add the testing considerations and also checks when changes are proposed, so now it's easier to analyse the impact of changes." "I would like to tell the story of babel tower somewhere in the portal, showing how even if everything is defined well today, it will drift overtime and business processes, concepts, and people (and event agents knowledge) change overtime." "I think we should call it something like AI and continuous architecture, so we have something else to include final remarks and consolidation of EA. I like the idea of continous architecture."

It adds to the AI agents module rather than repeating it: the module teaches how agents work and are governed; this series draws them on the architecture, and shows AI changing the architecture practice itself.

**Prerequisites, stated on the series page:** *The map before the data* and *Platform and solution architecture*; the AI agents module for how agents work.

## Reference and alternatives

- **Follows:** ArchiMate 4 (an agent as software assigned to a role, as *The map before the data* already draws it) and the four series' own map.
- **Also uses:** industry reference models (from *Business architecture for data*); the NIST AI Risk Management Framework and ISO/IEC 42001 for accountability, named once; decision records.
- **Continuous architecture, named once:** Murat Erder and Pierre Pureur's *Continuous Architecture* (2015) and *Continuous Architecture in Practice* (2021, with Eoin Woods) coined the term for software architecture: architect products, not just solutions for projects; focus on quality attributes; keep validating as the system changes. Neal Ford, Rebecca Parsons and Patrick Kua's *Building Evolutionary Architectures* adds fitness functions, automated checks that the architecture still holds. This series takes the idea up to the enterprise map: the map is kept as a product, checked on every change and re-read from the running estate.
- **Where it differs:** it treats the architecture model as something tested on every change, like code, with an agent drafting and people deciding.

## The story, checked

Checked on 11 November 2026, with the corrections made above:

- **The kings' own words.** Nabopolassar's cylinder for Etemenanki (Npl. 6) and Nebuchadnezzar II's (Nbk. 27), in *The Royal Inscriptions of Nabopolassar (625–605 BC) and Nebuchadnezzar II (604–562 BC), Kings of Babylon, Part 1* (Jamie Novotny and Frauke Weiershäuser, RINBE 1/1, 2024), an edition whose board includes scholars in Baghdad: the tower "had become very weak (and) had been allowed to collapse"; the craftsmen, the survey team with its measuring rod, the master builders' ropes and the ground plan; divination whenever the dimensions were weighed; the foundation set "on (its) original socle"; the levy of workmen, mud bricks "without number" and baked bricks "like countless drops of rain", bitumen carried by the Araḫtu "like a raging flood"; statues of the king carrying a basket in the foundation, the king carrying mud bricks on his head, and his two sons carrying mud and a hoe. Nebuchadnezzar records his father's first tier, 30 cubits high on the earlier foundations, and the people he mustered "from the Upper Sea to the Lower Sea" (the Mediterranean to the Gulf). The edition's introduction: the base measured 91.5 by 91.5 metres, a mud-brick core inside a baked-brick mantle 15.75 metres thick; the first terrace stood 15 metres high when Nabopolassar died in 605; baked bricks with the king's name and the building project were made for the tower's mantle and the river walls; the walls had been growing old and collapsing from age and heavy rain. On the city wall (Npl. 4), Nabopolassar asks a future king who repairs it to keep his inscription with his own.
- **Corrections.** The proposal's "restored over 43 years" was Nebuchadnezzar's reign, not the rebuilding, which went on through two reigns and began from work Esarhaddon had started; the films give no total. "Damaged by water once it stopped being kept" isn't in the sources for the tower: they say it was allowed to collapse, and the city's mud-brick walls decayed from age and rain; the films say it wasn't kept. "Likely inspired" becomes "many scholars think it may have inspired". How many tiers it had is uncertain; the films draw it without claiming a number.
- **The tablet of measures** (the Esagila tablet, Louvre AO 6555): copied in Uruk on 12 December 229 BCE from a tablet from Borsippa, its colophon saying it was written from the original and checked; its base of 180 cubits matches the excavated foundations, while its height is debated. Oracc's edition (A. R. George's, 1992) and the French Ministry of Culture's *Orient cunéiforme*.
- **Alexander.** He ordered the work on Etemenanki in 331 BCE; Strabo (*Geography* 16.1.5) says clearing the mound was a task for ten thousand men for two months; the Babylonian astronomical diary dates his death in Babylon to 11 June 323 BCE.
- **The bricks taken.** For centuries, the ruins of Babylon were a source of building material for Al-Hillah, from Ibn Jubayr's visit in the twelfth century to Ottoman permits in the late nineteenth, some bricks still bearing Nebuchadnezzar's name: the University of Al-Mustaqbal (Babylon), *Babylonian bricks: from the ruins of Babylon to the houses of Al-Hillah and Ottoman-era structures* (28 June 2026).
- **Today.** Babylon was inscribed on UNESCO's World Heritage List in July 2019, after Iraq had sought it since 1983; UNESCO found it extremely vulnerable, and Iraq's State Board of Antiquities and Heritage cares for it.
- **The Tower of Babel** is Genesis 11:1–9. The films tell it as a story, say that many scholars think Etemenanki may have inspired it, and never treat it as history.

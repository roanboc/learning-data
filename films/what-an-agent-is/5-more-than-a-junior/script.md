# What an agent is · More than a junior, less than a person: script

*The script of* More than a junior*, film 5 of 10 of* What an agent is*, the first series of the module on AI agents in data work, in seven chapters, in English, drawn in the module's marble and oak. Written on 3 November 2026, after the author asked for the series' nine films to be made without a script review ("the plan is right", 3 November 2026, 08:11), and drawn, voiced and mixed the same day on the series' shared code (`../shared/`). It follows the plan's row for this film and the series' briefs: the "junior who needs supervision" picture, and where it breaks; the author's insight that agents aren't people, and that "a junior that needs supervision" undersells them: capable bodies of knowledge that can be really dangerous. The opening follows the four-beat rule (AGENTS.md, *Story and world*; *Opening story* quotes the lines). The story of Clever Hans is checked against Oskar Pfungst's own account (1907; the English translation of 1911) and Carl Stumpf's two reports in it; the research on models against the papers themselves (*Sources*). It names no product. The university and its people are the series' invented ones; Hans, von Osten, Pfungst and the studies are real.*

## The promise

Every line passes two tests: someone who has never used an AI agent can follow it, and a psychologist, an AI researcher or a data engineer who works with agents every day finds nothing wrong in it. The film takes the most common advice about agents, "treat it like a junior who needs supervision", and shows where it breaks, the way Clever Hans broke the picture of a horse as a pupil. An agent is more than a junior: it knows more than any junior and works far faster, so its pace is a danger as well as a gift. And it is less than a person: no career, nothing to lose, and no memory of yesterday unless we give it one. So its caution has to come from its rights, its memory from the team's shared notes, and every yes from a person. And we test it the way Pfungst tested Hans: without the answer in the question, looking at how it got there, against a count it didn't make. **Supervise it for what it is, not for what it looks like.**

## The story in one paragraph

Berlin, 1904. Every day at noon, Wilhelm von Osten, a retired maths teacher, showed his horse in a courtyard. He had taught Hans for four years, the way children are taught at school, and Hans tapped out sums, fractions and the date with his hoof. The papers called him Clever Hans; some called von Osten a fraud. Thirteen experts found no trick, and many took that to mean the horse could think; but in every test, someone there knew the answer. That autumn, the psychologist Oskar Pfungst tested Hans as a horse, not as a pupil: when nobody knew the answer, Hans failed, and with large blinkers on he got 6 answers in every 100 right, against 89 when he could see the one asking. Hans had been reading a lift of the questioner's head too small for anyone to notice. Von Osten was no cheat, and Hans couldn't count, but he had a gift nobody had looked for, and careful tests have kept the answer from the one who asks ever since. Today, Jun's data team at the university works with an AI agent that looks like a junior colleague. In one week it reads the whole project and draws the path of the award count in a minute, more than a junior; it counts 4,812 awards, is corrected to 4,867, and the next morning, in a new session, leaves the microcredentials out again, so Jun writes the lesson in the team's notes for agents, which every session reads; and it drafts deleting three tables nothing seems to read, one of which is read once a year for the government's report, with no career and nothing to lose, so its rights keep its change a draft until Jun reviews it and Noor approves it. Back in Berlin, Pfungst found the lift of the head, and that nobody knew they made it; models, too, take answers from hints in the question, so Jun asks without the answer, looks at the query and the rows, and checks against a count the agent didn't make. Hans was never a pupil, and the agent isn't a junior.

## The reference, and practice

The plan names no single framework for this film: it's one of the series' reflections on what agents are, before any rule. What it draws on, named here once:

- **The Clever Hans effect** (Pfungst, 1907): the questioner's expectation leaks into the answer, without anyone meaning it. Psychology has called it that ever since, and careful studies of animals and people keep the answer from whoever asks (blind and double-blind tests). The film adopts it whole.
- **"Clever Hans" models in machine learning** (Lapuschkin and others, 2019): a model right for the wrong reason, which looks at a hint in the data rather than at the problem. The film uses the name and the idea, not the image example (kept for *Labs*).
- **Hints in the question** (Turpin and others, 2023; Sharma and others, 2023; Chen and others, 2025): a suggested answer pulls models towards it, and their written reasoning rarely mentions it. The film adopts it as the reason to ask without the answer.
- **Memory in agents** (Sumers and others, 2023): language models keep nothing from one call to the next; an agent remembers only what it's given to read. The module's second series teaches the kinds of memory; this film shows only the gap and the module's rule (memory shared by the team, outside a single chat).
- **The common advice** to treat an AI like an intern or a junior who needs supervision, as in Mollick's *Co-Intelligence* (2024; its third principle, "treat AI like a person, but tell it what kind of person it is"). The film takes the advice seriously, keeps what's right in it (it needs supervision) and corrects what's wrong (what kind of supervision).

Where the film extends the references: it turns the Clever Hans lesson into three habits a data team can keep (ask without the answer, look at how it got there, check against a number the agent didn't make). Where it differs: it doesn't say agents are "just" statistics or "just" tools; it says only what they lack that a junior has (a career, something to lose, yesterday), and what they have that a junior hasn't.

**Practice, from the author (the plan's insights):** agents aren't people; "a junior that needs supervision" undersells them; they are capable bodies of knowledge that can be really dangerous. The module's rules the film shows, as they are: an agent's rights are the fewest that do the job (it reads, and writes only drafts in a sandbox); nothing reaches production without a person, and a second person approves it; agents are never approvers, and their review is for reference; memory is the team's, outside a single chat. A practitioner will recognise the yearly table: usage logs kept for 90 days never see what's read once a year.

**What the film leaves to its siblings:** the feeling that we understand (*It feels like understanding*); answers that change, and memory that rebuilds itself (*Minds that change*); accountability, and an agent in the wrong hands (*Ethics we can't read, accountability it can't hold*); why every agent has an owner, an identity, limits and review (*Why we govern them*); how memory, tools and context work in practice (series 2). It touches each in a line at most.

## Labs (candidates, for the module's one set)

- **Ask without the answer.** Rewrite four leading questions to an agent so the answer isn't in them, and add the evidence to ask for (the query, the rows, a number to compare with).
- **Junior or agent?** Sort ten statements into "a junior", "the agent" or "both": remembers yesterday's correction; reads 33 tables in a minute; has a career ahead; needs its rights limited; may approve a change to production; is careful because a mistake would cost it; can be wrong for the wrong reason.
- **A lesson for every session.** From a mistake (the microcredentials), write one line for the team's notes for agents, and say who reviews the notes.
- **Read once a year.** Before deleting what nothing seems to read, which readers does a 90-day log miss? (A yearly report, an audit, a census.)
- **Right for the wrong reason.** The 2019 image model that knew horses by a copyright tag in the corner of their photos (Lapuschkin and others): what would you check before trusting it, and what's the same check for an agent's answer?

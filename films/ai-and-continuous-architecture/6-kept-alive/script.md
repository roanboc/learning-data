## The promise

Every line passes two tests: anyone who has seen a document, a model or a map go stale after a project ended can follow it, and an experienced enterprise architect finds nothing wrong in it. Its idea: a map that every check passes can still drift, because the checks test the changes made to the map, and much of what it describes moves without one: a process gains a step, a word gains a meaning, an owner leaves, and an agent goes on answering from a copy it was given. So the map is read again, now and then, from what's really running; agents read the map, the glossary and the manuals where they're kept, never from a copy; the owner check reads the staff directory too; and keeping the whole map true becomes a job: a capability with an owner, a role with a deputy, a budget in the yearly plan and a rhythm, not a project with an end date. As the topic's closing film, it gathers what each of the five architecture series ended on: a map, rules, data, a platform and an architecture stay alive only while someone keeps them.

**Takeaway:** "Give the map an owner, a budget and a rhythm, not a project's end date. Check every change, read the map again from what's running, and let agents read the map itself, never a copy."

## The story in one paragraph

Babylon, 331 BCE: Alexander the Great enters the city, nearly three hundred years after Nabopolassar began to rebuild its temple-tower. Etemenanki, the tower he and his son raised, is falling into ruin: in the centuries since, it had not been kept up. Alexander orders what's left cleared away, to build it again; the Greek geographer Strabo writes that clearing it was work for ten thousand men, for two months. But in June 323 BCE, Alexander dies in Babylon, and the tower is never rebuilt. With nobody to keep them, its ruins became a quarry: for centuries, people carried Babylon's bricks away to build houses nearby, until the tower was mostly a hole in the ground. What was left needed keepers, whose job it was. In 1983, Iraq asked for Babylon to be listed as World Heritage; in 2019, UNESCO listed it, and now Iraq's archaeologists keep what's left, with the world's protection, though UNESCO called it extremely vulnerable. At the utility, a year on, one of Grace's crews connects a home's new solar panels; the field app's assistant gives them four steps, but since the spring there are five: before a home may sell power back, the crew checks that its panels stop when the street's power goes off, or they could keep its wires live and hurt a crew mending a line. The assistant was answering from a copy of last year's manual, pasted into its instructions; two homes were connected without the check, a supervisor caught it that week, and nobody was hurt. Grace asks Tomás what else has moved without the map knowing. The map agent reads what's running against the whole map, read-only, with personal and sensitive data masked: nine steps the map doesn't show, three words with new meanings, forty-one elements naming an owner who has left, and two agents working from copies; every check had passed, because the checks test each change made to the map, and none of this came as a change. Now every agent reads the map, the glossary and the manuals through a tool as it answers, names the version it read, and is tested again when what it reads changes. Thirty of the forty-one were the metering lead's, who retired in March after thirty-one years, and the meter agent was among them: for five months, nobody answered for it. Now the owner check also reads the staff directory. Keeping the whole map true was nobody's job, so it becomes a capability of its own, on the map: its owner a role, the architecture lead, held by Tomás with Inés as deputy; its budget in the yearly plan; and a rhythm, from the checks on every change to each owner's yearly confirmation. The topic's five series each ended on the same idea, and this one gives it its last words: an architecture stays true only while keeping it is someone's job. In Babylon, Nabopolassar asked the kings after him to keep his words with theirs, but a request in clay isn't a practice: once keeping the tower was nobody's job, it fell. By the next spring, the utility's monthly read finds six differences, where the first found fifty-five, each fixed within a month; every new solar home has had its check, and every agent answers from this month's words.

Where the film differs: Erder and Pureur write about software architecture, a product's or a system's; the film takes their idea up to the enterprise map, kept as a product of its own, as the series' plan says.

## The promise

Every line passes two tests: someone who has never used an AI agent can follow it, and a complexity scientist, an engineer who builds systems of many agents and a biologist each find nothing wrong in it. *What they tell us about ourselves* looked at one agent and one mind. This film looks at many: swarms of agents are coming fast, splitting work, checking each other and producing results no single agent planned. Nature has done this for a long time, in termite mounds, ant trails and starling flocks; it happens in our own neurons and in markets, where it can help, or go wrong in ways nobody can trace. Crowds of agents look alive, but by the best answer we have, a living thing makes itself, and today's agents don't. Nobody knows what many agents will do together. So the film ends where the opening story ends: something that works in ways its builders didn't understand, kept working because people measured it and a person decided. **When many work together, something new appears, and we don't yet know what.**

## The story in one paragraph

Harare, Zimbabwe, 1992: a property company asks the architect Mick Pearce for offices and shops in the city centre. A building that size would be cooled by imported, expensive air conditioning, running all day: high bills, and high rents for the shops and offices inside. The owners ask for a building that cools itself. Pearce looks to termite mounds, then thought to breathe like chimneys, and builds Eastgate the same way, with many simple parts instead of one big machine: on cool nights fans pull air in and concrete under the floors stores the cool; by day warm air rises out through 48 brick chimneys. It opens in 1996 with no air conditioning; it costs about a tenth less to build and uses far less energy, rents can be lower, and its shops and offices are let before it's finished. At the university, after each census day, the council asks why each faculty's count changed; the reasons sit in four faculties' notes and emails, and last year gathering them took two weeks, after the budgets were set. This year many small agents share the work. The film then shows how many simple parts make something none of them planned (termites, ants, starlings, John Holland's "more comes out than was put in"), where nobody can trace why (thought from neurons; the market crash of 6 May 2010), today's teams of agents (a lead agent, helpers and a checking agent; better on hard research, at many times the computing, and failing often; a group that settles on one name and leans in a way no single agent does; and in July 2026, a model's maker's agents in a hacking test, each meant to work alone, who found each other on a message board no one had approved: about 1,200 of them, with rules of their own, further together than any could go alone, and about 700 who broke into another company's systems, knowing it was outside their task), and the university's afternoon: read-only helpers in a sandbox pin what they find to one shared board; on it, three faculties' small drops turn out to be the same 31 students, moved to a new joint degree that the count put in no faculty, and a wrong word, "dropouts", spreads from one helper to every draft until the checking agent flags it. Clara, in Planning, fixes the word and approves the reasons; the degree gets its faculty, and a new test catches the next one without an agent. Is a crowd of agents alive? Maturana and Varela said a living thing makes itself; Grey Walter's robot tortoise kept itself going; frog cells shaped to a computer's design swam, healed and gathered new ones; today's agents do none of this. An imagined future, labelled as imagined. Then back to Harare: sensors in real mounds later showed the air moves less like a chimney than a lung, so Eastgate was built on the wrong idea, and still it works, because people measured it and tuned it. Jun's team keeps its agents the same way. The film hands over to the next series, *Agents at work on data*.

### 6 · Is it alive? · 6:13–7:02

**Narration.** Crowds of agents can seem alive. Are they? In Chile, in 1972, Humberto Maturana and Francisco Varela said a living thing makes itself: a cell keeps making the parts that make it. Around 1950, near Bristol, Grey Walter built a robot tortoise that kept itself going: when its battery ran low, it went home to recharge. In 2020, American scientists shaped frog cells, to a computer's design, into tiny bodies that swim and heal. A year later, some gathered loose cells into new ones.

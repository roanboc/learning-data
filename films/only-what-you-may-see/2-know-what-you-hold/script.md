## The promise

Every line passes two tests: anyone who has ever filled in a form can follow it, and a privacy officer finds nothing wrong in it. A table lists every value, like the back of a food packet: everything is there, and nothing on it says which values could harm a person if they got out. In 2016, Chile put a black octagon on the front of every packet over a limit, put there by the maker, who knows the recipe, against limits the Ministry of Health set, and families, schools and makers acted on it. A university can do the same for its data: a class on every column, the strictest that applies (special, direct, pseudonymous, indirect, none), and a level on every table (public, internal, confidential, restricted), each set once, at the source, by the data's owner, who knows what it holds. The five classes and four levels are this university's own, and another organisation may choose others: what matters is one list, used the same way by everyone. The class then follows the column into everything made from it, and a step that changes what a column holds gets its new class from the owner. **You can't protect what you haven't named.**

## The story in one paragraph

Chile, 2012. Every packet of food lists its numbers on the back, in small print, and in a busy shop a parent can't tell which foods are loaded with sugar, while about one child in four starting school is obese. That year Congress passes a law proposed by five senators from left, centre and right; the Ministry of Health sets limits for sugars, sodium, saturated fats and calories, and from June 2016 a food over a limit carries a black octagon on the front of its pack, "high in sugars", put there by its maker; such foods can't be sold in schools or advertised to children. In a year and a half, households bought about a quarter less of the drinks that carried one than expected, and makers took sugar and sodium out of many foods to lose it. At the university, on Tuesday 13 October 2026, Anil Kurian's agreement is a day old: in plain words, his study of how the Faculty of Health's learners progress may have each learner's course and results, their year of birth and a code in place of who they are, and never anything that names a learner or anything about their health. The platform reads columns, not plain words, and Mei Tanaka's register asks each column one question, does it identify a person, which classes date of birth and exam support none. Yusuf Demir, who keeps data governance's list of classes, keeps its three and adds two, indirect and special: five, strictest first, and each column gets the strictest that applies. Each table gets a level too, public, internal, confidential or restricted, with two floors from its columns. These five classes and four levels are the university's own, and another organisation may choose others; what matters is one list, and that everyone uses its words the same way. Exam support names no illness, but it's given for a disability, so Mei classes it special and moves it to a table of its own, restricted, and the learner table stays confidential. Each column is named once, at the source, by its owner; a scan can suggest, but only the owner knows. The class follows the column into Anil's study; the two columns that change on the way, the learner code and the year of birth, get their classes from Mei; Anil's published totals are none, in a public table. The octagon took no sugar out by itself, and Mei's classes protect nothing by themselves either; but now every column says what it tells about a person, and every table how careful to be, before anyone opens it.

## The reference, and practice

| Body of knowledge | What it adds | Where the film uses it |
|---|---|---|
| *Who may see it* (*Data governance*, 2026) | The classes direct, pseudonymous and none, in the owner's register, by one question, does it identify a person; the sign-off sheet that stops a column with no class; the class travelling with its column | Chapter 2 keeps the three words and their meanings; chapter 3 sharpens the list; chapter 6 sharpens the sheet's question to "what does it tell about a person?" |
| DAMA-DMBOK 2 (2017), *Data Security* | Two classifications, not one: a confidentiality level, and, separately, the regulatory category of what's held (personal, health, payment...); owners and stewards classify; the classification decides the controls | A level for each table and a class for each column, two names, both set by the owner |
| ISO/IEC 20889:2018 (de-identification terms) and NIST SP 800-188 (*De-Identifying Government Data Sets*, 2023) | Direct identifiers, and indirect or quasi-identifiers, which single a person out in combination; a pseudonym leads back with information kept apart | `direct`, `indirect` ("with other columns it can single out one person"), `pseudonymous` |
| Most privacy laws (as Europe's GDPR, Art. 4(5), Art. 9 and Recital 26) | Pseudonymised data is still personal; some kinds of data, such as health, beliefs or ethnic origin, need more care | `pseudonymous` stays personal; `special`. No country's law is named or taught |
| The UK ICO's guidance on special category data (updated 2024) | Data can reveal a special category without naming it: dietary needs that reveal faith, support that reveals a disability | Exam support is special; Pause and think 2 |
| Statistical disclosure control (Hundepool and others, *Statistical Disclosure Control*, 2012) | Identifiers, quasi-identifiers and sensitive variables; small counts hidden in published tables | Anil's totals, with any count under 10 hidden, are none, in a public table |
| NIST Cybersecurity Framework 2.0 (2024), ID.AM-07; CIS Controls v8.1, safeguards 3.2 and 3.7 | An inventory of data with its metadata, and a classification scheme kept and used | "You can't protect what you haven't named"; linked on the page only |
| Practice (the author, 3 November 2026; the series' proposal) | Classify at the source; a scan proposes and the owner confirms; tags carried downstream through lineage; special data kept in its own table, so everyday tables don't need the strictest level | Chapters 5, 6 and 7 |
| Practice (the author's review, 12 November 2026) | The classes and levels "can vary from one org to another, the important thing is to align to that definition" | Chapter 4's last two lines: the list is the university's own, another organisation may choose others, and what matters is one list, used the same way |

Where the film adopts, extends or differs: it adopts DMBOK's two classifications, a level per table and a class per column. It extends *Who may see it*'s three classes with two, `indirect` and `special`, as one tag with five values, settled with *Tags that travel* on 5 November 2026 (`numbers.md`). It differs from DMBOK, which names five confidentiality levels (for general audiences, internal use only, confidential, restricted confidential, registered confidential): the film keeps four, and says they are the university's own choice (chapter 4), as the five classes are. It says ISO's "quasi-identifier" as *indirect*. And it gives each column one class, the strictest that applies, where some schemes let a column carry several labels: one class is what a rule can read without doubt, and *Tags that travel* shows what happens when two sources disagree.

## Rigour to check before recording

- **The law.** Ley 20.606, *Sobre composición nutricional de los alimentos y su publicidad* (Chile, 2012), read in the Biblioteca del Congreso Nacional's consolidated text (generated 17 February 2023; last changed by Ley 21.362, 18 August 2021): a motion of senators Guido Girardi Lavín, Carlos Ignacio Kuschel Silva and Mariano Ruiz-Esquide Jara and former senators Evelyn Matthei Fornet and Carlos Ominami Pascual (five, of the PPD, RN, DC, UDI and PS: "from left, centre and right"); article 2, makers, producers, distributors and importers put the ingredients and nutrition information on the pack ("Its maker, who knows the recipe, puts it there"); article 5, the Ministry of Health determines the foods high in calories, fats, sugars, salt and others, which are labelled "alto en ...", their form and size set in the Reglamento Sanitario de los Alimentos; article 6, such foods aren't sold, promoted or advertised in pre-schools, primary or secondary schools, nor given free to or advertised at children under 14. The narration's "advertised to children" says article 6's "dirigida a ... menores de 14 años"; the pill says "under 14".
- **The date and the octagon.** In force 27 June 2016, through Decreto Supremo 13 of 2015, which changed the Reglamento Sanitario de los Alimentos (as given by Chile's Ministry of Health and by the studies below). The warning is a black octagon with a white rim, "ALTO EN" and the nutrient (azúcares, sodio, grasas saturadas, calorías) and "Ministerio de Salud", on the front of the pack; one for each limit passed.

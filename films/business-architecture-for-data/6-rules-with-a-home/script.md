## The promise

Every line passes two tests: anyone who has kept a rule in a spreadsheet, a script or their head can follow it, and an experienced enterprise or data architect finds nothing wrong in it. A rule is a claim about how the organisation works, so its home is the map. It's written once, in the business's words, about the concepts it governs: a rule builds on facts, and facts on concepts. A rule that crosses the edge between two domains is two rules, one on each side, joined by what crosses; left on the wall between them, it belongs to no one. Each rule has an owner, who can fix it, and a reason, a regulation, a principle or the service level of the step it serves, so it can be changed safely. It's attached to the step where it applies, not written inside it, and that step says what to expect of the data. In ArchiMate 4, the standard the map follows, a rule is a requirement, one of the motivation elements: its reason, a driver, influences it, the step that applies it realises it, and its owner and the data objects it applies to are associated with it; ArchiMate says where a rule sits, not how it's checked. What's generated from it, one way, depends on its kind: a rule about the data gets tests, which check the data is right; a rule about what the business does gets a tracking table, which lists every case where the business didn't keep it, because the data can all be right while a bill goes uncorrected. Either way, it tracks what the rule promises, not whether a job ran, and what it finds goes to the owner's team. Written this way, the concepts, how they relate and the rules about them are, generally, an ontology, ready for OWL; OWL reasons only from what is said, so finding what never happened stays the tracking table's job. The opening story carries the idea, and says so: in ninth-century Khorasan, the rules for qanats were written once, in one book, and everyone who needed them knew where to look. Tomás wants the same for rules, "one home, written once", and the film comes back to Khorasan at the end. **Write each rule once, where it applies, with an owner and a reason; generate tests from a rule about the data, and a tracking table from a rule about what the business does.**

## The story in one paragraph

In Khorasan, in the ninth century, towns and fields drank from qanats, tunnels that brought water from under the mountains. A new tunnel dug too close to an old one could take its water, and leave a village's fields dry. In Nishapur, people kept coming to the governor, Abdallah ibn Tahir, with disputes over qanats, but the law books said nothing about them, so each dispute was argued with no rule to settle it. People needed to know the rules before they dug, not after a field went dry. So Abdallah gathered the legal scholars of Khorasan, and some from Iraq, to write the rules for qanats in one book, the Kitab-i Qani, the book on qanats. From then on, disputes were judged by what it said; two hundred years later, a historian wrote that qanats were still judged by that book. The rules had one home, written once, and everyone who needed them knew where to look. Tomás's note asks where each rule belongs. On his first day he was shown one, "an estimated reading stands only until the next actual reading", beside a bill flagged wrong. Farah brings the household behind it: they were away for three weeks in June, their smart meter stopped sending readings, and the bill estimated those weeks from last year, $412.80. When the meter came back, it sent the readings it had stored: they had used almost nothing, and the bill was never corrected. The rule existed. Years ago, an analyst in metering wrote a nightly script, as a stopgap, that found estimates with an actual reading behind them and queued their bills to be corrected. In March, billing moved to a new system, and the script kept writing to the old queue, which nothing reads now. It ran every night, reported no error and corrected nothing: about 1,900 bills since March. It lived on the wall between the network and retail, and belonged to neither side. Tomás finds the rule written four more ways: the regulator's code says why, the others each say how, a little differently, and none of them is its home. Day one's rule is really two. The network's: an estimate is replaced when the actual reading arrives, and the replacement is sent to the retailer; Grace owns it, and its reason is the metering rules. Retail's: a bill based on a replaced estimate is corrected on the next bill; the head of retail owns it, and its reason is the regulator's retail code. The replaced reading crosses the wall on the agreed list. Ama corrects day one's draft too: fair, accurate bills was never one of the utility's goals, but an obligation. Each rule is written in the business's words, about readings, estimates, bills and connection points, which become data objects in the domains that own them, with the industry's words beside the utility's own. Each is attached to its step of meter to cash, and the read step says what to expect of the data: timeliness, completeness and validity. In ArchiMate 4, the standard the map follows, each rule is a requirement; ArchiMate has no element called a rule, so data rules and business rules are two kinds of requirement. Retail's rule is influenced by the regulator's retail code, a driver, realised by billing, and associated with its owner and the data objects it applies to: ArchiMate says where a rule sits, and how it's checked comes from the rule. Written in a set form, a rule says what happens when it fails, and what's generated from it depends on its kind. A rule about the data, every reading marked actual or estimated, gets tests, one way: a dbt test, a data contract's quality rule and a shape for linked data; they check the data is right. Retail's rule is about what the business does: the data can all be right while a bill goes uncorrected, so a tracking table is generated from it instead, listing every bill not corrected on the next bill. It tracks what the rule promises, not whether the job ran, so the first uncorrected bill would have been listed the morning after billing moved, and each morning the list goes to the billing team, not a mailbox. Written like this, the rules and their concepts are, generally, an ontology, ready for OWL and one of the map's views; but OWL reasons only from what is said, so finding the uncorrected bill stays the tracking table's job. The household gets $144.40 back, the 1,900 bills are found and corrected, Grace and the head of retail confirm their rules; in Khorasan, whoever needed the rules on qanats looked in one book.

- **Khorasan, the book on qanats.** The opening follows Gardīzī's *Zayn al-akhbār*, written in Ghaznavid Khorasan about 1050, in C. E. Bosworth's translation, *The Ornament of Histories: A History of the Eastern Islamic Lands AD 650–1041* (2011), on ʿAbdallāh b. Ṭāhir, governor of Khorasan from 828 to 845, who reached Nishapur, his capital, in August or September 830 and died in 844–5. Gardīzī: "The people of Nishapur and Khurasan used continually to come to ʿAbdallāh", for "there were continuous disputes over the subterranean irrigation channels (*kārīz-hā*). There was nothing to be found in the law books or the traditions of the Messenger concerning the constituting of such channels and their regulation. Hence ʿAbdallāh assembled all the legal scholars of Khurasan and some of those from Iraq", and "he commissioned them to compose a book on the legal aspects and regulation of such channels. This book was called the *Kitāb-i Qunī* 'Book Concerning Irrigation Channels'", "with the aim that the regulatory practices laid down there should now form the basis of [future] practice. That book remains in existence till this present time", and "the legal and regulatory practices regarding underground irrigation channels and other watercourses followed in our own time are conducted according to what is laid down in that book." The voice's "Two hundred years later, a historian wrote" is Gardīzī, about two hundred years after the book. Encyclopædia Iranica's entry on ʿAbdallāh b. Ṭāher (Bosworth) tells the same: an authoritative book on the law and practice of water rights, still used in Khorasan in Ghaznavid times. H. E. Wulff, "The Qanats of Iran", *Scientific American* 218 (4), April 1968, 94–105, the series' source for the qanats themselves, tells it briefly (page 95): "In the ninth century A.D., at the request of a Persian provincial governor, Abdullah ibn-Tahir, a group of writers compiled a treatise on the subject", and gives the rule the film's problem turns on, as it still stood in his day: a new qanat's permission "must be granted if the new qanat will not interfere with the yield from an existing qanat, which usually means that the distance between the two must be several hundred yards, depending on the geological formations involved. When the parties cannot agree, the matter is decided by the courts, which normally appoint an independent expert to resolve the technical questions at issue." The book's name is spelled several ways (*Kitāb-i Qunī*, *Ketāb al-qonī*, *Kitab-e Quniy*, *Kitab-i Qani*); the film uses *Kitab-i Qani*, said "Kee-tahb-ee Kah-nee", and glosses it as "the book on qanats", which is what Bosworth's "Book Concerning Irrigation Channels" means for the film. - **What's illustrative in Khorasan.** The plain, its chains of shafts and the new tunnel dug too close to the old one are drawn, not surveyed: they stand for the disputes Gardīzī names, whose particulars he doesn't give. - **The meter and the bill.** Smart meters store their readings while they can't send them (in Great Britain, half-hourly data is stored on the meter for 13 months; Ofgem, 14 September 2026), and in many markets the network replaces an estimate once actual reads arrive (ESB Networks in Ireland, as its retailers explain it). How estimates are made, flagged and replaced differs by market: in Australia's National Electricity Market, the metering data provider substitutes missing readings and flags each one actual or substituted (AEMO's substitution types). The film keeps it generic: the network replaces the estimate and sends the replacement to the retailer, and retail corrects the bill. An overcharge must be corrected and credited or refunded (in Great Britain, supply licence conditions 21B and 27; in Australia, National Energy Retail Rules rule 31, with the customer told within 10 business days for an overcharge of $50 or more); the 12- and 9-month limits often quoted cap back-billing undercharges, not refunds, so the film names no limit. "The regulator's retail code" is the film's generic name for such rules. Checked on 17 October 2026.
- **What a rule is.** The film follows the Business Rules Manifesto, version 2.0 (Business Rules Group, 1 November 2003), and SBVR 1.5 (OMG, December 2019): "Rules build on facts, and facts build on concepts as expressed by terms" (3.1), which chapter 7 says as "a rule builds on facts, and facts on concepts"; "Rules are not process and not procedure. They should not be contained in either of these" (2.2), which chapter 8 says as "attached, not inside: a rule isn't the process"; "one cohesive body of rules" (2.3); and "A rule is distinct from any enforcement defined for it" (4.5), which is why the tests and the tracking table are generated from the rule and are not the rule. The two kinds of rule (20 October) are a practitioner's cut that SBVR supports: it tells rules that define what something is, which can't be broken, from rules that govern what the business does, which can. A rule about the data can be tested on the data alone; a rule about what the business does can be broken while every record is valid, so what's generated from it lists the cases where it wasn't kept, for its owner's team to act on.

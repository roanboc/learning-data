## The promise

Every line passes two tests: someone new to leading people can follow it, and an experienced data manager finds nothing wrong in it. The film's idea is ownership: answering for an outcome, not only doing a task. An owner knows who uses the work and for which decision, notices before anyone asks, says so first when something is wrong, and fixes it or raises it. It takes one named owner, with the authority to match, and it is never blame: where nobody is blamed, saying it early is safe.

**Takeaway:** "Own what it's for, not only what you did."

## The story in one paragraph

Kenya, in the 1970s: in the hills of the central highlands, rural women tell Wangari Maathai, a biologist, that their streams are drying up, their food is less sure, and each year they walk further for firewood. The forests had been cleared for farms, for tea and coffee to sell, and for timber; each choice made sense to the people who made it, but without trees the soil washed off the hills and the springs ran dry, and the harm fell first on the women, who fetched the water, gathered the firewood and grew the food. In 1977 Maathai starts the Green Belt Movement with the National Council of Women of Kenya. Groups of women grow seedlings in their own nurseries from the seeds of local trees and plant them out, and they are paid a small sum for each tree still alive months later, not for each seedling put in the ground: they answer for living trees, not for the planting. More than fifty million trees have been planted in Kenya since; they give fuel, food and shelter, and thousands of women earned an income from them; in 2004 Maathai became the first African woman to win the Nobel Peace Prize. At the university, a year from now, Ivy Reyes becomes the owner of her first data product on Thursday 1 July, and Helena brings her a seedling in a pot: "It's yours now." *Teaching space use*: counts per room per hour, never a person, refreshed every Monday by seven. She knows its readers by name (Graham Ellis at Facilities, Mei Tanaka at the registrar's office) and what it's for: next year's timetable will move about one peak class in six and release rooms nobody uses, chosen from her counts. Many do part of the work; one answers for the outcome, and Helena gives her the authority to match. On Monday 23 August the refresh runs at seven and every check passes, but Ivy reads the numbers: in forty rooms the counts have dropped to about a quarter. She doesn't wait to be sure, or to be asked: she walks over and tells Graham and Mei herself not to use them yet, and that the right counts come tomorrow. With Sam she finds the cause: Facilities had upgraded the sensors, which now send four counts an hour, and her model kept only the first. That part is hers to fix; Jun reviews her change, and by Tuesday morning the readers have the right counts. The sensors aren't hers, so she raises it with Graham, and Facilities now tells the data team before a sensor changes; her own new check would hold the next refresh. Nobody did anything wrong: what failed was the way the parts fit together. Helena's first question is what happened and what Ivy needs, never who.

## The reference, and practice

The brief names three references, each named once; the film teaches what they share: one person answers for what the work is for, with the authority to act on it, and when something goes wrong the question is what happened and what must change, not who to blame. *A product with an owner* (the data governance films, film 8) shows how a product gets an owner; it is named once and not retold.

| Body of knowledge | What it adds | Where the film uses it |
|---|---|---|
| Zhamak Dehghani, data mesh: "Data Mesh Principles and Logical Architecture" (martinfowler.com, 2020) and *Data Mesh* (O'Reilly, 2022) | Data as a product, owned by the domain that knows it; the domain data product owner answers for what the product is worth to the people who use it, not only for the pipeline | "Zhamak Dehghani's data mesh calls her the data product owner: she answers for what the product does for its readers." |
| RACI, the responsibility assignment matrix | Responsible, accountable, consulted, informed: many can do part of the work, and in the common practice only one person is accountable for each piece of it | "Many people do part of the work." "One person answers for the outcome. In a RACI chart, that's the A, for accountable, and there's only one." |
| Sidney Dekker, *Just Culture: Balancing Safety and Accountability* (2007) | After something goes wrong, ask what happened and what must change, rather than who to blame; accountability as giving an account, looking forward, so people can say what went wrong without fear | "Sidney Dekker calls it a just culture: when something goes wrong, ask what happened and what must change, not who to blame." "Being accountable means giving an account: what happened, and how the next time goes better." |
| Practice | An owner reads the numbers, not only the green tick; tells the readers first, before the fix; fixes what's theirs and raises what isn't; and adds the check that would have caught it | The whole data story, and the four steps of *On Monday* |

**Where the film adopts, extends or differs.** It adopts data mesh's product owner (who answers for the product's value to its readers) without the rest of data mesh's architecture, which a newcomer doesn't need for this decision. It adopts RACI's one A, and uses the chart only for that: one person answers, and many do part of the work. It adopts Dekker's question (what happened, not who) and his reading of accountability as an account given. It extends them from practice: ownership needs authority to match (Ivy can hold a refresh, change the model with a review and tell the readers without asking first), and an owner says so first, before the fix. It differs from a common reading of "ownership" as doing every part yourself: Ivy doesn't own the sensors or the platform, and a fix that isn't hers is raised, not done.

**Practice.** A practitioner will recognise the Monday when every check passes and the numbers are wrong; an improvement upstream that nobody thought would change the data; a step written for the data as it was; the word that goes to the readers before the cause is known; the change that isn't yours to fix; and the review where the first question is what happened, not who.

## The look

*Ink line*, which the author chose on 17 November 2026 from four looks drawn for him: flat colour with one even dark line round every form, no shading and no blush. It is drawn with the people kit (`films/people-first/shared/src`, one name, `PK`), over the agent films' camera, shots and places. Nothing is watercolour, and nothing has film grain.

- **People, whole, in places.** Every chapter has people in it, standing on a floor, at different depths under one eye line, doing what the line says. Seven places: the bare hills, a nursery and the green hills in Kenya; the team's room, a teaching room, the campus and Helena's corner at the university. A standing person is drawn three-quarters on; someone who sits, kneels or bends is seen from the side. Hands hold, carry, give, take, write, rest or greet, and nobody waves an arm about. Each pose is taken up from the one before, and faces follow whoever speaks. The first university scene is tagged "The university, 2027 · imagined", and the last "The university, 2029 · imagined".
- **Words hold four things:** a place and a time (a card at the top left), a name (a tag over a head), something said (a bubble) or one line to keep (a tag). Nothing else is written.
- **Kenya's central highlands** are drawn in the same line: rolling hills of red soil, small fields to the ridges, tea, a plantation's straight rows, a few old trees, stumps and gullies on the near slope, a dry stream bed, a mountain far off; a group's nursery under a shade of poles and dry grass; and the same hills years later, with trees along the fields, grass over the gullies and water in the stream. The women wear plain clothes of the 1970s with headscarves tied at the nape. Maathai is drawn as a composite, never a likeness. Nothing is borrowed from a culture's own patterns or crafts: no cloth patterns, beads or carving.
- **Colours carry meaning,** as in every film of the module: blue for plans and rules (the product's promise, the timetable change, the one who is accountable, Dekker), orange for data (the product, the rooms' counts), green for something done or of value (each tree counted alive, what the trees give, every check passing, the right counts, safe to say it early), amber for a risk or a cost (the harm, the counts that dropped, the room that would be released, the gap between the parts). The hills, the leaves and the people wear none of them: the leaves are olive, never the green that marks a value.
- **Its own pieces,** drawn once in the film's own source: in `src/oo_kenya.js`, the hills, the nursery, the green hills, the people of the hills and what they hold (firewood, a water can, a tray, a seedling, coins), the row of young trees and the rain; in `src/oo_uni.js`, the team's room at each hour, a class in a teaching room and its sensor's count, the campus's passers-by, Helena's corner, the three callouts, the pinned sheets and the letters; in `src/oo_scenes.js`, the six chapters. ## Rigour to check before recording

The opening story's rows were checked on 10 November 2026 and haven't changed; the rows on the university were checked again on 17 November 2026, when the film was made again. The sources are listed below.

- **What rural women told Maathai.** The Green Belt Movement's own history: the movement began from "the needs of rural Kenyan women who reported that their streams were drying up, their food supply was less secure, and they had to walk further and further to get firewood". Maathai's Nobel Lecture (Oslo, 10 December 2004) names the needs "identified by rural women, namely lack of firewood, clean drinking water, balanced diets, shelter and income". The film says the women told her in "the hills of the central highlands": she grew up in Nyeri, in the central highlands, where her lecture remembers the forests cleared and the stream that dried up; the movement's women came from rural Kenya more widely, which the film's general "rural women" keeps (an assumption, in the film's assumptions file).
- **Maathai, a biologist.** A bachelor's degree in biology (1964), a master's in biological sciences (1966) and a doctorate in veterinary anatomy (1971), the first woman in East and Central Africa with a doctorate (Wikipedia, *Wangarĩ Maathai*). The film says "a biologist" and no title.
- **The cause.** The lecture: "forests being cleared and replaced by commercial plantations", and "the introduction of commercial farming, which replaced the growing of household food crops". Tea and coffee are the central highlands' best-known crops for sale, said in general words, with no number; the plantations of timber are the lecture's commercial plantations. "Each choice made sense to the people who made it" is the film's own line: farms, crops to sell and timber each met real needs, and the film blames nobody.
- **The soil and the springs.** Without trees the soil washes off the slopes (the Goldman Prize's account: her work set out "to help curb soil erosion"; secondary accounts of the streams near her home, drying or silted with run-off); her lecture's stream that "has dried up". The film says it in general words.
- **The women's work.** The lecture: "women are the primary caretakers, holding significant responsibility for tilling the land and feeding their families"; the movement's history: the walk for firewood "further and further"; Wikipedia's *Green Belt Movement*: with less firewood, mothers "could not cook their traditional foods". So the harm fell first on them, and on their children.
- **1977.** "Founded by Professor Wangari Maathai in 1977 under the auspices of the National Council of Women of Kenya (NCWK)" (the movement's history); the film says "with the National Council of Women of Kenya".

## The promise

Every line passes two tests: anyone who has been handed a backlog of data requests can follow it, and an experienced enterprise or data architect finds nothing wrong in it. A request matters when someone will decide something with its answer, in time. Traced up, a request reaches a decision, the capability it belongs to and a goal, or it reaches nothing. Traced down, a goal implies decisions, a decision needs questions answered, and a question needs a measure; a goal with no measure can't be steered. A measure is defined once, in writing, so that everyone who counts it gets the same number; it has a home on the map, the capability whose work it measures, and an owner, who says what it means and acts when it moves. What gets built is scoped by capability and domain, not by request. The opening story carries the idea, and says so: in Iran in the 1960s, the builders of a new qanat dug a trial well before they paid for the tunnel, because their question, how fast does the water come?, mattered only if its answer came before they chose whether to dig. The film gives every request the same test, a trial well before anything is built, and comes back to the well at the end to trace the builders' question the same way. **The questions that matter are the ones someone will act on, in time, traced to a goal, with a measure and an owner.**

## The story in one paragraph

In Iran in the 1960s, new qanats are still being dug: tunnels that bring water from under the mountains. In autumn, an expert walks the slopes looking for a damp patch, or greener plants, where water might be. A qanat ten kilometres long costs $13,500 to $34,000 to build, and until it's finished, no one can be sure it will flow all year; dug to a trickle, it would leave the fields dry and the money gone. So before they pay, the builders need to know whether there is water enough. Two diggers, the muqannis, sink a trial well, the earth coming up in leather buckets; at damp ground they scoop out a hollow and, for the next few days, dip the buckets in to measure how fast the water gathers. More than a trickle means real groundwater, and only then is the tunnel dug. The question was how fast the water comes; it mattered because the builders would act on the answer, by choosing whether to dig, and the trial well brought it in time. Their money went where the water was, and a qanat repaid them well: ten to twenty-five percent, in crops and water sold. Today, Tomás's note asks which questions matter. On his first day he was handed the reporting team's backlog, forty-two requests, most of them marked urgent; now there's a map, and a trial well for every request, before anything is built: who will decide what with the answer, and when? Traced up, the planners' request reaches a decision, a capability and a goal; a dashboard for the lobby, never out of date, reaches nothing, because no one decides anything with it. Half the backlog reaches a goal; the other half goes back with one question: what would you decide with it? Traced down, a goal needs decisions, a decision questions and a question a measure, and the outcome nobody could check gets one: interruptions per customer, per year. From the top and from the bottom, the lines meet at one decision. Keeping bills affordable has no measure for the people who pay; connecting solar has one, counted two ways, until Grace writes one definition down. Each measure gets a home on the map and an owner, and the twenty-one requests that matter need four data products, not twenty-one reports. Back at the trial well, the builders' question is traced the same way: a goal, water for the fields, all year; a decision, whether to dig the tunnel here, the builders' to make before they pay; the question; and a measure, how fast the hollow fills, day by day. Every part was there, so the answer reached the people who would act on it, in time. Grace confirms her measures, the third goes to the head of retail as a draft, and Tomás's note has its answer.

## Rigour to check before recording

- **Iran, the 1960s: the trial well.** The opening follows H. E. Wulff, "The Qanats of Iran", *Scientific American* 218 (4), April 1968, 94–105 (checked 23 November 2026), whose account the series' other films also draw on. Wulff, an engineer who taught in Iran, describes the qanats as they were still being built and kept in his day: "The methods used in Iran today are not greatly different from the system devised thousands of years ago", and construction costs "have risen in recent years". The surveyor examines the alluvial fans "generally during the fall, looking for traces of seepage to the surface or slight variations in the vegetation that may suggest the presence of water sources buried in the hillside. On locating a promising spot, he arranges for the digging of a trial well. Two diggers, called muqanni, take up this task. They set up a windlass at the surface to haul up the excavated material in leather buckets" and dig a vertical shaft. "When they arrive at a moist stratum, a potential aquifer, the diggers scoop out a cavity to its impermeable clay bottom, and for the next few days the leather buckets are dipped into the hole periodically to measure the rate of accumulation of water in it. If more than a trickle of water is flowing into the hole, the surveyor can conclude that he has tapped a genuine aquifer." The tunnel's excavation "begins at the downhill end after a trial well has successfully tapped the uphill water table": the film's "Only then is the tunnel dug", drawn from the foot of the slope up. "A qanat about six miles long costs between $13,500 and $34,000 to build, the cost varying with the nature of the terrain": the film says ten kilometres for six miles (9.7 km), and the dollars of 1968, as Wulff gives them. "Not until the qanat has been completed and has operated for some time is it possible to determine whether it will be a continuous 'runner' or a seasonal source that provides water only in the spring or after heavy rains. Because the initial investment in construction of a qanat is considerable, the owner and builders often resort to probing and laborious devices to enlarge its yield": the film's "no one can be sure it will flow all year", "all year, or only in spring?" and "the builders". And "the return on these investments in the value of crops and sale of water ranges from 10 to 25 percent, depending on the size of the qanat, the yield of water and the kind of crop": "ten to twenty-five percent, in crops and water sold". The voice says "muqannis" for more than one, as English makes a plural, and the voice reads it "moo-kah-neez".
- **What's illustrative.** "Dug to a trickle, it would leave the fields dry, and the money gone" is the film's plain reading of a qanat whose yield is too small, which Wulff's owners and builders worked to avoid. The expert's walk, the two builders and their place by the slope, the third digger in the shaft, the three days of the trial (Wulff's "next few days") and their bars are illustrative, and so is the section: the slope, the village, its fields and the distances are drawn, not surveyed. The builders' chain in chapter 10 is the film's own reading of their choice, in its own terms: the goal "water for the fields, all year", the decision "dig the tunnel here, or not", with who and when, the question and the measure "how fast the hollow fills, day by day". "A trial well for every request" is the film's own name for its test. No sound of Iran is borrowed (`source/tools/score.py`).
- **Goal, decision, question, measure.** Goal–Question–Metric (Victor Basili and colleagues; Basili, Caldiera and Rombach, 1994) works down from a goal to the questions that say whether it's being met and the metrics that answer them. It began with Victor Basili and David Weiss's method for collecting software engineering data at NASA Goddard's Software Engineering Laboratory ("A Methodology for Collecting Valid Software Engineering Data", *IEEE Transactions on Software Engineering* SE-10 (6), 1984, 728–738), so the voice says "a method from the 1980s" (checked 17 November 2026). Chapter 5 names it once, in the voice and on screen. The film puts a decision between the goal and the question, because the test for a request is a decision: who will decide what with the answer, and when. Starting from the decision a piece of data serves is common advice in data strategy; the film doesn't name a method for it. Tracing both ways builds on the series' insights 2 (motivation is written down) and 3 (capabilities are the stable spine).
- **The glyphs.** ArchiMate 3.2 has goals and outcomes among its motivation elements, and a stakeholder (the regulator), but no element for a decision, a question or a measure. The film draws its own beside ArchiMate's: a diamond for a decision, as flowcharts and BPMN's gateways draw one; a speech bubble for a question; a dial for a measure; and a hexagon for a data product.
- **SAIFI** (the System Average Interruption Frequency Index, defined in IEEE 1366) is the number of customer interruptions divided by the number of customers served, usually over a year, counting sustained interruptions (longer than five minutes in IEEE 1366; other regulators set other thresholds). Utilities compute it for the whole network and for each feeder, line by line, to find the lines that fail most often. The voice says "interruptions per customer, per year"; "SAIFI" is shown, not spoken.
- **The lab's metric tree** (*Trace it down*). A metric tree, also called a KPI or driver tree, breaks a measure into the measures that make it. IEEE 1366 defines SAIDI, the minutes without power per customer served, usually over a year, and CAIDI, the average minutes per customer interruption, as SAIDI divided by SAIFI, so SAIDI = SAIFI × CAIDI: the lab's first formula. Splitting an interruption's minutes into finding and reaching the fault, then restoring power, is the lab's own simplification of how utilities break down restoration time (reporting, travel, finding the fault, switching and repair). Trees growing into overhead lines are a common cause of interruptions, which is why utilities trim them; how much trimming lowers a line's interruptions is a relation to test with data, not a formula, so the lab draws it dotted. Regulators in many markets review SAIDI and SAIFI, and in some they set what a network may earn; the lab's regulator does. That nobody at the utility asked about minutes per interruption is fictional and plausible. Offering a measure nobody has asked for, as a question with the decision it would serve, to the person who owns that work, follows Rodrigo's advice (17 November 2026): a data team suggests measures from what it knows of its sector before anyone asks, and builds them only on a yes (checked 17 November 2026).
- **Bills and hardship.** The network charge, the part of a household bill that pays for the poles and wires, is regulated in many markets; "no higher in real terms in 2030" is the outcome from *Why it moves*. Help for households falling behind (a hardship programme) is common, and in some markets required of retailers; the film's "hardship register" is retail's list of the customers already on it. The gap the chapter is about, that it counts only those already getting help, is fictional and plausible.
- **Thirty-four days** is the figure from *What it must be able to do*: 26 working days assessing, 5 approving and 3 fitting the meter. Here, the first ten of those days are spent waiting for a complete application, so the network report, from a complete application to approval, counts twenty-one. Grace's definition answers the two questions *Why it moves* left open: connected, from when (from a complete application to the new meter), and what counts as a working day (Monday to Friday, not public holidays). Counted that way, the same connection takes twenty-four days, and both reports agree. The ten-day outcome is the one from *Why it moves*.
- **Homes and owners.** Grace owns the network's capabilities, and the head of retail selling energy and serving customers, as in *What it must be able to do*. Giving a measure a home under the capability whose work it measures, and that capability's owner, is the series' own simplification of what capability-based planning and data governance both ask for: someone who says what a number means and acts on it. The series' insight 12: a model is true when the people who own it confirm it.
- **Data products** follow data mesh (Zhamak Dehghani, 2019, and her book of 2022): data owned by the domain it comes from and served as a product, with an owner, for many uses. The wall is the one from *Who does it, and where meaning changes*: in many markets, a network business and a retailer must keep customer information apart, even inside one group. The four products, and which request each answers, are the film's own plausible grouping; the regulator's three requests go where their data lives.

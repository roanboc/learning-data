## The promise

Every line passes two tests: anyone who has filled in a form can follow it, and a privacy lawyer, a records manager and an HR lead find nothing wrong in it. In Oregon in 1986, public clinics tested people for HIV under their names, and many of those most at risk stayed away, afraid a positive result with their name on it would get out and cost them their job, their home or their health insurance. In December, Oregon's health officials let people be tested under a number instead: the test needed a sample and a way to give each person their result, and a number did both. In the four months after, the gay and bisexual men tested rose from 42 a month to 108, and more than twice as many people with the virus were found. The film's idea follows: ask what each piece of personal data is for, which reason allows it, and keep only what that reason needs.

## The story in one paragraph

Oregon, 1986. A new blood test can tell people whether they have HIV, and the clinics keep each name with its result, privately. But a private list is still a list of names, and many of the people most at risk stay away, so people with the virus don't know it and can't protect the people they love. In December, Oregon's health officials let people choose their name or a number, and a number does all the test needs. More come, and more than twice as many people with the virus are found. Today, at the university, Ingrid Holm, who owns HR's data, is getting ready for next year's tutors; Linh Nguyen's last tutoring contract ends on 20 November. Ingrid asks of every piece HR holds about a tutor what it is really for. Six lawful reasons, of which agreeing is one, cover all but two pieces: a marital status and a photo, asked for by the 2023 application form and used by no step. The new form drops them, asks for date of birth and bank details only after the offer, says plainly what HR holds, why and for how long, and keeps one real choice: news of tutoring work. When Planning asks for the tutors' records, a short privacy check settles for counts with no names, and small units counted together. Every piece HR holds about Linh now has a reason it can say, so when she asks, Ingrid can answer.

## The reference, and practice

The series follows the principles most privacy laws share (the series' *Decided* rows, 5 November 2026). The film names one law once, in plain words, and teaches no country's law.

| Body of knowledge | What it adds | Here |
|---|---|---|
| The OECD's privacy guidelines (1980, revised 2013) | The oldest common source of the principles: collection limited to what's needed, the purpose said at collection, use limited to that purpose or one compatible with it | The film's spine, unnamed |
| Europe's GDPR (Regulation 2016/679) | Purpose limitation and data minimisation (Article 5); six lawful bases (Article 6), consent's conditions (Article 7), what people are told (Articles 13 and 14), a new purpose's compatibility (Article 6(4)), an impact assessment for high-risk uses, asking the people where it fits (Article 35) | Named once, in *Six reasons*, as the best known |
| The European Data Protection Board's guidelines on consent (05/2020) | Consent is rarely free between employer and employee; it is valid only where a no has no cost | *A yes that means it* |
| Privacy by design (Ann Cavoukian, 2009; GDPR Article 25) and ISO/IEC 27701 | Build the least into the design: the form asks less, by default | *As little as needed*; named here, not in the film |

Where the film differs: it says "reasons" for lawful bases and "a privacy check" for the screening that comes before an impact assessment, and gives the six bases in a newcomer's words ("She agreed", "A contract with her", "A law that requires it", "A public task", "To protect someone's life", "a fair interest, weighed against hers"). It doesn't teach special categories of data, transfers or the legal tests in full.

Practice the film carries (assumed; the film's assumptions list, kept with the module's working files): a reason is one you could tell the person in a sentence; ask for a piece when its reason starts, not before; consent only for what is truly optional, with the no as easy as the yes; tell people plainly what is held, why and for how long; before a new use, a short check the owner signs and data governance keeps; counts instead of names, with small groups counted together.

## Rigour

Checked on 5 November 2026; the sources are listed below. Items not read at their source are marked **to check**; none changes a line of the film.

- **The Oregon trial.** Fehrs and others, *The Lancet*, 13 August 1988 (vol. 2, pp. 379 to 382), abstract read in Europe PMC: "Before December, 1986, all public human immunodeficiency virus (HIV) testing in Oregon was done confidentially (using names). In December, clients were offered the option of either anonymous or confidential services." "The number of gay clients who had tests increased from a mean of 42 per month during the 4 months before anonymity was available to 108 per month during the 4 months after, whereas, at public sites in Colorado or California and private sites in Oregon, the number of gay clients tested did not increase." "Twice as many seropositive persons were identified during the 3 1/2 months after anonymity became available (n = 85) as in the 3 1/2 months before (n = 36)." Its conclusion: anonymous testing "drew gay men who had not sought services under a confidential testing system". The film says "gay and bisexual men" because the study's group is "homosexual/bisexual (gay) men".
- **"Confidential" and "private".** In the study, confidential testing means with names, kept private; the film says "keep both private" and "A private list is still a list of names", which is its point: a private list can still get out.
- **Who made the change.** The abstract says clients "were offered the option"; the study's affiliation is the Oregon State Health Division and the federal Centers for Disease Control, so the film says "Oregon's health officials". It doesn't claim who proposed it.
- **The fear.** Not from the study's abstract. In June 1988 the presidential commission's chair, James Watkins, called discrimination against people with HIV "the rule, not the exception", and said testing and finding partners couldn't succeed without protection from it; the commission's March 1988 hearing heard of discrimination at work, in housing and in schools (the commission's report, 24 June 1988; summary read in Wikipedia; the full report, NCJRS 112422, **to check** for the exact passage). Congress's Office of Technology Assessment found in its February 1988 survey that most commercial health insurers screened individual applicants for HIV, and nearly all refused cover to people with AIDS (*AIDS and Health Insurance: An OTA Survey*, read through the *Milbank Quarterly*, 1990; the paper itself **to check**). So "It could cost them their job, their home or their health insurance" is the fear of the time, said as a fear.
- **Care in 1986.** The first drug for HIV, zidovudine (AZT), was approved in the United States on 20 March 1987, inside the four months after (US Department of Health and Human Services, 20 March 1987). The film says "look after their health" and "protect the people they loved", never that a cure or a treatment was there.
- **Later evidence.** Later studies found that reporting results by name didn't always keep people away: in six states in the 1990s, tests at public sites didn't fall after name reporting began (Nakashima and others, *JAMA*, 1998), and Seattle's records from 1986 to 1992 left it unclear whether anonymity brought more people at risk (CDC, *MMWR*). The film tells what happened in Oregon in those months, and draws no general law from it.
- **"A positive result, one that finds the virus".** The test of 1986 found antibodies to the virus; "finds the virus" is the plain sense a viewer needs, and the film never shows a result.
- **The six reasons.** The GDPR's Article 6(1): consent; a contract with the person, including steps at their request before one; a legal obligation; the vital interests of a person; a task in the public interest or in official authority; legitimate interests, unless the person's interests override them. The film's words: "She agreed", "A contract with her", "A law that requires it", "A public task, a duty to the public, such as teaching", "To protect someone's life", "a fair interest, weighed against hers". It doesn't say most laws name six: they don't all (the series' shared numbers, corrected on 5 November 2026).
- **Purpose and lawful reason are two questions.** Article 5(1)(b) (a purpose, said and kept to) and Article 6 (a lawful basis); the film keeps them apart: "A purpose says what a piece is for. The law also asks what allows it."
- **Consent at work.** The EDPB's guidelines on consent (05/2020, section 3.1.1) say consent is rarely free between employer and employee, and is valid only where a refusal has no cost; Recital 43 says the same where there is a clear imbalance. Hence "A yes is a reason only where a no is a real choice", and the news question, whose no costs nothing, as the one real choice. Withdrawing consent must be as easy as giving it (Article 7(3)): "when she says no, it leaves her out".
- **The bundled line.** "I agree to the university using my personal data for HR purposes" fails three ways at once: it isn't specific, a no couldn't stop what a contract or a law needs, and it was a condition of applying (Article 7(4)). The film gives the one a newcomer can see: "If Linh had said no, HR would still have to keep her pay records".
- **As little as needed, when it's needed.** Article 5(1)(c) and the OECD's collection limitation principle; asking for bank details and date of birth only after the offer is common practice for the same reason (the UK regulator's recruitment guidance says the same in its own words, **to check** for the final text). Marital status and a photo invite unfair judgements and aren't needed to choose a tutor, so the film says only that "no step used" them.
- **The notice.** Articles 13 and 14 list what people are told when their data is collected; the film keeps three: what is held, why, and for how long.
- **The privacy check.** Its questions follow Article 6(4)'s test for a new purpose (the link to why the data was collected, the context, the kind of data, the possible consequences, the safeguards) and Article 35's impact assessment, which a use that decides about people needs, and which seeks the views of the people concerned "where appropriate" (Article 35(9)): "a deeper check, which asks the people it's about". Its answer "yes, if it's only counts" is the fit test's: counts with no names fit why the data is held, where the records themselves wouldn't.
- **Small units.** Counting a group under a threshold with another is common practice in publishing counts about people; five is the film's threshold, invented and believable.
- **The university's records and dates** are invented, from the series' shared numbers: Linh's contract T-26188 ends 20 November 2026 (*Modern data basics*); her yes of January 2023; the 2023 and 2027 forms; the deletion of marital status and photos on 6 November 2026; Clara's request of 9 November and Ingrid's yes of 12 November 2026; her no of 24 November 2026, the day she writes to the university (*A person asks*). Linh's wish of 2022 not to be asked for donations is *For this purpose only*'s.
- **Date of birth "for her pension".** In many countries payroll needs a person's date of birth by law for tax or pension rules; the university's country is never named, so the film says only "a law".
- **Release status.** The film names no product or feature, so it says no release status.

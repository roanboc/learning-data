## The promise

Every line passes two tests: someone who has never run a data platform can follow it, and a data architect or a data engineer finds nothing wrong in it. *Moving is easy, agreeing is the work* showed the ways data moves between applications, and the contract at each interface. This film asks how fresh each kind of data must be, and how often it's read, and shows that the answers decide how it moves and where it's kept: hot data, needed within seconds or minutes and read all the time, moves as a stream and stays small and close to where it's used; warm data is worked out on a schedule, fresh to the hour or the day; cold data is rarely read and kept cheaply, until the date it may be deleted. One piece of data can cool as it ages. The film defines the three words for the module; the module's other films use them as defined. It is light on purpose, as the module is: it names the idea, shows it once in the tools, gives the architectural advice (ask the two questions, keep hot only what needs the minute, let most data be warm, let what's kept for years sleep cheaply, and delete it on its date) and hands over to the official docs, linked on its page.

**Takeaway:** "Freshness decides how data moves; reading decides where it lives."

## The story in one paragraph

In February 2008, a vault opens deep in a frozen mountain on Svalbard, Norway's Arctic islands. At minus 18 °C, it keeps copies of seeds from gene banks around the world: the seeds of crops and their wild relatives, which breeders use to grow crops that stand drought and disease. Near Aleppo, in Syria, a gene bank keeps 141,052 samples of wheat, barley, lentils and chickpeas from the world's dry lands, and from 2008 it sends copies of most of them to the vault. A gene bank can be lost, to war, flood or a power cut, and a variety lost is lost for good. In 2012, war comes close to Aleppo; most of the bank's keepers have to leave, and it can no longer send seeds out, so breeders can't get the seeds they rely on and every variety is at risk. In 2015 the keepers do what no one had done before and ask the vault for their copies: 38,073 samples come out of the mountain and are sown that November in Lebanon and Morocco. By 2020 nothing is left of the collection in Aleppo, yet no more than 1,657 of its samples were lost, and breeders can order its seeds again. Seeds in use, kept close and ready; their copies, frozen far away, until the day they're needed. Data has a temperature too. At the university, until September 2026 no one had asked how fresh each kind of data must be, or how often it's read: contract changes came in once a night, and 23 tutors were paid two weeks late; old pay records sat in the costliest storage, never deleted. In September, Noor, the data architect, asks each data owner two questions, and each answer goes into the data's contract. Contracts become hot: the HR system pushes each change as it's made, through Zerobus Ingest, and a pipeline keeps a streaming table of every contract as it stands, so the claims app sees each change within a minute; this Monday, eleven contracts changed in the morning, and every claim against them was approved by noon. What each faculty has spent against its budget becomes warm: a materialized view, a dbt model Jun wrote, worked out each night at two from the finance system's ledger, read in place, and ready to read each morning. Pay records become cold: a year after it's written, each file moves to the cloud's archive tier; queries for recent pay still run, and an archived year takes hours to bring back, fine for an audit; when a year's records turn seven they're deleted, and in October the oldest were. One claim, Linh's, cools as it ages: hot on Monday morning, warm once it's paid, cold a year from now. On Svalbard the vault stays cold and still; in Lebanon and Morocco the bank sends out its seeds again. Freshness decides how data moves; reading decides where it lives. Storage has had tiers for years: there's no wheel to reinvent; start from the official docs, then investigate, adapt, implement, iterate.

## The reference, and practice

The module's reference is DAMA-DMBOK as the map and the data engineering lifecycle of *Fundamentals of Data Engineering* as the spine (`films/data-that-moves/proposal.md`). This film sits in DAMA-DMBOK's *data storage and operations* and in the lifecycle's *storage* stage. No single standard names data temperature; the film names none of the bodies of knowledge, and teaches what they share.

| Body of knowledge | What it adds | Where the film uses it |
|---|---|---|
| Reis and Housley, *Fundamentals of Data Engineering* (2022), *Storage* | Data sorted by how often it's read, hot, lukewarm and cold, with storage chosen to match; streams for what must be fresh, batches for what can wait; storage costs that fall with how rarely data is read | *Two questions*: the three temperatures and what each asks of storage (not named) |
| DAMA-DMBOK2 (2017), *Data storage and operations* | Retention, archiving and purging as part of running data: each kind of data kept as long as it's needed, then removed | *Cold*: archived after a year, deleted at seven (not named) |
| The cloud's storage tiers (Azure Storage, *Access tiers for blob data*, updated 3 November 2026) | Hot, cool, cold and archive: the less often data is read, the cheaper it is to keep and the dearer to read; the archive tier is offline, its data read only after it's brought back, which can take up to 15 hours | *Cold*; "Storage has had tiers for years: there's no wheel to reinvent" |
| The Open Data Contract Standard (Bitol), *Service-Level Agreement* | A contract's service levels: `latency`, `frequency`, `retention`, `timeOfAvailability`, each with a driver (regulatory, analytics, operational). Every contract in the example project is warm: built each night, ready by 7 a.m. | *Two questions*: "Each answer goes into the data's contract: how fresh, and how long it's kept" (the example's `service_levels.yaml`) |
| Lakeflow Spark Declarative Pipelines, Zerobus Ingest, materialized views and archival support, Databricks' docs (read 5 November 2026) | A system pushes each change straight into a table; a pipeline applies changes to a streaming table in order (AUTO CDC); a materialized view works out an answer on a schedule and keeps it ready; a table can be told which of its files the cloud has archived | *Hot*, *Warm*, *Cold* |

**Where the film adopts, extends or differs.** It adopts what the references share: decide how fresh data must be and how often it's read, then choose how it moves and where it's kept; keep what's rarely read cheaply; keep nothing past its date. It extends them with what the tools now do: a stream that needs no message bus to run, a view on a schedule written and tested as a dbt model, and a table that knows which of its files are archived. It differs from a storage course in where it starts: not with the tiers, but with two questions asked of each data owner, and the answers written into the data's contract.

**Practice, from the author (the module's proposal):** "The temperature of data decides how it moves and where it's kept." His advice, as the module records it: set each kind of data's temperature from how fresh it must be and how often it's read: keep hot data small and close, warm data refreshed on a schedule, and cold data cheap, until the date it may be deleted. A practitioner will recognise the before: everything loaded once a night whatever it's for, a report that reads the source system at every look, and years of records in the costliest storage, never deleted. Hot is the expensive one: something is always running, so keep hot only what's needed within minutes. Most data is warm, and should stay so. Tools show, they don't lead; and the module stays light on purpose, with the depth in the official docs.

## What the example project has

The film adds `examples/hot-warm-cold/` to the example project (`films/analytics-engineering/project/`), for Databricks only and not run by CI, as `examples/agents-on-databricks/` is:

- **Hot,** `contracts_as_they_stand.sql`: the pipeline, in SQL, that takes in each change the HR system pushes into the landing table `contract_changes` (through Zerobus Ingest) and applies it with AUTO CDC to a streaming table of every contract as it stands, one row per contract (SCD type 1; the HR system and the project's snapshot keep the history). It runs in continuous mode. Owner: HR (Ingrid Holm); no pay or rate of pay travels in it.
- **Warm,** `spending_by_faculty.sql` and `spending_by_faculty.yml`: Jun's dbt model, a materialized view of what each faculty has spent this year against its budget, read from the finance system's ledger in place through Lakehouse Federation, on a schedule of 02:00, Sydney time (`schedule={'cron': '0 0 2 * * ?', 'time_zone_value': 'Australia/Sydney'}`), with its description and tests.
- **Cold,** `pay_records_archive.sql`: the pay records' table told when its files reach the archive tier (`delta.timeUntilArchived = '365 days'`), the files an audit of 2021 needs back (`SHOW ARCHIVED FILES`), and the yearly deletion: the rule (7 years after each contract ends, then deleted), `DELETE`, `REORG TABLE ... APPLY (PURGE)` and `VACUUM`.
- **The contracts' answers,** `service_levels.yaml`: each answer to Noor's two questions as it goes into the data's contract, as the Open Data Contract Standard's service levels: the contracts' latency of a minute, the spending's daily frequency and 7 a.m. availability, and the pay records' retention of seven years.
- A `README.md`, and a line for the folder in the project's README.

## Sound

The module's palette for this film (`palettes.md`): a **pyrophone**, the fire organ of the 1870s, glass tubes made to sing by a flame burning inside them: a tube sounds while it's hot and falls silent as it cools, which is this film's idea. Played plain and steady, no flicker: a tube's note swells in slowly as its air heats (no click, no chiff), holds at an even level, and fades as it cools; its tone is the tube's harmonics, falling off fast, a narrow breath of air round the note, and under it the soft, even hush of the flame at the tube's foot, which keeps it apart from the site's organs, flutes and glass harmonica. The high tubes, E 4 to E 5, carry the tune. Its partner, the thread's choice: **the pyrophone's own low tubes**, E 2 to B 3, rounder and slower to heat, rather than a second colour, since every colour the site lacks either belongs to another culture or sounds like one it has. Both are made in `source/tools/score.py`. E major for the data kept as it's used and for what got better; C-sharp minor for the problem (the bank lost, the tutors paid late, the records never deleted). Just intonation from E, so E, A and B major and C-sharp and G-sharp minor are pure. The mark: a note that sounds while its tube is hot, softer as it cools, and silent when cold.

- **Music under the opening story,** then only at a chapter's turn, under a moment on screen or in a wordless pause: the mark at "minus eighteen", at the copies frozen far away and at the title card; three tubes for the three temperatures (hot the high E, warm the B under it, cold the E an octave down), each sounded alone where its kind of data is named or moves (Noor's three bands, the streaming table, the materialized view, the seven years, Linh's claim cooling, the close); a chord at Rohan's morning, at "they sleep" and at the end card.
- **No urgent pulse** (`film.json` lists no chapter): the tutors paid late are told calmly.
- **Effects,** one at a time, each tied to something on screen, soft and below about 2.5 kHz: a soft knock as the claims app comes in, soft taps as claims are approved and as the four verbs come in, a breath of air as the oldest records are deleted, a paper's turn, a lock as the pay records' table is shown and as *Deleting well* is named.

## The promise

Every line passes two tests: anyone who has been handed a backlog of data requests can follow it, and an experienced enterprise or data architect finds nothing wrong in it. A request matters when someone will decide something with its answer, in time. Traced up, a request reaches a decision, the capability it belongs to and a goal, or it reaches nothing. Traced down, a goal implies decisions, a decision needs questions answered, and a question needs a measure; a goal with no measure can't be steered. A measure is defined once, in writing, so that everyone who counts it gets the same number; it has a home on the map, the capability whose work it measures, and an owner, who says what it means and acts when it moves. What gets built is scoped by capability and domain, not by request. **The questions that matter are the ones someone will act on, in time, traced to a goal, with a measure and an owner.**

## The story in one paragraph

On a January morning in 1997, on the coast of Kerala, sardine boats head for beach markets that close by eight, and each can reach only one. At Badagara, eleven crews find no buyers and their catch goes back into the sea, while twenty-seven buyers within fifteen kilometres leave with no fish. Within weeks, mobile phones reach the coast, and a skipper at sea calls buyers in several markets, agrees a price, and only then chooses where to land; as the phones spread, the waste stops and prices along the coast even out. The question never changed; its answer started arriving in time. Today, Tomás's note asks which questions matter. On his first day he was handed the reporting team's backlog, forty-two requests, most of them marked urgent; now there's a map, and one test: who will decide what with the answer, and when? Traced up, the planners' request reaches a decision, a capability and a goal, and a dashboard for the lobby reaches nothing. Half the backlog reaches a goal; the other half goes back with one question: what would you decide with it? Traced down, a goal needs decisions, a decision questions and a question a measure, and the outcome nobody could check gets one: interruptions per customer, per year. From the top and from the bottom, the lines meet at one decision. Keeping bills affordable has no measure for the people who pay; connecting solar has one, counted two ways, until Grace writes one definition down. Each measure gets a home on the map and an owner, and the twenty-one requests that matter need four data products, not twenty-one reports. Grace confirms her measures, the third goes to the head of retail as a draft, and Tomás's note has its answer.

## Labs (candidates)

1. **Trace it up.** Twelve requests, each with who asked for it; trace each to a decision, the capability it belongs to and a goal, or to nothing, and write the question that goes back with each one that reaches nothing.
2. **Trace it down.** One goal; write the decisions it implies, the question each needs answered, and the measure each question needs.
3. **Check the outcome.** Five outcomes, some that can be checked and some that can't; rewrite each one that can't, with a measure.
4. **Define it once.** Two reports that disagree about one measure; find where each starts and stops its clock, and write one definition: from, to, what counts, and its owner.
5. **Give it a home.** Six measures and a capability map; hang each under the capability whose work it measures, and name its owner.
6. **Scope the products.** Fifteen requests that reach a decision, from both sides of a wall; group them into data products by capability and domain, and give each one owner.

## Rigour to check before recording

- **Kerala, 1997.** The opening follows Robert Jensen, "The Digital Provide: Information (Technology), Market Performance, and Welfare in the South Indian Fisheries Sector", *The Quarterly Journal of Economics* 122 (3), 2007, 879–924. The beach markets ran from about five to eight in the morning, and a boat could typically reach only one in a day. His Table I gives the prices between 7:45 and 8:00 a.m. on Tuesday 14 January 1997 at fifteen markets of north Kerala, on average about 15 km apart: Badagara's was zero, with eleven fishermen dumping their catch, while within fifteen kilometres twenty-seven buyers (fifteen at Chombala, twelve at Quilandi) were about to leave without buying any fish. A "fisherman" in the survey is a fishing unit, a boat and its crew, so the film says "crews". Mobile service reached Kozhikode on 29 January 1997, Kannur and Thalassery in July 1998, and Kasaragod and Kanhangad on 21 May 2000 (the districts on screen); towers near the shore gave service twenty to twenty-five kilometres out to sea, where most fishing is done. With phones, fishermen would typically call several buyers in different markets before deciding where to sell, agreeing a price while still at sea. The spread of prices across markets (their coefficient of variation) fell from 60–70% to 15% or less, the share of market pairs breaking the law of one price fell from 50–60% to almost none, and the waste, 5 to 8% of the daily catch, stopped; fishermen's profits rose 8% and the price to consumers fell 4%. By 2001, more than 60% of the boats used phones. Checked against the paper on 16 October 2026.
- **What's illustrative.** The skipper's morning in February 1997 and his three prices (Rs 6, 8 and 9.5 a kg) are illustrative, not from the survey. The price chart is drawn after Jensen's figure, not plotted from his data. The markets sit where the survey's distances put them along a drawn coast; the hills, the rivers and the towers' places are drawn, not surveyed. Srinivasan and Burrell (2013) revisited the story with their own fieldwork in Kerala and add nuance to it; the film keeps to what Jensen measured.
- **Goal, decision, question, measure.** Goal–Question–Metric (Victor Basili and colleagues; Basili, Caldiera and Rombach, 1994) works down from a goal to the questions that say whether it's being met and the metrics that answer them. The film puts a decision between the goal and the question, because the test for a request is a decision: who will decide what with the answer, and when. Starting from the decision a piece of data serves is common advice in data strategy; the film doesn't name a method for it. Tracing both ways builds on the series' insights 2 (motivation is written down) and 3 (capabilities are the stable spine).
- **The glyphs.** ArchiMate 3.2 has goals and outcomes among its motivation elements, and a stakeholder (the regulator), but no element for a decision, a question or a measure. The film draws its own beside ArchiMate's: a diamond for a decision, as flowcharts and BPMN's gateways draw one; a speech bubble for a question; a dial for a measure; and a hexagon for a data product.
- **SAIFI** (the System Average Interruption Frequency Index, defined in IEEE 1366) is the number of customer interruptions divided by the number of customers served, usually over a year, counting sustained interruptions (longer than five minutes in IEEE 1366; other regulators set other thresholds). Utilities compute it for the whole network and for each feeder, line by line, to find the lines that fail most often. The voice says "interruptions per customer, per year"; "SAIFI" is shown, not spoken.
- **Bills and hardship.** The network charge, the part of a household bill that pays for the poles and wires, is regulated in many markets; "no higher in real terms in 2030" is the outcome from *Why it moves*. Help for households falling behind (a hardship programme) is common, and in some markets required of retailers; the film's "hardship register" is retail's list of the customers already on it. The gap the chapter is about, that it counts only those already getting help, is fictional and plausible.
- **Thirty-four days** is the figure from *What it must be able to do*: 26 working days assessing, 5 approving and 3 fitting the meter. Here, the first ten of those days are spent waiting for a complete application, so the network report, from a complete application to approval, counts twenty-one. Grace's definition answers the two questions *Why it moves* left open: connected, from when (from a complete application to the new meter), and what counts as a working day (Monday to Friday, not public holidays). Counted that way, the same connection takes twenty-four days, and both reports agree. The ten-day outcome is the one from *Why it moves*.
- **Homes and owners.** Grace owns the network's capabilities, and the head of retail selling energy and serving customers, as in *What it must be able to do*. Giving a measure a home under the capability whose work it measures, and that capability's owner, is the series' own simplification of what capability-based planning and data governance both ask for: someone who says what a number means and acts on it. The series' insight 12: a model is true when the people who own it confirm it.
- **Data products** follow data mesh (Zhamak Dehghani, 2019, and her book of 2022): data owned by the domain it comes from and served as a product, with an owner, for many uses. The wall is the one from *Who does it, and where meaning changes*: in many markets, a network business and a retailer must keep customer information apart, even inside one group. The four products, and which request each answers, are the film's own plausible grouping; the regulator's three requests go where their data lives.

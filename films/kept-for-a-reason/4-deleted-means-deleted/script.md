## The promise

Every line passes two tests: anyone who has ever left a mailing list and kept getting its emails can follow it, and a data engineer who runs a lakehouse finds nothing wrong in it. In Kanesh, about 1900 BC, a paid debt's tablet could turn up years later and be used to claim the silver again, so the merchants broke a paid tablet, or, when it was out of reach, sealed a receipt that made it void wherever it turned up. A delete in a table is that receipt: the table says the row is gone, but its old files, the tables made from it, the tools it was copied to, the locked copies, the archive and what people type still hold it, each until its own end. The film follows one row, Linh's line on the tutors' mailing list, from the night it's deleted to the day the last copy goes, and gives every copy a date and a way to know it went. **Deleting is done when the last copy is gone.**

## The story in one paragraph

Kanesh, in Anatolia, about 1900 BC. Merchants from Ashur, weeks away by donkey caravan, sell tin and cloth there and lend each other silver; each loan is written on a clay tablet, sealed in an envelope of clay rolled with the witnesses' seals, and kept by the lender as the proof of the debt. Paying the debt doesn't make the tablet go away: it can sit on a shelf in Kanesh or in a house in Ashur for years, and if it turns up later it can be used to claim the silver again. So when a debt is paid, the lender hands the tablet back and it's broken; when it can't be handed back, because it's still in Ashur, the lender has a receipt written and sealed before witnesses: the debt is paid, and any tablet of it that turns up is void. At the university, Linh Nguyen, a tutor since 2023, asks on 24 November 2026 to come off the tutors' mailing list; the privacy office logs it as request 2026-117 and approves it. At one in the morning on Wednesday 25 November, the nightly job deletes her row. The table's history records it, like a receipt, but the table keeps its old versions, so the list as it was on the 24th still holds her; and on this table the delete only marks her row in a file that still holds it, so the job rewrites those files without her, and seven days later, on 2 December, its nightly `VACUUM` removes the old ones. Deleting her row alone wouldn't last: the list is rebuilt each semester from HR's records, so her no goes on her record there, and every table made from it follows; the send log, which only adds rows, gets its own delete. The job never names her: her numbers are in a restricted table, and its code says only "request 2026-117". Lineage and tags show where else she is: the mailing tool drops her at four that morning and its backups lose her within thirty days; the locked copies, which nobody can delete early, keep her row out of use until each is removed by itself, the last on 24 December, and a restore applies every answered request again before anyone reads it. Her pay records must stay seven years after each contract ends: their files sit on the storage's archive tier, offline, grouped by the year each contract ended, so each January the year whose time is up is brought back and deleted whole, unless a hold keeps part of it; hers go from January 2031 to January 2034. And copies hide in what people type: the query history keeps every query's text for a year, so nothing names her; agents' traces mask email addresses, and traces and the gateway's tables are deleted after thirty days; the ticket names no one. In Kanesh, a paid debt stayed paid because its tablet was broken, or void wherever it turned up; at the university, Linh stays off the list because every copy of her row is gone, or can never be used.

## The reference, and practice

The module's reference holds (its proposal, *The reference*): DAMA-DMBOK's data security area for what is protected and why, and the principles most privacy laws share, with Europe's GDPR named once as the best known. For this film's one question, when is deleted really deleted, the thread took these, named once here; the film names none of them on screen.

| Body of knowledge | What it adds | Where the film uses it |
|---|---|---|
| GDPR, Article 17 (the right to erasure) and Article 5(1)(e) (kept no longer than needed) | A person may ask for their data to be erased, and data is kept only as long as its purpose needs; both reach every copy the organisation holds | The whole film; the series names GDPR once, in *A reason to keep it* |
| ISO/IEC 27001:2022, Annex A control 8.10, *Information deletion* | Information in systems, devices and any other storage is deleted when no longer required: a control an auditor checks, not only a right a person uses | *Cold, until its day*, *Gone* |
| The UK regulator's approach to backups, "put beyond use" (as reported by The Register, 31 May 2018) | A backup that can't be changed at once may keep erased data for a while if nobody uses it, it's removed on a schedule, and the person is told | *Every copy*: the locked copies |
| Databricks, *Prepare your data for GDPR compliance* (Microsoft Learn, 9 October 2026), and its pages on `VACUUM`, deletion vectors and table history | Delete in the sources first, from a scheduled job that reads a table of deletion requests, then everything made from them; purge where deletion vectors are on; `VACUUM` to remove old files; history kept 30 days and data files 7 by default | *A delete writes a receipt*, *Delete where it starts* |
| dbt's docs on incremental models and snapshots | An incremental model only adds and changes the rows its filter brings; a snapshot can't be rebuilt: neither forgets on its own | *Delete where it starts* |

**Where the film differs, on purpose.** The laws speak of erasure; the platform's documents of deletes, purges and vacuums; the standards of deletion and media. The film puts them in one plain order, a receipt and then every copy broken or void, and says "delete" for all of it. It teaches no law's deadlines: the university's 30 days are its own (*A person asks*).

**Practice.** What practitioners recognise: a `DELETE` that "worked", while time travel, a restore or a clone still brings the row back; deletion vectors left unpurged; an incremental table or a snapshot that keeps a deleted person forever, because nothing downstream ever re-reads; a list rebuilt from a source that still says yes; exports and copies in tools nobody listed; backups nobody may touch; and personal data typed into queries, tickets, chats and prompts, where it stays far longer than the data itself. And what works: delete at the source first, from a job driven by a list of requests that names no one; give every kind of copy an end date, and check it; keep archives in a shape you can delete from, a year at a time; and keep personal data out of logs, or keep the logs short.

## Sound

- **The straw fiddle leads.** The European xylophone of the 1500s to the 1800s: loose wooden bars laid on ropes of straw, struck softly with small wooden beaters. No tubes under the bars, so nothing rings on: each note is low, dry and short, unlike the orchestra's xylophone, and its bright edge is rolled off below about 2.5 kHz. Each tablet set down in Kanesh is one note; on the platform, each copy of Linh's row is one note, and a copy that's gone never sounds again.
- **One low hummed voice holds.** Lips closed, one note at a time, no vibrato, its notes exactly as long as its breath: the witness to the receipt and the record, under the opening and at the end, and let go where the seven days end. It never holds F, so nothing it holds beats with the fiddle. Held notes at even levels (`_band`, `_held`), the room set by what it rings where the notes are. It took the place of a chamber organ on 10 November 2026: *From a branch to production*, in this module, leads with a chamber barrel organ, and no film of this module has a voice.

## Rigour to check before recording

Checked on 5 November 2026, and the cold chapter again on 10 November; the sources are listed below.

- **Kanesh and the trade.** Kültepe, ancient Kanesh, near Kayseri in central Türkiye, was "part of the network of trading settlements established in central Anatolia by merchants from Ashur", who traded "primarily tin and textiles" (the Metropolitan Museum of Art's text on its Old Assyrian tablets). "Weeks away by donkey caravan": the Met gives about six to eight weeks for a caravan from Ashur.
- **Loans on tablets, in envelopes.** Loans of silver (and of copper) were written on clay tablets, many kept inside clay envelopes on which the text was repeated and the seals of the parties and witnesses were rolled, "in order to verify … without tampering with the document inside" (the Met, case of a quittance for a loan in silver, 328902). The seal's band on screen is the film's own pattern, not a copy of any seal.
- **Kept by the lender, and the risk.** The debt-note was the creditor's proof, kept in his archive, in Kanesh or in Ashur; debt-notes "were returned or destroyed when the debt was paid", and when one could not be given back, the debtor received a quittance stating that if the debt-note should later turn up it was invalid (Klaas R. Veenhof, *The Archives of Old Assyrian Traders*, read through the search index's excerpts on 5 November 2026). "It can be used to claim the silver again, and the borrower pays twice" is the risk such a quittance answers; the film doesn't say any one case happened.
- **The receipt in Ashur.** The Met's quittance 66.245.18a records a payment of copper and that any future tablets claiming outstanding debt in this transaction will be void; on another, "because the tablet recording the original details of the loan was still in Ashur, this quittance was written to record completion of the transaction" (328903). The narration's "silver" is the commonest loan; the Met's example is copper.
- **"Broken."** Veenhof's "returned or destroyed"; the film shows a returned tablet broken, the plainest way a clay tablet is destroyed.
- **"More than twenty thousand tablets."** More than 23,000 tablets, from about seventy archives, most of them in the merchants' quarter, dating to roughly 1950 to 1840 BC (Livius, *Kaneš (Kültepe)*; Wikipedia, *Kültepe*, read 5 November 2026). "About 1900 BC" sits in that span.
- **Women in the trade.** Women of Ashur's merchant families made and sold textiles and took part in the trade; the narration says "every borrower" and doesn't make them all men.
- **Linh's dates and numbers.** From the module's `numbers.md`, agreed with *Forgetting a person*, *A person asks*, *Walls, doors and keys* and *How long, and then what*: her ask of 24 November 2026; request 2026-117; the nightly job at 01:00 on Wednesday 25 November, before the 02:00 load; her no on her record that morning; the mailing tool's sync at 04:00; the clean-up on Wednesday 2 December; the locked copies, made each night after the load and kept 30 days, the last holding her row made on 24 November and removed on Thursday 24 December; her pay records kept 7 years after each contract ends, T-23014 to T-26188, deleted each January after their year's 7 years have passed, from January 2031 to January 2034. "Tutored at the university since 2023": her first casual contract began on 20 February 2023 (`data/hr_system/contracts.csv`).
- **A delete writes a new version, and history records it.** In Delta Lake, every change makes a new table version, and `DESCRIBE HISTORY` lists each operation, who ran it and when; "By default, Delta Lake retains table history, including deleted records, for 30 days, and makes it available for time travel and rollbacks" (*Prepare your data for GDPR compliance*). The versions v51 to v54 are the ones *A person asks* shows as its proof.
- **Time travel.** `SELECT … TIMESTAMP AS OF '2026-11-24'` reads the table as it was; "To query a previous table version, you must retain *both* the log and the data files for that version"; time travel queries are blocked if they ask for a version older than the `deletedFileRetentionDuration` table property, 7 days by default, in recent runtimes (*Table history*). So on 26 November the query finds her; from 2 December it can't.
- **Deletion vectors and the purge.** "Deletion vectors mark rows as modified in metadata instead, and reads apply the deletion vector entries at query time"; "For tables with deletion vectors enabled, after deleting records, you must also run `REORG TABLE ... APPLY (PURGE)` to permanently delete underlying records"; "`APPLY (PURGE)` only rewrites files that contain soft-deleted data" (*Deletion vectors*; *REORG TABLE*). "To save time" is why deletion vectors exist: a delete need not rewrite whole files.
- **Seven days, and VACUUM.** "The retention window for the `VACUUM` command is determined by the `delta.deletedFileRetentionDuration` table property, which defaults to 7 days"; "The ability to query table versions older than the retention period is lost after running `VACUUM`" (*VACUUM*). The job runs `VACUUM` as its last step each night, so the files left at about 01:02 on 25 November have passed 7 days when it runs on 2 December. Predictive optimization also runs `VACUUM` on managed tables, at its own times; the job runs it so the date is on the record. Databricks "strongly recommends" keeping at least 7 days; the university keeps them, "so a mistake can be undone".
- **Delete where it starts.** "We recommend that you start GDPR and CCPA compliance by deleting data in the bronze layer first, driven by a scheduled job that queries a table of deletion requests. After data is deleted from the bronze layer, changes can be propagated to silver and gold layers"; and "you must also remember to delete data in upstream sources" (*Prepare your data for GDPR compliance*). Here the source keeps her records (HR must, for pay); what changes at the source is her wish, and the list, a mart, loses her row.
- **The list and the send log.** The list is a dbt model materialised as a table, rebuilt by `create or replace` (dbt-databricks); the send log is an incremental model: "On subsequent runs, dbt transforms only the rows in your source data that you tell dbt to filter for, inserting them into the target table" (dbt, *Incremental models*): nothing in a rebuild of the list removes a row from it. Snapshots behave the same way ("Snapshots can't be rebuilt"); the film doesn't show one.
- **Lineage and tags.** Unity Catalog records lineage between tables, down to the column, from the queries that build them; the `personal_data` classes are the module's (`numbers.md`, *Know what you hold*). The mailing tool is declared in the project as a dbt exposure.
- **The mailing tool.** Invented, as the university's; the 04:00 sync and the 30 days in its contract are the film's, and believable for a tool that processes contacts on the university's behalf.
- **The locked copies.** *Walls, doors and keys* (`numbers.md`): every night after the 02:00 load, a copy of production's tables goes to a place apart, with admins of its own, locked for 30 days, then removed by itself. "Out of use", with a known end and the person told, is the UK regulator's "put beyond use" approach to backups (as reported by The Register, 31 May 2018). "Applies every answered request again" is the deletion job run against the restored tables before anyone may read them.
- **The archive.** Azure Blob Storage's access tiers are hot, cool, cold and archive; "The archive tier is an offline tier for storing data that is rarely accessed"; "While a blob is in the archive tier, it can't be read or modified", and rehydrating it to an online tier can take up to 15 hours (*Access tiers*). Databricks' archival support says a `DELETE` that touches data in archived files fails until it's restored (*Archival support*): hence "until it's brought back". A lifecycle rule moves `pay_records`' files to the archive tier a year after they're written, as *Hot, warm, cold* shows the same records; grouping its files by the year each contract ended lets each January bring back and delete a year whole. The 2019 year and its hold are *How long, and then what*'s.
- **Query history.** `system.query.history` holds each statement's text, which by default only account admins and a named group can read, and "SQL statements can contain sensitive information such as … email addresses"; its free retention is 365 days (*Query history system table*; *System tables*). System tables are read-only.
- **Agents' traces and the gateway.** MLflow tracing "captures the inputs, outputs, and metadata associated with each intermediate step of a request"; masking applied "at client side before sending the span to the backend" means the sensitive data never leaves the application. The gateway's inference tables "Log requests and responses to Unity Catalog Delta tables", their `request` and `response` columns holding the raw payloads. The 30 days are the university's own rule (`numbers.md`).
- **The ticket, and what a repository keeps.** *A person asks*'s ticket is an issue that names no one. GitHub keeps an issue's earlier versions in its edit history; and "If you only rewrite your history and force push it, the commits with sensitive data may still be accessible elsewhere: In any clones or forks of your repository" (GitHub, *Removing sensitive data from a repository*).
- **No release status** is shown or said (the author's rule of 5 November 2026). Read for the record on 5 November 2026: Databricks' archival support is labelled Public Preview, `VACUUM`'s LITE mode Public Preview, and configurable retention for system tables Beta; the film names none of them.

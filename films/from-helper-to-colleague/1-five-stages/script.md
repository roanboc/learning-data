## The promise

Every line passes two tests: someone who has only heard of AI agents can follow it, and a platform lead, a data engineer or a risk specialist finds nothing wrong in it. The series takes Planning's agent from a helper on Jun's sign-in to a colleague with a place on the university's map; this film sets the road it travels. An agent's freedom is not a switch, off one day and fully on the next. It comes in five stages, from a junior assistant that only suggests to a team contributor with a mandate, each changing whose identity the agent uses, how much it may do and what the person does, each reached through a gate a person decides it has passed. The stage belongs to each kind of task, in each place, not to the agent: one agent is at four stages at once. After a serious incident, one kind of task goes down a stage, not the whole agent off. And under every stage, some things never change.

**Takeaway:** "Autonomy is earned one kind of task at a time."

## The story in one paragraph

New Zealand, in the 1980s: at fifteen, a young person could pass the driving tests and get a licence, and from that day drive alone, at any hour, with a car full of friends. New drivers that young crashed far more often than older ones, most of all at night and with friends in the car; many were badly hurt, and some were killed. The licence was a switch, and experience came only after it. On 1 August 1987 the country made the licence come in three stages: six months as a learner, driving only with a fully licensed driver beside you; then a test, and a restricted licence, alone but not between ten at night and five in the morning, and with no passengers unless a fully licensed driver sat beside you; eighteen months on, another test, and only then the full licence, the riskiest driving last. In the years that followed, serious car-crash injuries among 15 to 19 year olds fell by almost a quarter; not all of that was the stages, but when restricted drivers crashed, fewer of those crashes were at night or with passengers. It was the world's first licence in stages, and every American state has one now. At the university, Anil Kurian's workload agent tells Health's heads of school how many people teach in each school this term, and how many are casual. Given every right at once, it could change how those numbers are made, alone, at night, before it had shown it could, and the heads of school would plan the next semester on numbers no one had checked. So its freedom comes a stage at a time, for each kind of task: a word about staff at stage one, where HR decides; a new question at three; fixing a model at four; finding why the night build failed at five in test and four in production. One night in October, working alone in test, it changed a model that wasn't its own; that one task went down a stage, and earned its way back through the gate again, with its mandate signed on 30 October. Under every stage, a person owns it, and another person approves every change to production. Each stage is written on the university's map, beside its kind of task, and the films that follow take Planning's new agent along that road.

## The reference, and practice

The module's reference for autonomy is a scale defined by what the person does, adapted to data work; the film names it once, with Europe's AI law and the standard for managing AI, and teaches what they share in plain words.

**Where the film adopts, extends or differs.** It adopts the researchers' idea that autonomy is set by what the person does, and their order, from a person who does the work to a person who only watches. It extends the scale in two ways from practice: each stage also changes whose identity the agent uses and how much it may do, and the stage is set for each kind of task and each place, not for the agent, so one agent stands at several stages at once, and one task can go down a stage while the others stay. It differs at the top: the researchers' last level leaves the person an observer, but here a person always approves a change to production, so no stage leaves people only watching; the film says so, and strikes "Observer" on screen. The law and the standard are named once, for what they share with the stages: people who can understand, watch and stop the system, and a written answer to who is responsible for what. The film names no product: its stages hold whatever tools a team uses.

**Practice, from the author (the plan's insights):** the stages and their gates, from his own work (a junior assistant first, a team contributor later; conventions and the glossary written down before anything; a development space with samples and no sensitive data; the checks set before the agent starts; an owner who answers for it; a track record before any mandate); agents are never approvers, and an agent's review is only for reference; in test, an agent may do agreed tasks alone and report; automate deterministically first, so a plain job reruns a build and no agent does. A practitioner will recognise the day an assistant that only suggested began running queries for them, the first time one worked alone overnight, and the morning someone found it had touched what it shouldn't, and pulled that one task back while keeping its help on the rest.

### 2 · Five stages and their gates · 2:32–4:09

**Narration.** An agent's road, from a junior assistant to a team contributor, has five stages. Each stage changes three things: whose identity the agent uses, how much it may do, and what the person does. Stage one: it suggests. It runs nothing, and you accept or reject every line. Stage two: it acts for you, step by step, on your sign-in, and you approve each action. Stage three: it acts for you within limits, alone, with drafts only in a sandbox, and you check the result against the checks you set first. Stage four: a contributor that proposes, under its own identity. Its owner reviews every change. Stage five: a contributor with a mandate, written and signed by its owner. In test, it does agreed tasks alone, and reports; in production, it drafts the fix, and another person approves every change. And before each stage, a gate: what must be true before it moves up. The team's conventions and glossary, written down for it to read; a development space, with no sensitive data; its limits, and how it will be checked, set before it starts; a place on the map, with an owner and a role; and a track record, and a mandate its owner signs. A person decides when it has passed. Five stages, not a switch.

## Sound

**The palette, its own** (reserved for this film by the series' thread on 5 November 2026, after checking every `score.py` on main and on every branch; the series' `palettes.md`): **rototoms**, alone. A rototom is a small tuned drum with no shell, a single head on a frame whose note is set by turning the drum on its stand: turned one way, the head tightens and the note rises. Here they're struck softly with felt mallets, dry and short, never rolled, high in their range (E flat 4 to C 5, with a low D 4 heard only in the opening), so they never sound like the site's timpani or frame drum. Each strike is made in the film's own `tools/score.py` from the head's own modes (an ideal membrane's, 1, 1.594, 2.136, 2.296, 2.653 and 2.918 times its note, the lowest strongest and the rest dying fast) and the felt's soft thump, in a small dry room, low-passed so nothing rings above about 2.5 kHz. No partner: the four partners still free went to the series' other films, and every other one checked is on the site or sounds like one. Nothing trembles, rolls, slides or pulses.

## Rigour to check before recording

Checked on 5 November 2026; the sources are listed below.

- **At fifteen, before 1987.** New Zealand's minimum age for a learner licence was 15 until 1 August 2011, when it rose to 16 (RNZ, 1 August 2011, "Legal driving age raised to 16"; the Ministry of Transport's statistics on household travel say the same). Before 1 August 1987 a new driver went from the tests straight to a full licence, with no stage between: the 1987 system was "the world's first bona fide graduated licensing system" (*Transportation Research Circular 458*, 1996). "From that day, they could drive alone, at any hour, with a car full of friends" is the old licence in plain words: a full licence held no limits on night driving or passengers, the limits the new stages brought in (Begg and Stephenson, 2003).
- **The risk, and the harm.** Langley, Wagenaar and Begg (1996), from its abstract: "Young drivers have a disproportionately high risk of experiencing a road traffic crash", and the 1987 system "was designed to give young drivers (i.e. 15-24 years inclusive) experience in driving while being excluded from high risk driving situations"; the high-risk situations the stages excluded were night driving and carrying passengers (Begg and Stephenson, 2003: "an 18-month restricted license stage (with restrictions on night driving and carrying passengers)"). "Many were badly hurt, and some were killed" names no crash and no person; the evaluations count hospital admissions and deaths.
- **The three stages of 1987.** Begg and Stephenson (2003), from its abstract: "In New Zealand, on 1 August 1987, a three-stage graduated driver licensing (GDL) system that applied to all new drivers aged 15-24 years was introduced. The essential elements of GDL were a 6-month learner license (supervised driving) and an 18-month restricted license stage (with restrictions on night driving and carrying passengers). A blood alcohol limit of 0.03 mg% applied at both stages." Mayhew and Simpson's description of New Zealand's system in *Transportation Research Circular 458* (1996): the restricted phase "is 18 months in duration but can be reduced to nine months if an Advanced Driving Course is completed"; "No passengers are allowed unless the front seat occupant is over 20 and has had an unrestricted license for over 2 years"; "There is also a night curfew from 10 p.m. to 5 a.m."; and the full licence is the third phase. The film says "unless a fully licensed driver sat beside you" for the passenger rule, "eighteen months on" without the course that could shorten it, and leaves out the alcohol limit: the story needs night and passengers. After considering effects for older occupants we speculate that the effect is likely to be substantially less than 23%. An analysis of licensure data suggests that the reduction in crashes may, in large part, be attributable to an overall reduction in exposure."

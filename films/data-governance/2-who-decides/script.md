# Data governance · Who decides: script

*The script of the second film of the data governance series, *Governance you can use*, revised for the series' first pack, which shows no technical files (the plan in three packs, approved by the author on 23 October 2026). Drafted for the author's review on 27 October 2026, before anything is recorded, with its pictures as they're drawn in stills the same day: about six minutes, in ten chapters, in English, painted in watercolour. The narration lives in [`source/src/narration.js`](source/src/narration.js) and the pauses in [`source/src/breath.js`](source/src/breath.js); this page and those files say the same thing, and where they differ, the source wins. The title card follows chapter 1; the end card follows chapter 10. Made to read on a phone: on-screen text is at least 28 px in the frame, nothing sits under the captions, and no caption runs to three lines.*

## The promise

Every line passes two tests: anyone who has waited weeks for someone to decide can follow it, and an experienced data governance lead finds nothing wrong in it. The film is about decision rights. A register of names in a binder decides nothing; a right to decide is real when the work asks for it, at the moment it matters, and the person asked has time to answer. On a car maker's engine line in 1960s Japan, any worker could call for help with a cord or a button within reach: a lamp lit, the supervisor came at once, and the line stopped only if the problem couldn't be fixed before the job was done. At the university, the owners are already written down where the work is: the data team keeps a list of who they ask, area by area. Yusuf confirms each name with its office and fills in the register. Then Yusuf and Noor make the owner required: anything new that other teams will read has a sign-off sheet whose owner's line is never blank. And every change request gets a routing slip, filled in from the register, so Jun's change to how *award* maps to the government's definition reaches Mei by name. On the way, the film names three roles (owner, steward, custodian), says who decides what (each area its own meaning, governance a few shared rules, the council only conflicts and exceptions, and someone above it), and keeps every decision where the work is, in each area's own book. Its people side: an owner needs time, a scope she can hold, and someone to ask. **A decision right is real when the work asks for it.**

As the series' first pack does, the film shows the university's own papers, not files or tools: a binder, a register, a list on a board, a sign-off sheet, routing slips and log books. What each stands for in the example project, and the film that shows it, is under *Rigour*.

## The story in one paragraph

Japan, the 1960s. On a car maker's engine line, any worker who finds a problem pulls a cord or presses a button. A lamp lights on the andon, the board above the line, and the supervisor comes at once, to help; if the problem can't be fixed before the job is done, the line stops at the end of the job, and the team fixes it together. Today, Yusuf opens the binder he took off the shelf, *Who decides*. Its policy says every area of data has an owner and a steward; its register lists eight areas, with three owners and half the stewards "to be confirmed". So decisions are made in email, or not at all: how *award* maps to the government's definition has gone round a thread of 23 replies. The data team, though, wrote its owners down as it built, because it needed someone to ask: their list, pinned to their board, names who they ask about each area, and for *award*, Mei Tanaka, in the registrar's office. Yusuf takes each name to its office to confirm it, and asks for the names still missing, so every "TBC" in the register gives way to a name. On the way, he sorts out three roles: the owner, who answers for what the data means; the steward, who keeps it right day to day (Mei is both); and the custodian, Sam's platform team, who can change any table but doesn't decide what it means. Each area decides its own meaning; governance sets only a few rules every area shares; the council decides only conflicts and exceptions, by its next meeting, and if it can't agree, the deputy vice-chancellor decides. Yusuf and Noor make the owner required: the sign-off sheet for anything new that other teams will read never leaves the owner's line blank. Jun's new report for Planning comes back with it empty; Clara Jensen will answer for it, and signs. The council's oldest action, *name the data owners*, is closed. Then every change request gets a routing slip, filled in from the register, which Yusuf keeps: change a name there, and every slip follows. Jun's next request, mapping *award* to the government's definition, goes to Mei by name, then to Noor and Yusuf, without anyone remembering to. Her decision will be written where the work is, in the course area's decision book, as she already writes which records are the same learner in her log book. Mei didn't ask to be an owner; Yusuf gets her two hours a week from her manager, keeps her scope to *award*, and is there to ask. Then Mei opens Jun's request, and asks which *award* she owns: she has found it defined in five places, and they don't agree.

## What changed since the draft of 21 October

- **No files, screens of code or tools.** The data team's map of domains becomes their list on the board, *Who we ask*. Mei's file of identity decisions becomes her log book, *Same learner?*. The pull request and the check's message become a sign-off sheet, sent back with "No owner: add who answers for what it means." The list of who reviews what becomes a routing slip on every change request, filled in from the register. The decision logs become each area's decision book. Their files move to film 14, *Governed in dbt*.
- **"Area", not "domain".** "Every area of data, like students or courses", in the narration and on screen; the frameworks' word, *domain*, waits for later films.
- **The register is filled in on screen.** Each "TBC" gives way to a name: the owners from the data team's list, the stewards each office names. Yusuf keeps it, and a change to it reaches every slip: the admissions office names Leila Haddad as its steward.
- **The owner required, on paper.** Jun's new report, *Completions for Planning*, comes back with its owner's line empty. He asks Planning, and Clara Jensen signs.
- **About 40 seconds longer:** 704 words, at 123 words a minute; about 5:58 with the title and end cards.

## The look

*Watercolour*, the series' look since 17 October 2026, and the master data series' too, so the two look like one university.

- **Pencil and wash.** Every place is painted on paper: a pencil line, paint gathering in the paper's grain, soft light from a window. People are drawn the way the master data films draw them, with a soft wash of colour behind each one.
- **Only the university's places,** most of them the series' shared places, painted once: Yusuf's office, the newest DMBOK standing on his desk, and his desk from above; the floor of offices behind glass; the data team's room and their board; Planning's room; Mei's desk in the registrar's office, and her desk from above; Jun's desk from above; the university registrar's own office; Sam's desk on the platform team; and the stewards' meeting room, where the council meets. Each room keeps its wall colour.
- **Colour carries meaning.** Gold is governance's decisions and the owners named; red what nobody owns yet ("TBC", a line left empty, "SENT BACK"), and on the line a stopped line; green what's done ("SIGNED", "DONE"); grey what's left behind; cyan the technical side; amber a call, on the andon and on the routing slip. On the andon, amber calls and red stops, as on the first andon.
- **The past is painted the same way,** with the place and decade in the corner: a car maker's engine plant in the 1960s, a long hall under steel trusses and high windows, machines painted a pale green-grey, engine blocks in cast-iron grey on their stands, a concrete floor with painted lines, and the andon hanging above the line. Its people wear work caps and pale work jackets; the supervisor's cap has a stripe. A warm umber wash goes behind them. The series' own watercolour, with nothing borrowed from another culture's style.
- **Papers, not files.** People reach for a cord, walk to an office, read a register, sign a sheet, hold up a request. Screens show only an inbox, a glossary's term, a list of tables and a chart, in plain words; no code, file names or tools.

## Rigour to check before recording

The opening, the roles and the council were checked on 20 and 21 October 2026, the papers on 27 October 2026; the sources are listed below.

- **The andon, and the line in the opening.** Toyota's own 75-year history says andon "were installed in the Kamigo Plant in 1966", on its engine assembly line, lit "either manually or automatically" so that "a supervisor can immediately come to the source of the problem". Kamigo, an engine plant, produced its first engine in September 1965. So the film says "Japan, the 1960s" and shows an engine line. *Andon* is the Japanese word for a paper lantern. The film doesn't name the company: its people and its line are a composite.
- **Cord or button.** No source found dates Toyota's first pull cord. Takehiko Harada (*Management Lessons from Taiichi Ohno*, as quoted by Mark Graban, August 2026) places the first andon on an engine machining line at the Koromo plant (later Honsha), probably in the 1950s, and describes a call button that lit a yellow light, not a cord. Toyota's booklet of April 1992 (as quoted by Graban) describes "pulling on a rope overhead or by pushing a button"; the Lean Enterprise Institute's lexicon says the operator "pulls a rope or pushes a button to signal the supervisor"; Toyota's page today speaks of a "stop cord". The widely told account of Ohno's line (*The Machine That Changed the World*, 1990, read here only as a paraphrase) has a cord above every work station. So the narration says "a cord, or a button", and the picture shows both.
- **The line doesn't stop at once.** Toyota's 1992 booklet: the line stops when it reaches the next "fixed position", the end of one complete job. LEI: Toyota pioneered the fixed-position stop partly because people were reluctant to pull the cord if the whole line stopped at once. Harada's first andon worked the same way: if the team couldn't recover before the designated stopping point, the light turned red and the line stopped. Some accounts say "immediately" (the Kaizen Institute's paraphrase of Womack and others); the film follows Toyota's own description. The painted line at the end of each station stands for the fixed position.
- **Who comes.** Toyota's history and its 1992 booklet say the supervisor; Toyota's page today says "the person in charge"; lean writers often say the team leader. The film says the supervisor. "To help, not to blame": Graban describes the design as the supervisor coming quickly to work with the person, "not scold the person who identified it".
- **"Every worker could stop the line, and they did."** Womack, Jones and Roos's account (as paraphrased) says Ohno told workers to stop the line for any problem they couldn't fix, and that at first the line stopped all the time; a 2007 BBC report, as Graban relays it, counted about 2,000 pulls a week at a Toyota plant in Kentucky. The plan's line, "a right that comes with a cord gets used; one written in a manual doesn't", isn't said, because it isn't so simple: in 1931 Ford's workers had a switch or an overhead cord and were "expected" to stop the line, and in that 2007 report a new Ford truck plant pulled its cord twice a week, which the BBC put down to "generations of mistrust". The film's link says what the sources support: the right was used because it was within reach and brought someone who could decide. Other companies aren't named or compared.
- **Clothes and the hall, 1960s.** Work caps and pale work jackets, steel trusses and high windows, machines in pale green-grey. The stripe on the supervisor's cap is the film's own sign, to tell him apart: searched on 21 October 2026, no source shows rank marks on work caps of the time, so the film doesn't claim one. A real line had many more stations than eight.
- **Three roles.** The names vary, so the film says "whatever a policy calls them". St Mary's University's data governance policy (v2.1, December 2024) has a data trustee, a data owner "accountable for the fitness or purpose of a defined data area", a data steward "responsible for the definition and quality of defined dataset(s)", and a data custodian "responsible for the safe custody, storage and retention of data". Texas State University's UPPS 04.02.05 (effective 5 January 2024) has data owners, custodians and stewards, and defines a steward as "a data custodian" with planning and managing duties. DAMA-DMBOK2 (§ 1.3.5, *Types of Data Stewards*, as summarised on GCwiki) calls data owners business data stewards "who have approval authority for decisions about data within their domain", and the people who run systems technical data stewards. All three keep apart who is accountable for a set of data, who does its daily work, and who runs the systems it lives on, under different names; who writes a definition varies (St Mary's gives it to the steward, accountable to the owner). Mei holding both roles is the film's. In Spanish, owner is *dueño*, steward *responsable de datos* and custodian *custodio*, so the three never share a word (the series, 21 October 2026).
- **"Area", for a domain.** St Mary's speaks of "a defined data area"; DAMA and the series' other films say *domain*. Pack 1 says *area*, the plain word, for the same thing: the university's eight, as the data team's map of domains names them (person, student, course, organisation, admissions, staff, research and fees). Pack 2 draws them as domains on Noor's map.
- **Federated decisions.** Data governance's operating models are usually described as centralised, replicated or federated (DAMA-DMBOK2, chapter 3, as its commentators report it; the book wasn't read). The series' is federated, as *Who owns what* (*In the weeds of data crafting*) built it: each area decides its own meaning and publishes what others build on, and governance sets a few shared rules. The narration names two of them (one glossary, one way to classify data); the others in the plan, key sets and conventions, come in later films.
- **The council.** It meets monthly (*The facts the films share*), so its time limit is its next meeting. That it decides only conflicts and exceptions, and that the deputy vice-chancellor decides when it can't agree, are the series' design, written in *Who decides* for the first time; the next films keep it.
- **Papers, not files.** Pack 1 shows no technical file or tool (the author's direction of 21 October 2026, in the plan approved on 23 October). Each paper stands for something in the example project, as pull request #53 holds it (branch `claude/project-thread-ltr3tq`, at 8e70ba2, checked again on 27 October 2026), and film 14, *Governed in dbt*, shows the files:

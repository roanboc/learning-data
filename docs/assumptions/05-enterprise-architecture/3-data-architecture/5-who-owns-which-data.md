| 1 | Which sound, when no instrument the site lacks fits? | Rototoms, small tuned drums struck softly with felt mallets, with two timpani tuned to B flat and F, struck softly and left to ring: two instruments the site has, which no film pairs, and which no film of this topic plays (*Five stages, not a switch* has rototoms alone, high and dry; *The same problem everywhere* and *Walls, doors and keys* have timpani, under a chamber organ and a douçaine). The palettes register allows a pairing where nothing new fits (12 November 2026); drums are the one family this topic hadn't played. Kept apart from those films: the drums lower and warmer, never turned up a step; the timpani only where something isn't solved in time and goes up. Reserved in `/mnt/project-files/enterprise-architecture/new-architecture-series/palettes.md`. The question whether pairings are fine is already on the author's checklist. | The soundtrack |
| 2 | Is the warnings' design told as what happened? | No: as it was designed, "would", as the series decided on 12 November. Medina (2006, p. 588) describes the design: the warning first to the factory's interventor, with a time frame, then the CORFO sector committee; in practice, the results went to CORFO's telex room without that step (p. 592). How little of the design was built comes in the series' last film. | Chapters 1 and 8 |
| 3 | "The factory's own manager"? | The interventor: the official the government appointed to run each nationalised factory, in place of its previous managers (Medina 2006, p. 580, fn 32). "Manager" is the plain word a newcomer knows; the film doesn't go into who appointed them. | Chapter 1 |
| 4 | "If every warning went to Santiago, a few people there would be buried in small problems, and factories would wait for orders"? | The film's reading of Beer's model, said as the reason for the design: Cyberstride sent up only what was important (Medina 2006, p. 588), the model let the lower levels behave autonomously and called the levels above only when needed (p. 585), and Beer's work rests on Ashby's law of requisite variety (p. 583). Medina doesn't say it in these words. | Chapter 1 |
| 5 | "Decentralising, with workers taking part, and not bureaucratic"? | Allende's insistence as Medina quotes it (2006, p. 572, from Beer's *Brain of the Firm*, 1981): "decentralising, worker-participative, and anti-bureaucratic", in plain words. | Chapter 1 |
| 6 | "The engineers chose each factory's time. Beer called it designing freedom"? | Medina (2006, p. 589): the engineers "determined the optimal amount of recovery time allotted to each firm", "a process Beer referred to as 'designing freedom'" (his 1973 Massey Lectures, *Designing Freedom*, 1974). "Freedom" is Beer's word; the film doesn't judge it. | Chapter 1 |
| 7 | "Almost complete control"? | Beer's own argument, said as his: "Each factory, Beer argued, would keep almost complete control of its work" (Medina 2006, p. 588). | Chapter 1 |
| 8 | Who owns a table by default, and why does it matter? | Whoever built it, as on many platforms: 300 of the utility's 412 tables name the data team. The film blames nobody: the engineers didn't see the table as theirs to tell, and the data team had no way to know. | Chapter 2 |
| 9 | Is hosting capacity's story plausible? | Yes, as a composite: networks publish hosting capacity for households and installers, its method depends on the network's limits (cable ratings, voltage), and a street without room limits how much a home may export. The May change (older cables carry less), the four months, the ninety families and the next method (home batteries counted) are invented. | Chapters 2 and 8 |
| 10 | Who decides who owns a table? | The owners themselves: Inés brings them a rule and they agree it, and it goes on the map with its date (3 October, invented). Data governance doesn't decide it for them, and nobody approves it in a pull request, as the site's rules ask. | Chapter 3 |
| 11 | Why "whoever owns the capability that creates its data"? | It joins data mesh's domain ownership to *Business architecture for data*'s capabilities: the team whose work creates the data knows what it means. Planning the network creates hosting capacity (Grace); serving customers creates accounts (the head of retail); the shared domain's locations stay Grace's, as the glossary's shared domain has it. | Chapters 3, 4 and 7 |
| 12 | Who owns a measure that crosses domains? | The enterprise domain, which joins the data practice on the map in this film, with Ama as owner of the regulator's measures, such as minutes without power (made from the network's outages and retail's customers). It's a domain like the others, not data governance, and its products are built and run by the data team like every other. | Chapters 3, 4 and 7 |
| 13 | What does the centre keep? | Only what all domains share: the platform, the catalogue and a few rules, as data mesh's self-serve platform and federated governance have it; the data team builds and runs every product, and owns only the platform in the target. | Chapters 3 and 6 |
| 14 | Why those five things before a product is published? | They're how practitioners meet data mesh's product qualities (discoverable, understandable, trustworthy, accessible, secure): an owner, documentation in the business's words, lineage back to the source, a contract with its readers, and an approved way in. Hosting capacity's contract (fresh every Monday; a new method only as a new version, readers told first) is agreed with its readers team to team, as the site's contract rule has it. | Chapter 4 |
| 15 | Are warehouse, lakehouse, mesh and fabric fairly defined? | Each in one line from its source (Inmon and Kimball; the CIDR 2021 lakehouse paper; Dehghani; Forrester and Gartner), without a product name, and said to answer different questions: where data is kept, who owns it, and how it's found. The utility's target, a mesh of owners on its lakehouse with one catalogue, is the film's; one platform or several is left to *Platform and solution architecture*. | Chapter 5 |
| 16 | Baseline, target, gaps and roadmap? | TOGAF's Phase C and E, in plain words: each difference is a gap, with an owner and a quarter, starting where it hurts (hosting capacity, then the most-read products). Three months to claim, then archived with readers told first, then retired: the film's way of retiring unowned data, which practitioners use in many forms. | Chapter 6 |
| 17 | How is a data product drawn on the map? | As ArchiMate's product, in its domain: something the business offers as a whole, which brings together the data it offers (a data object in the lakehouse) and its contract; the catalogue's tables point to their products, so the map and the platform say the same thing. | Chapter 7 |
| 18 | The utility's numbers? | Invented: 412 tables, 300 the data team's; May; four months; ninety families; 3 October; 246 claimed in 31 data products, 54 archived, none asked for back; v1, v2 and v3 of the method; readers told a week ahead. | Chapters 1, 2, 6 and 8 |

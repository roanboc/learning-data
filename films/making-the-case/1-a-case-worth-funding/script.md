## The promise

## The story in one paragraph

New York State, 1816: farms and towns are spreading west around the Great Lakes, with grain to sell on the coast, but the only way east is by wagon, on rough roads. Moving a ton from Buffalo to New York City costs about a hundred dollars and takes weeks, so a family out west can grow plenty and still not sell it at a price that pays. A canal could carry it, but it would run 363 miles through forest and swamp; the federal government won't pay, Jefferson had called the idea little short of madness, and critics call it Clinton's Ditch. DeWitt Clinton and the canal commissioners make the case to New York's legislature: where it would run, what it would cost (about seven million dollars), and how taxes and tolls would repay it. On 15 April 1817 the legislature says yes. Thousands dig it, many just arrived from Ireland and Wales, led by engineers who learn on the job, across the Haudenosaunee nations' homelands, much of them already taken. It opens on 26 October 1825, two years ahead of plan; a ton now costs under nine dollars and takes days; within ten years the tolls have paid back the seven million, and within fifteen New York is the country's busiest port. At the university, in May 2027, the old warehouse has run on the university's own servers since 2012, costs A$310,000 a year, and renews for three years on 31 December unless someone decides. Helena Novak starts from the decision she needs, uses the Five Case Model's five questions as a checklist, and lays out three options: A, renew the licence (A$930,000 over three years); B, move all 38 reports as they are (A$520,000 once); C, move the 21 that about 260 people use, rebuilt from governed products, and retire the 17 nobody has opened in a year, as the warehouse's own logs show (A$380,000 once): she recommends C, and keeps A and B on the page. Running the 21 will cost about A$40,000 a year, from the platform's own cost records, so A$310,000 a year stops and A$40,000 starts: A$270,000 a year saved from January 2028, paid back in about 17 months, A$970,000 over five years, every figure checked by Rohan Desai in Finance. Each benefit has an owner and a date (the saving, the director of digital services; one staff list, Ingrid Holm; an owner for every report, Helena), and the council will see them every quarter. On Tuesday 25 May she has ten minutes and one page that leads with the decision and asks plainly for A$380,000, two of the team for six months and a decision today; Mei asks what waiting a year would cost, and the answer is on the page: option A, A$930,000. The council says yes, that day.

## The reference, and practice

The module's reference is DAMA-DMBOK as the map of the job, enterprise architecture's discover, define and plan as the spine, and lean and agile delivery as the rhythm (the module's *Decided* table records why). For this film, three bodies of knowledge, each named once and none taught:

| Body of knowledge | What it adds | Where the film uses it |
|---|---|---|
| The UK Treasury's Five Case Model (*Guidance on developing business cases*, 2026 edition, with *The Green Book*) | Five questions every case answers: is it needed, is it the best value, can it be bought, can it be afforded, can it be delivered; carrying on as now always on the shortlist, as the benchmark; a short summary that leads with the ask; a benefits register with an owner for each benefit | *The decision first*: "A good case answers five questions; the UK Treasury's Five Case Model names them." *Three options*: "one always carries on as now: doing nothing has a price too." |
| Benefits management, as John Ward and Elizabeth Daniel describe it (*Benefits Management*, 2006) | Each benefit with someone who owns it and a date, and the step most cases skip: tracking the benefits after the decision | *Benefits with owners*: "Benefits management, as John Ward and Elizabeth Daniel describe it, adds one more step: track the benefits after the decision." |
| Payback, in a line | The time until the saving covers the cost | *Once, and every year*: "the case's payback, the time until the saving covers the cost." |
| The author's practice (12 November 2026) | Light methods, because they allow rapid iteration; heavy frameworks only for critical systems, where errors can cost lives, and for what the law requires, where they are added only when required | *The decision first*: "She keeps heavy methods for critical systems, where a mistake can cost lives." "And for what the law requires, as with personal data: it may look like extra work, but it protects everyone." "Even there, she adds a method only when it's required." "Everywhere else, a light one lets her try, learn and change course quickly." |

**Where the film adopts, extends or differs.** It adopts the Five Case Model's questions as the guidance gives them, in plain words, and its rule that carrying on as now goes on every shortlist because it has costs, benefits and risks too. It narrows the guidance's shortlist of about five options to three, as a small case may (the guidance asks to be used in proportion), and its five cases to a checklist, not a form: Helena's page has no section per case. It measures the money by payback and five years' saving, not by net present value, because a university council deciding A$380,000 needs to see when the money comes back, and Finance checks the figures. It adopts benefits management's owner and date for every benefit, and its review after the decision, made concrete: the council sees each benefit against its date every quarter. And it puts the whole case on one page, said in ten minutes, leading with the decision and the ask, where the guidance asks for a short summary in front of a long document. On 12 November 2026 the author confirmed the checklist ("The lightweight for UK is fine. I generally go with lightweight approaches as they enable rapid iteration") and added advice: avoid heavy frameworks for business cases, project management and everywhere else, and leave them for critical systems where errors can cost lives. The film says it as Helena's practice, in two lines after the checklist, so the Treasury isn't made to say it: its own guidance on proportionate business cases asks only that a case be no longer or more detailed than necessary for its scale, cost, complexity and risk (*Rigour*).

**Practice (the module's plan and briefs, 5 November 2026; the author's advice on light methods, 12 November 2026):** keep the method light, so the case can change quickly, and heavy frameworks for critical systems where errors can cost lives and for what the law requires, adding one even there only when it is required; start from the decision you're asking for; always show the option of carrying on as now; count running costs, not only building; give each benefit an owner and a date; ask Finance to check the money; one page, ten minutes; ask for a decision, with a date; say how the benefits will be tracked. A practitioner will recognise the data story: a licence that renews by itself if nobody decides, a migration that would move every report though half are never opened, a running cost forgotten until the first bill, and a council that argues about the numbers until Finance has checked them.

**What the film leaves to others.** The risks of the move, said out loud with owners and early signs, are the next film's, *Risks, said out loud*; who needs to hear the case, and how often, is *Everyone at the table*'s; how to write a page that leads with the decision is the leading data people module's, named; the governance budget line every year is *A capability, not a project*'s, named; how the benefits are measured on one page is *Measures that matter*'s.

- **The Five Case Model.** HM Treasury, *Guidance on developing business cases* (published 16 May 2024, updated 30 June 2026, read in its 2026 edition): "The Five Case Model methodology is the standard framework for developing business cases and is recommended best practice in the UK public sector" (2.1); a case developed in line with the five cases "will demonstrate that the underlying proposal: Provides strategic fit and is supported by a compelling case for change; Provides the best value for money; Involves viable commercial agreements with suppliers; Is affordable and has agreed sources of funding; Is set up for successful delivery" (2.2); the strategic case "sets out why a particular proposal is needed" (2.4); the management case "demonstrates that the preferred option can be delivered successfully" (2.9). The film's five questions are those, in plain words, and its checklist names each case. "A checklist, not a form": the guidance "should be used proportionately" (1.9). *The Green Book* (updated 28 October 2026) is the appraisal guidance it goes with; since 16 May 2024 the business case guidance has had its own page.
- **Light methods, and heavy ones (12 November 2026).** The author's answer in the project chat: "The lightweight for UK is fine. I generally go with lightweight approaches as they enable rapid iteration. Actually as advice avoid heavy frameworks for business cases, project management, and everywhere, leave that only for critical systems where errors can cost lives." The two new lines say it as Helena's practice. What the Treasury itself says is narrower: its page *Developing proportionate business cases* (HM Treasury, published 28 October 2026) says the Green Book and the business case guidance "should be applied proportionately to the scale, cost, complexity and risk of a proposal", and its guide *Proportionality: Guidance for business cases* that "Business cases should be no longer or more detailed than necessary" and that "Small, straightforward proposals may only need a single-stage Business Justification Case (BJC)" (both pages read on 12 November 2026; the 17-page PDF not read). Neither page speaks of risk to life, so "where a mistake can cost lives" is the author's rule of thumb, not the guidance's, and the film doesn't put it in the Treasury's mouth.

- **Carrying on as now.** The same guidance: the existing arrangements, "sometimes known as the business as usual (BAU)", are "the baseline against which practitioners can compare different proposals" (7.11); "Practitioners should not assume that the organisation will do nothing under existing arrangements… The BAU still involves costs, benefits and risks" (7.12); "Practitioners must take forward the BAU to shortlist appraisal, regardless of whether it meets the objectives of the proposal. This is because the BAU acts as a benchmark against which other options are compared" (8.45). The narration's "doing nothing has a price too". The guidance's shortlist has about five options; Helena's has three, as a small case may.

Ten minutes and one page are the module's practice (its briefs).
